## Findings
1. [source] hirsch-smale-devaney-2013, §6.4, p. 125. The displayed chain $\sum_{k\ge0}|a_{ij}(k)|/k! \le \sum_{k\ge0} n^{k-1}a^k/k! \le \sum_k (na)^k/k! \le \exp na$ fails term by term at $k = 0$ with $i = j$ and $n \ge 2$, since $|a_{ii}(0)| = 1 > 1/n$, while the bound $|a_{ij}(k)| \le n^{k-1}a^k$ is only asserted for $k\ge1$. Harmless, and the unit separates the $k=0$ term explicitly and says in step 2 of its proof that the source does not; already logged in `verification/discrepancies.md`. No action in the unit.
2. [source] rhb-2006, §8.16, Eq. (8.104), pp. 287-288. The trace formula $|\exp A| = \exp(\operatorname{Tr}A)$ is stated for a general square matrix but proved "by choosing the similarity transformation so that it diagonalises $A$", which is not available for a defective $A$. The identity is true in general; the unit's property 6 states the general form, proves it through the triangular (Jordan) form, and says in its proof that "the triangular form is what covers defective $A$ as well". Already logged in `verification/discrepancies.md` (two entries). No action in the unit.
3. [minor] method step 1 (line 257): "The same formula computes any $f(A)$ with $f(A) = S\operatorname{diag}(f(\lambda_k))S^{-1}$, provided every $\lambda_k$ lies inside the disc of convergence." Strictly, the statement "Convergence" above also covers the boundary case negatively only through $\rho(A)$, and for a diagonalizable $A$ a $\lambda_k$ *on* the circle of convergence is fine whenever the scalar series converges there — the diagonalizable case is exactly where the Jordan structure drops out. The proviso as written is sufficient, not necessary, which is all the method needs, but it is slightly stronger than the companion `idea` block's analysis would suggest. Fix: "provided $\sum_k c_k\lambda^k$ converges at every eigenvalue, for which $\rho(A) < R$ suffices".
4. [minor] statement "Convergence", part 2 (line 44) and proof step 4: the $A = I$ example settles that $\rho(A) = R$ "is not decided by $\rho(A)$ alone" by varying the coefficients, but the `idea` block (line 50) makes the stronger claim that at $\rho(A) = R$ "the answer depends on the Jordan structure of $A$ and not on the spectrum or the coefficients alone" — a dependence on Jordan structure that is asserted but never exhibited (both halves of the example use $A = I$, which is semisimple). Fix: either soften the `idea` sentence, or add the one-line witness ($c_k = 1/k^2$, $R = 1$, with $A = I$ convergent against $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, where the $(1,2)$ entry is $\sum_k c_k k$ and diverges).

## Checked
- Counterexample search. Tested the statements at their stated edges with numpy/scipy and sympy.
  - Convergence part 1: random $A$ with $\rho(A)$ just below and just above $R$ for $c_k = 1$ ($R=1$), $c_k = 1/k!$ ($R=\infty$) and $c_k = k!$ ($R=0$ — correctly excluded by the hypothesis $R\in(0,\infty]$). The divergence half was probed with a defective $A$ as well as a diagonalizable one: the eigenvector argument of step 3 does not need diagonalizability, and the series diverges as claimed.
  - Convergence part 2: both halves verified at $A = I$ — $\sum_k I$ diverges, $\sum_{k\ge1}I/k^2$ converges to $(\pi^2/6)I$ — and both coefficient sequences have $R = 1$ exactly as the unit computes. The nilpotent-perturbation case is the gap in the `idea` block noted above.
  - Properties: property 2 tested on random non-commuting pairs, where it fails as it must; property 3, 4, 6 on random complex matrices and on defective ones; property 6 also on a $4\times4$ with two $2\times2$ Jordan blocks ($\det\exp A - e^{\operatorname{tr}A} \sim 10^{-14}$).
  - Adjoints statement: tested $\exp(A^\dagger) = (\exp A)^\dagger$ on random complex $A$; unitarity of $\exp A$ for anti-Hermitian $A$ ($\|(\exp A)^\dagger\exp A - I\| \sim 10^{-16}$); positive definiteness for Hermitian $A$ with negative eigenvalues (smallest eigenvalue of $\exp A$ positive, as it must be since $e^\lambda>0$). The splitting $\exp A = B^\dagger B$ with $B = \exp(\tfrac12A)$ is legitimate because $\tfrac12 A$ commutes with itself — the proof says so.
  - Product rule, part 3: the counterexample was rebuilt from scratch, not read off. $\exp\begin{pmatrix}0&\beta\\0&d\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&\beta(e^d-1)/d\\0&e^d\end{pmatrix}$ was confirmed symbolically from $M^k = \begin{pmatrix}0&\beta d^{k-1}\\0&d^k\end{pmatrix}$. With $A = \operatorname{diag}(0,2\pi i)$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&2\pi i\end{pmatrix}$: `expm` gives $\exp A = \exp B = \exp(A+B) = I$ to $10^{-16}$, and $[A,B] = \begin{pmatrix}0&-2\pi i\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ exactly. At $(s,t) = (1,\tfrac12)$: $\exp(A+\tfrac12B) = \begin{pmatrix}1&0.1061033i\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\exp A\exp(\tfrac12 B) = \begin{pmatrix}1&0.3183099i\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$, i.e. $i/(3\pi)$ and $i/\pi$ — both printed values right, and the two sides do differ. Also checked that the identity fails at points arbitrarily near the origin for this pair, as part 2 forces.
  - Part 2's expansion: re-derived both second-order expansions independently; the $st$ coefficients are $\tfrac12(AB+BA)$ and $AB$, whose difference is $-\tfrac12[A,B]$, and $\sigma^3/h^2 = 8|h|\to0$ at $s=t=h$. Correct as printed.
  - Traps: $\exp\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, while the entrywise exponential is $\begin{pmatrix}1&e\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ — both printed correctly. $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ has determinant $-1$ and so is not a real exponential, and the boundedness criterion (every $\operatorname{Re}\lambda\le0$ *and* semisimplicity on the imaginary axis) was tested against $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and against $\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}$.

  - Example, $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&-3&3\end{pmatrix}$.   - The displayed $\exp(tA)$ matrix was checked entry by entry against $e^t(I+tN+\tfrac12t^2N^2)$ and against `expm(t*A)` at $t = 0.3$ and $t = 1.7$ (max entrywise difference $8\times10^{-14}$). The integer determinant is $1\cdot(-14+24)+1\cdot(-8+6) = 8$, so $\det\exp A = (e/2)^3\cdot8 = e^3 = 20.085537$; `det(expm(A))` returns $20.08553692$ and $e^{\operatorname{tr}A} = e^3 = 20.08553692$. The $t=0$ and derivative checks ($I$, and $I+N = A$) both hold.
