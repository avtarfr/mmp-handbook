## Findings
1. [minor] proof of "When the product rule holds", part 3, last sentence: "the coincidence at
   $(s,t) = (1,1)$ is isolated". Locally this is right -- I verified it: for this pair the identity
   reduces to $t(e^{2\pi i(s+t)}-1)/(s+t) = e^{2\pi i t}-1$, and expanding about $(1,1)$ with
   $t = 1+\tau$, $s+t = 2+2\tau+\delta$ forces $\delta = -2\pi i\tau^2$, which is non-real unless
   $\tau = 0$, so $(1,1)$ really is an isolated *real* solution (numerically,
   $(1.001,1)$ and $(1,1.001)$ both fail by $5\times10^{-4}$). But the identity also holds at every
   other integer pair with $s+t\neq0$ -- I checked $(2,1)$, $(1,2)$ and $(2,3)$ numerically, all
   exact -- so a reader may take "isolated" to mean $(1,1)$ is the only coincidence. Fix: say
   "isolated" explicitly in the local sense, e.g. "no pair near $(1,1)$ other than $(1,1)$ itself
   satisfies it (though other integer pairs do)".
2. [minor] statement "Properties of the exponential", closing paragraph: "the binomial theorem for
   $(A+B)^k$, which is valid only when $AB = BA$". The binomial expansion can hold for particular
   non-commuting pairs and for small $k$; what is true is that it is not available *in general*
   without $AB=BA$. Fix: "which is not available in general unless $AB = BA$".
