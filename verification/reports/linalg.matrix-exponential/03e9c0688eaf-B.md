   the single identity $\exp(A + B) = \exp(A)\exp(B)$", and backs it with a counterexample. The unit

## Checked
- Counterexample search (statements as written):
  - Definition "Power series in a matrix": correct, including "convergence means entrywise
    convergence, which is the same as convergence in any norm" (finite dimension, all norms
    equivalent). The Neumann series claims ($\to (I-A)^{-1}$ for $\rho(A)<1$, divergent for
    $\rho(A)>1$) and the definition of $\rho$ are right, and the block correctly defers the conditions
    to the Convergence statement rather than asserting them twice.
  - "Convergence" part 1: both halves are right. For $\rho(A) < R$ absolute entrywise convergence
    follows from the Jordan form (the derivative terms $k^j|c_k|\rho^k$ still converge); for
    $\rho(A) > R$, picking an eigenvector $v$ with $|\lambda| > R$ makes the partial sums act as
    $(\sum_{k\le N}c_k\lambda^k)v$, which cannot converge since $v \ne 0$ and
    $\sum c_k\lambda^k$ diverges. No counterexample.
  - "Convergence" part 2: the inductive bound $|(A^k)_{ij}| \le n^{k-1}a^k$ for $k \ge 1$ is correct
    ($k=1$ gives $a$; the step is $|(A^{k+1})_{ij}| \le n\cdot n^{k-1}a^k\cdot a$). I tested it on 300
    random matrices of sizes 1-5 with entries scaled over several orders of magnitude, for
    $k = 1,\dots,8$: no violation. The summation then telescopes exactly as displayed:
    $\sum_{k\ge0}|(A^k)_{ij}|/k! \le 1 + \frac1n\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(na)^k}{k!} = 1 + \frac{e^{na}-1}{n}$;
    for $n=3$, $a=0.7$ this bound evaluates to $3.3887233042$, finite as claimed. The spin-off test
    "$\sum_kc_kA^k$ converges absolutely whenever $na < R$" is correct, and the unit itself flags it as
    crude. Degenerate case $A = 0$ ($a = 0$) is fine.
  - "Convergence" part 3: correct. $\sum_kz^k$ and $\sum_kz^k/k^2$ both have $R = 1$, and at $A = I$
    (so $\rho(A) = R = 1$) the first diverges and the second converges — so $\rho(A) = R$ really is
    undecided by $\rho$ alone.
  - "Properties of the exponential" 1-6: verified numerically on 500 random complex matrices of sizes
    1-5 with random invertible $T$: $\exp(T^{-1}AT) = T^{-1}(\exp A)T$, $\exp(-A) = (\exp A)^{-1}$,
    $\det(\exp A) = e^{\operatorname{tr}A}$ and the eigenvector property $(\exp A)v = e^\lambda v$ all
    held (tolerances $10^{-7}$ or better). Property 2's hypothesis is stated correctly and the closing
    remark localises it exactly where it is used (the binomial theorem for $(A+B)^k$).
  - "Adjoints, unitarity and positivity": correct. $\exp(A^\dagger) = (\exp A)^\dagger$ held on all 500
    random cases. On 500 further random cases, the anti-Hermitian part always exponentiated to a
    unitary matrix ($\|E^\dagger E - I\| < 10^{-8}$) and the Hermitian part always to a Hermitian
    matrix with strictly positive spectrum. The argument given for positive definiteness
    ($\exp A = (\exp\frac12A)^\dagger(\exp\frac12A)$ with $\exp\frac12A$ invertible) is valid, and the
    block's "Neither cited source states these; they are ours" is accurate — I found neither in
    hirsch nor in rhb.
  - "When the product rule holds" part 2 (the converse): verified symbolically. Expanding both sides to
    second order in generic symbolic $2\times2$ matrices, the coefficient of $st$ is exactly
    $\tfrac12(AB+BA)$ on the left and exactly $AB$ on the right (both residuals simplify to the zero
    matrix), so equating them gives $BA = AB$ as stated. Both sides are entire in $(s,t)$, so equality
    on a neighbourhood does force equality of coefficients — the hypothesis "for all $s,t$ in a
    neighbourhood of $0$" is the right one and is not overstated.
  - "When the product rule holds" part 3: the counterexample is correct in every detail (see
    Calculations), and so is its consistency check against part 2.
  - Part 4 (BCH): correct as stated, including "the discrepancy is of second order".
  - Remarks: $\exp\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and the
    entrywise $\begin{pmatrix}1&e\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ are both as stated. Non-surjectivity over
    $\mathbb{R}$ is right ($\det\exp A = e^{\operatorname{tr}A} > 0$ for real $A$, so
    $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ is not a real exponential), and so is surjectivity over $\mathbb{C}$.
    The boundedness remark is stated as a necessary condition ("only if"), with both clauses — the
    sign condition and the semisimplicity of the imaginary-axis eigenvalues — which is correct;
    $\exp(t\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}) = \begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ confirmed
    symbolically, so $\rho(A)=0$ with unbounded growth really does happen.
  - Summary vs statements: finding 1.
