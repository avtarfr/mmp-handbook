[minor] proof "Transfer function and analyticity in the upper half-plane", Step 1 (line 172): the step is run with the Fourier transform of a *tempered distribution* and the distributional derivative rule, and insists on it ("the rule to use is the distributional one", "No decay hypothesis enters"). No listed prerequisite supplies that: `fourier.fourier-transform` covers the derivative rule for functions, and $\hat\delta = 1$ does follow from the sifting property in `vcalc.dirac-delta-basics`, but the tempered-distribution framework is `fourier.distributions`, which is in the map and is not listed (nor in the transitive closure of what is). There is no mathematical gap — the same conclusion follows inside the listed prerequisites by integrating $\hat G(\omega) = \int_0^\infty G_0(t)e^{i\omega t}dt$ by parts $p$ times, using $G_0^{(j)}(0^+) = 0$ for $j \le p-2$ and $G_0^{(p-1)}(0^+) = 1/a_p$, which produces $P(-i\omega)\hat G = 1$ directly. Fix: add `fourier.distributions` to `prereqs`, or add that one-line classical derivation beside the distributional one.
2. [minor] statement "Transfer function, and what causality does to it", point 2 (line 117): "All the poles of $\hat G$, which are the zeros of $P(-i\omega)$, lie in the closed lower half-plane" holds only under the stability assumption parenthesised in the previous sentence; for the unstable $L = d^2/dt^2 - 1$, $P(-i\omega) = -\omega^2-1$ has the zero $\omega = +i$, in the open *upper* half-plane (and then $G_0(t) = H(t)\sinh t$ is not integrable, so $\hat G$ is not its transform at all). Step 5 of the second proof does carry the hypothesis explicitly. Fix: write "For a stable system, all the poles ...".
3. [minor] proof "The causal Green's function and the solution formula", Step 5 (line 146): "the integrand and its $t$-derivatives are continuous in $(t,\tau)$" is false at a jump of $f$, while statement (ii) assumes only that $f$ is piecewise continuous; the boundary term $u_t(t)f(t)$ in Steps 5-6 needs $f$ continuous at $t$. The conclusion $Lu = f$ then holds at every point of continuity of $f$ (and $u$ is $C^{p-1}$ with $u^{(p)}$ jumping where $f$ does). Stakgold and Holst's Theorem 3.1.4 is stated with the same looseness, so this is not infidelity, only a mismatch between our statement and our proof. Fix: say "at every point of continuity of $f$" in (ii), or assume $f$ continuous in the proof and note the piecewise case.
4. [minor] statement (iv) (line 90) and method, step 5 (line 217): the step-response form $u = F(t,a)f(a) + \int_a^t F f'\,d\tau$ needs $f$ to be (piecewise) $C^1$; when it is applied to "a staircase forcing", $f'$ is a sum of deltas and the identity is being read distributionally. The reading is correct — $\int F f' = \sum_k \Delta_k F(t,\tau_k)$ — but it is not the same sense as the displayed formula. Fix: one clause saying the staircase case is the distributional reading of the same formula.
5. [minor] remark "Sign conventions in the transform" (line 205): Arfken's §12.8 fixes no transform convention at all — it simply hypothesises a function analytic in the upper half-plane (p. 591) — so calling our sign "the convention ... of Arfken's §12.8" attributes to him something he does not state. The substance is right: it is the half-plane his §12.8 works in, and his §20.5 does use the opposite sign in Eq. (20.110). Fix: "...and it is the half-plane in which Arfken's §12.8 works".
