## Findings

1. [blocking] definition (line 44): the key justification of the keyhole limit rests on a false claim. The text asserts that "the function $(r,\alpha)\mapsto f(re^{i\alpha})$ is continuous on the compact set $[\rho,R]\times[0,2\pi]$ once the two edge values of the jump statement below are assigned at $\alpha = 0$ and $\alpha = 2\pi$, hence uniformly continuous there." That is false whenever the integrand has a pole in the annulus $\rho < \lvert z\rvert < R$ -- which is every case the method is used for, and both keyhole examples in this very unit: in the first, $f(z) = z^{-1/3}/(z+2)$ blows up at $(r,\alpha) = (2,\pi)$; in the second, $f(z) = z^{1/2}/(z^2+9)$ blows up at $(3,\pi/2)$ and $(3,3\pi/2)$. The sentence immediately before even says the integrand is "analytic apart from its poles", so the block contradicts itself one line later. Since the uniform continuity is exactly what is used to pass $\delta \to 0$ in the two ray integrals, the limit argument -- the one piece of mathematics the definition block is there to supply, and the one Ahlfors (p. 159) flags as needing justification -- is unjustified as written.

   Fix: restrict the compact set to a neighbourhood of the two edges. There are finitely many poles and none on the cut, so choose $\delta_0 > 0$ smaller than the argument of every pole and smaller than $2\pi$ minus it; then $(r,\alpha)\mapsto f(re^{i\alpha})$ *is* continuous on $[\rho,R]\times\bigl([0,\delta_0]\cup[2\pi-\delta_0,2\pi]\bigr)$, hence uniformly continuous there, which is all the ray integrals need. Add that $\rho$ and $R$ are chosen so that no pole lies on either circle, which is what makes the two arc integrands continuous and the arc limits valid.

2. [blocking] example 3, step 1, the Morera paragraph (line 207): the approximation step cannot be carried out as described. The text says: "Approximate it by closed contours in $U$ that avoid the diameter -- push each crossing of the real axis up or down by $\eta$ -- and let $\eta\to0$; the integrals converge to $\oint_\gamma w\,dz$ because $w$ is uniformly continuous on the compact $\overline U$, while each approximating integral is $0$ by Cauchy's theorem ... on the upper or the lower half-disc." But $U$ minus its real diameter has exactly two connected components, the open upper and lower half-discs, so a *closed* contour in $U$ avoiding the diameter lies wholly in one of them. If $\gamma$ has points strictly above and strictly below the axis -- the only case where there is anything to prove -- no such contour is uniformly close to $\gamma$, and the appeal to uniform continuity is empty. The conclusion ($w$ analytic across $(1,\infty)$) is true and standard, but this route to it does not work, and steps 3-4 depend on it.

   Fix: split rather than perturb. Cut $\gamma$ at its crossings of the diameter; group the resulting arcs into those lying in the closed upper half-disc and those in the closed lower half-disc, and close each group along the diameter. The added diameter pieces are traversed once in each direction and cancel, so $\oint_\gamma w = \oint_{\gamma_+} w + \oint_{\gamma_-} w$ with $\gamma_\pm$ closed contours in the closed upper/lower half-disc. Each of those vanishes: translate $\gamma_+$ upward by $\eta$ (a translate *is* uniformly close to $\gamma_+$, which is what the earlier argument wanted), where $w$ is analytic, so the translated integral is $0$ by Cauchy's theorem, and let $\eta\to0$ using continuity of $w$ up to the diameter.
