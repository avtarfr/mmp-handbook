## Findings

1. [blocking] application "Fourier solution of the heat equation", paragraph "Why the set must be named" (line 220): "By the continuity theorem the convergence then cannot be uniform on any set whose closure meets $t = 0$" is false as written, and the justification offered does not reach the claim even where the claim is true.

   *Counterexample.* Take $E_0=\{0\}\times[0,\infty)\subset[0,\pi]\times[0,\infty)$, the end of the rod. Its closure is itself and meets $t=0$. Every term of the series vanishes identically on $E_0$, because $\sin(n\cdot0)=0$, so $s_N\equiv0\equiv u$ there and $\sup_{E_0}|u-s_N|=0$ for every $N$: the convergence is uniform on $E_0$. The same happens on $\{\pi\}\times[0,\infty)$.

   The failure is not confined to the two boundary lines. Take $f=1$ on $(0,\pi/2)$, $0$ on $(\pi/2,\pi)$ — the jump at $\pi/2$ that the sentence is about — and $E_1=\{\pi/4\}\times[0,\infty)$, whose closure also meets $t=0$. With $b_n=(2/\pi)(1-\cos(n\pi/2))/n$, $k=1$ and the supremum taken over $t\in\{0\}\cup[10^{-8},1]$, I computed $\sup_t\bigl|\sum_{n>N}b_ne^{-n^2t}\sin(n\pi/4)\bigr|$ = $4.77\times10^{-2}$ at $N=10$, $1.21\times10^{-2}$ at $N=50$, $5.43\times10^{-3}$ at $N=200$, $1.07\times10^{-3}$ at $N=1000$ — tending to $0$, so the convergence is uniform on $E_1$ too. (This is Abel's test, the very test the unit's own remark on line 139 describes: $e^{-n^2kt}$ is non-increasing in $n$ and lies in $(0,1]$ for all $t\ge0$, and $\sum b_n\sin(n\pi/4)$ converges, so the tails are uniformly small in $t$. Numerically the series at $t=0$, $x=\pi/4$ sums to $0.99995$, i.e. $f(\pi/4)=1$, as expected at a point of continuity.)

   *And the reasoning is a non sequitur for the sets where the claim does hold.* For $E=[0,\pi]\times(0,T]$ — closure meets $t=0$, no point of $E$ has $t=0$ — the sum $u$ **is** continuous on $E$, so the continuity theorem is satisfied and yields no contradiction. Non-uniformity there needs the extra step that uniform Cauchy-ness on $E$ of the continuous partial sums transfers to $\overline{E}$, and only then does the discontinuity at $t=0$ bite.

   Fix: restrict the claim to sets that contain a neighbourhood of the jump point inside $t=0$ — e.g. "cannot be uniform on $[0,\pi]\times[0,\infty)$, nor on any set containing a segment of $t=0$ around the jump" — or keep the general form and supply the density step ("the partial sums are continuous, so a uniform Cauchy estimate on $E$ holds on $\overline{E}$ as well, and the limit would then be continuous at $t=0$"), which also makes clear why the one-point-in-$x$ sets are not counterexamples to the intended claim.

2. [minor] application, same paragraph (line 220): "if $f$ jumps at an interior point then the series at $t = 0$ is the sine series of $f$, whose sum takes the mean of the two one-sided values at that point". That is Dirichlet's theorem and needs more of $f$ than the paragraph above it assumes — line 212 asks only that $f$ be integrable ("however rough"), and for a merely integrable $f$ the sine series need not converge anywhere. Fix: say "if $f$ is piecewise smooth and jumps at an interior point".
