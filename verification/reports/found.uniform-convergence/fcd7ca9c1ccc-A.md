## Findings
1. [blocking] application "Fourier solution of the heat equation", paragraph "Why the set must be named" (line 220): "By the continuity theorem the convergence then cannot be uniform on any set whose closure meets $t = 0$" is false as written, and the justification offered does not reach the intended case either. Counterexample to the claim: take $E = \{0\}\times(0,\infty)$. Every term $b_n e^{-n^2kt}\sin(nx)$ vanishes identically on $E$ because $\sin 0 = 0$, so the series converges uniformly on $E$ (to $0$, with zero error at every $N$), yet $\overline{E} = \{0\}\times[0,\infty)$ meets $t = 0$. Separately, the continuity theorem proved in this unit concludes at points *of* $E$, so for a set that merely has the jump point in its closure it yields nothing directly: one needs the extra step that a uniformly Cauchy sequence of partial sums, each continuous on $\overline{E}$, is uniformly Cauchy on $\overline{E}$, so the sum would extend continuously there. Fix: state the claim for sets whose closure contains the jump point $(x_j, 0)$ — for instance $[0,\pi]\times(0,\infty)$ — and supply the closure step, or argue directly that $\sup_{x}\lvert u(x,t)-u_N(x,t)\rvert$ does not tend to $0$ uniformly in $t$ as $t \downarrow 0$.
2. [blocking] application, same paragraph (line 220): "if $f$ jumps at an interior point then the series at $t = 0$ is the sine series of $f$, whose sum takes the mean of the two one-sided values at that point and is therefore discontinuous there" does not follow from the hypothesis actually in force. Two paragraphs earlier (line 212) the only assumption on $f$ is integrability — "finite for any integrable initial profile, however rough" — and for a merely integrable $f$ the sine series need not converge at the jump at all, so neither the convergence nor the mean of the one-sided limits is available. The jump-mean value requires a Dirichlet- or Jordan-type hypothesis. Fix: assume $f$ piecewise smooth, or of bounded variation near the jump, and cite that convergence theorem; or replace the step by one that needs no pointwise statement at $t = 0$ (for example, exhibit a specific $f$, such as the step function on $[0,\pi]$, for which the Gibbs overshoot gives $\sup_x\lvert u(x,t_N) - u_N(x,t_N)\rvert \not\to 0$ along some $t_N \downarrow 0$).

## Checked
Re-derived all three proofs. Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence: necessity by the triangle inequality with $\varepsilon/2$; sufficiency builds $f$ pointwise from the completeness of $\mathbb C$ (the listed found.limits-of-sequences result), then recovers uniformity by letting $m \to \infty$ at fixed $n$ and $x$ inside a non-strict inequality, with the index produced from $\varepsilon$ alone — the route Ahlfors takes on p. 36. Continuity of a uniform limit: the three $\varepsilon/3$ pieces, the order of quantifiers (the index $n$ chosen before $\delta$, which is where uniformity enters), and the series form via continuity of the finite partial sums. M-test: the block estimate $\lvert\sum_{m+1}^{m+p}u_n(x)\rvert \le \sum M_n < \varepsilon$ with $N$ independent of $x$, and the pointwise absolute convergence by comparison. Prerequisites are now complete and all three are used: found.limits-of-sequences (completeness of $\mathbb C$), found.series-convergence-tests (the Cauchy criterion for a series, the comparison test, the $p$-series and the ratio test in the application), found.absolute-convergence (the notion invoked in the M-test and the remark); found.termwise-operations is a forward pointer only and correctly not listed.
