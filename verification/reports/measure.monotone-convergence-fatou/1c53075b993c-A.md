## Findings
1. [blocking] statement "Monotone convergence theorem" (line 53) and statement "Fatou's lemma" (line 75): both almost-everywhere versions omit the hypothesis that the limit function be measurable, which the source carries and which the conclusion needs. Line 53 reads "if the $f_n$ are in $\mathcal{L}^{+}$ and $f_n(x) \uparrow f(x)$ for a.e. $x$, then still $\int f = \lim_n \int f_n$"; line 75 reads "if $f_n \in \mathcal{L}^{+}$ and $f_n \to f$ a.e., then $\int f \, d\mu \le \liminf_n \int f_n \, d\mu$". Folland's Cor. 2.17 and Cor. 2.19 (p. 51 and p. 52) both state "$\{f_n\} \subseteq L^+$, **$f \in L^+$**, and ...". The hypothesis is not redundant here. The unit's own definition block (line 35) derives measurability of the limit from $f = \sup_n f_n$, which is available only when $f_n \uparrow f$ *everywhere*; in the a.e. versions $f$ is unconstrained on the exceptional set $Z$, and the unit's own definition of "almost everywhere" (line 37) allows the failure set to be any subset of a measurable null set, so on a measure space that is not complete $f$ need not be measurable and $\int f$ need not be defined. The proofs inherit the gap: Step 6 (line 105) writes $\int f$ and concludes "$\int f = \int f\mathbf{1}_E$" from "$f - f\mathbf{1}_E$ ... are $0$ a.e., hence have zero integral", and Step 4 of the Fatou proof (line 143) says "Altering $f$ and the $f_n$ on the exceptional null set changes no integral" — both presuppose exactly what is missing. Note that `measure.lebesgue-integral` explicitly treats completeness as a property of Lebesgue measure that has to be invoked, not as a standing assumption, so it cannot be read in here. Fix: add "$f \in \mathcal{L}^{+}$" to both statements (and to Step 6 and Step 4), or state the corollaries for the completion of $\mu$ and say so.
2. [minor] remark, first bullet (line 147): "Monotonicity is used in step 3 of the first proof, to make the sets $A_n$ increase, and nowhere else." It is also used in Step 1 (line 85), twice: $f_n \le f_{n+1}$ is what makes the numbers $\int f_n$ increase so that $L = \lim_n \int f_n$ exists, and $f_n \le f$ (which is $f = \sup_m f_m$, again monotonicity) is what gives $L \le \int f$. Without monotonicity neither holds, and the easy half of the theorem is already lost. Fix: "Monotonicity is used twice in step 1, to make $\lim_n\int f_n$ exist and to give the easy inequality, and essentially in step 3, to make the sets $A_n$ increase."
3. [minor] application, second paragraph (line 222): "For non-negative $Z_1, Z_2, \dots$ the identity $\mathbb{E}(\sum_i Z_i) = \sum_i \mathbb{E}(Z_i)$ holds with no integrability hypothesis at all, as :cite[Grimmett and Stirzaker] record." Their item (13) (p. 201) in fact reads "Let $Z_1, Z_2, \dots$ be non-negative random variables **with finite expectations**", and concludes "whether or not the summation is finite" — i.e. they allow the *sum* to be infinite but assume each term integrable. The stronger claim is true and does follow from this unit's own termwise theorem, but it is not what the cited source records. Fix: attribute only the no-condition-on-the-sum part to Grimmett and Stirzaker, and attribute the removal of the per-term hypothesis to the theorem proved above.
4. [minor] statement "Termwise integration of a non-negative series" (line 65): "In particular a non-negative double series may be summed in either order" is a corollary only after identifying the integral against counting measure on $\mathbb{N}$ with the sum of a series, which is nowhere established in this unit or in its prerequisite (Folland leaves it as Exercise 22, p. 52). Fix: either add the half-sentence "apply the theorem on $\mathbb{N}$ with counting measure, for which $\int f\,d\mu = \sum_m f(m)$", or drop the sentence.
5. [source] remark, third bullet (line 149): the unit reports that Stakgold and Holst state Fatou's lemma "for a sequence of measurable functions on a measurable $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n}$", which is a faithful report of their Thm 0.7.3 (p. 47). As printed that statement carries no lower bound at all and is false ($f_j = -j\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/j)}$ on $(0,1)$ gives $0 \le -1$). The unit correctly follows Folland and Evans and only notes the difference, so this does not affect the verdict; recorded with `tools/discrepancy.mjs` against stakgold-holst-2011. Optional improvement: say that their hypotheses are *weaker than any version that is true*, rather than only that they "differ from ours".
6. [minor] idea block (line 79): "In all three results the inequality $\int f \ge \lim \int f_n$, or its analogue, is free: the limit function dominates each term, and the integral is monotone." That is right for monotone convergence and for the series, but for Fatou's lemma there is no free inequality of this shape — the limit function does not dominate the terms, and the whole content of the lemma is the single inequality it states. Fix: restrict the sentence to the first two results and say that Fatou's lemma has no easy half.

Monotone convergence: checked that $A_n = \{f_n \ge a\varphi\}$ with the *non-strict* inequality really does exhaust $X$ (at a point with $\varphi(x)=0$ one needs $f_1(x) \ge 0$, which Folland's strict $E_n = \{f_n > a\varphi\}$ on p. 50 does not give — our version is the correct one), that $A_n$ increases, that $\int_{A_n}\varphi = \sum_j a_j\mu(E_j \cap A_n)$ is the stage-1 integral restricted to $A_n$, that $E_j \cap A_n \uparrow E_j$ so continuity from below applies termwise over the finitely many $j$, and that $a \uparrow 1$ and the supremum over $\varphi$ close the argument; also checked that the $\mathcal{L}^+$ setting is what makes every quantity defined with no finiteness assumption and that both sides may be $+\infty$. Fatou: verified $g_k = \inf_{n\ge k}f_n$ increases to $\liminf f_n$, the step $\int g_k \le \inf_{j\ge k}\int f_j$, and that $\lim_k \inf_{j\ge k}\int f_j$ is by definition $\liminf_n \int f_n$. Checked both counterexamples in the remark: $\mathbf{1}_{(n,n+1)}$ and $n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ have every integral $1$ with pointwise limit $0$, so Fatou is strict ($0<1$) and monotone convergence would be false; the single-point example $X=\{p\}$, $\mu(\{p\})=1$, $f_n \equiv 1-1/n$, $\varphi = \mathbf{1}_X$ really does make $\{f_n \ge \varphi\}$ empty for every $n$ while $\{f_n \ge a\varphi\}$ is eventually $X$ for each $a<1$; and $-n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ has $\liminf = 0$ pointwise with every integral $-1$. Verified the Reed–Simon reconciliation: under $\int f_n \le C$ their conclusion does follow from ours, with the subtraction $\int|f-f_n| = \int f - \int f_n$ legitimate because $\int f \le C < \infty$.
