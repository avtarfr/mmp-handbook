## Findings
1. [minor] example (line 202): "The generalised inverse inf{x : F(x) >= 0.9} returns 3, which is the right answer — nine runs in ten do finish by the time the counter stops, because in fact more than nine in ten do." The intended reading is P(X <= 3) = 1 >= 0.9, which is correct, but "finish" invites the reading "the decay occurs within the window", whose probability is 1 - e^{-3/2} = 0.7769 — the number printed in the same sentence. As written the sentence can be read as contradicting its own neighbouring figure. Fix: "every run records a value of at most 3 hours, so the 0.9-quantile is 3 even though only 77.7% of states have actually decayed by then."
2. [minor] note "A catalogue of the standard distributions" (lines 144-172): the block is `adapted` with src "G&S §3.5, Examples (1)-(6), pp. 64-65, and §4.4, Examples (1)-(8), pp. 106-108", but those pages give means and variances only for Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, geometric, negative binomial (implicitly) and the exponential mean. Every value is correct (checked below). Fix: say in the src, or in the block's lead sentence, that the continuous means and variances are computed by us; the block already points forward to prob.expectation-variance, which is most of the way there.

- Counterexample search: tried to break the three characterising properties and the converse. Property 3: the proof's intersection argument (a real that is <= x + 1/n for all n is <= x) is the only place the choice of <= matters, and the idea block's claim that F(x) = P(X < x) would come out left-continuous is right (the decreasing family {X < x+1/n} has intersection {X <= x}, so right-continuity fails and the increasing family {X < x-1/n} gives left-continuity). Step 5's generalised inverse: checked X(w) <= x iff w <= F(x) against F with a flat stretch (uniform on [0,1] ∪ [2,3]) and against F with a jump (the unit's own clipped variable), and against an F that is constant 1/2 on an interval; P(X <= x) = leb((0, F(x)]) = F(x) in each case, and property 2 is what keeps X finite. The claim "at most countably many atoms because the jumps sum to at most 1" holds. The tails T1(x) = 1 - F(x), T2(x) = F(-x) agree with the source's definition. No counterexample found.
