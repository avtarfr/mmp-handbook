## Findings
1. [source] hassani-2013, §10.6.1, Thm 10.6.2, p. 320. Hassani's theorem reads "The power series $S(z) = \sum a_n(z-z_0)^n$ is uniformly convergent for all points within its circle of convergence and represents a function that is analytic there" — i.e. uniform convergence on the whole open disc, which the geometric series contradicts. The unit reports this correctly, follows Ahlfors's $\rho$-form, and says so in its second remark bullet; already logged in `verification/discrepancies.md` (two entries). No action in the unit.
2. [source] hassani-2013, §10.6.1, Thm 10.6.3, p. 321. Termwise integration is asserted "for any path $\gamma$ lying in the circle of convergence of the power series", with no rectifiability condition, although the identity needs a finite length. The unit states Hassani's unqualified form and supplies piecewise smoothness in the deferral note, naming the difference; already logged in `verification/discrepancies.md`. No action in the unit.
3. [minor] statement "Convergence of a power series (Cauchy-Hadamard)", part 1 (line 51): "for each $\rho$ with $0 \le \rho < R$ it converges uniformly on the closed disc $\lvert z-z_0\rvert \le \rho$" is vacuous at $R = 0$, which the proof then has to dispose of in its first sentence ("If $R = 0$ there is no $\rho$ ... and nothing to prove"). Harmless but it makes part 1 read as if it said something at $R=0$. Fix: open the statement with "If $R > 0$ then ...", or keep as is and note the convention in the definition.

## Checked
- Counterexample search. Attacked each of the four parts and the two statements at their edges.
  - $R = 0$ ($a_n = n!$) and $R = \infty$ ($a_n = 1/n!$): Hadamard's formula under the stated conventions gives both, and parts 1-2 degenerate correctly.
  - Part 1 cannot be strengthened to the open disc: for $\sum z^n$ the tail at $z = \rho$ is $\rho^N/(1-\rho)$, unbounded as $\rho\to1^-$ — the unit's own second remark bullet, and the reason its part 1 carries a $\rho$.
  - Part 3: the three series $\sum z^n$, $\sum z^n/n$, $\sum z^n/n^2$ were checked at $z=1$, $z=-1$ and random $e^{i\theta}$: divergence everywhere, convergence everywhere but $z=1$, absolute convergence everywhere. So no boundary assertion is possible, as claimed.
  - Hadamard versus the ratio form: $a_n = 1$ (even), $2$ (odd) gives $|a_n|^{1/n}\to1$, $R = 1$, while $|a_{n+1}/a_n|$ alternates $2, 1/2$ — verified numerically; the ratio form decides nothing, Hadamard's does.
  - Abel's theorem: tested the necessity of the Stolz restriction and the absence of a converse. $\sum(-1)^nx^n = 1/(1+x) \to 1/2$ as $x\to1^-$ while $\sum(-1)^n$ diverges — so no converse, as the fifth remark bullet says. Also checked that the theorem's conclusion is not claimed for tangential approach.
  - End-point behaviour under termwise operations: $\sum x^n$ diverges at $x=-1$ while $\sum x^{n+1}/(n+1)$ converges there to $-\ln2 = -0.693147$ (confirmed numerically) — the fourth remark bullet.
  - The two descriptions of the Stolz approach: re-derived both implications in the proof's last paragraph independently. The identity $1-|z|^2 = 2\operatorname{Re}(1-z)-|1-z|^2$ holds; $1-|z| \ge \tfrac12(1-|z|^2)$ for $|z|\le1$; hence $1-|z| \ge |1-z|(\cos\theta - \tfrac12|1-z|)$ and the quotient is $\le 2/\cos\theta$ once $|1-z|\le\cos\theta$. Converse: $\operatorname{Re}(1-z) \ge \tfrac12(1-|z|) \ge |1-z|/(2K)$, so $\cos\arg(1-z) \ge 1/(2K)$. Both as printed.
- Calculations (`.venv/bin/python`, mpmath at 30 dps).
  - Example 1. $a_n = 2^{-n}$ (even), $3^{-n}$ (odd). $|a_n|^{1/n}$ is exactly $1/2$ and $1/3$ on the two subsequences, so $R = 2$. The split closed form $4/(4-z^2) + 3z/(9-z^2)$ reproduces the series (checked against partial sums at several complex $z$ with $|z|<2$), and $f(1) = 4/3+3/8 = 41/24 = 1.7083333\overline{3}$ — the unit's value. On $|z| = 2$ the even terms have modulus $1$, so the terms do not tend to $0$: divergence at every point of the circle, as stated. The integral: $\int_0^1 4/(4-x^2)dx = \ln3$ and $\int_0^1 3x\,dx/(9-x^2) = \tfrac32\ln\tfrac98$, total $\ln3 + \tfrac32\ln\tfrac98 = 1.27528684215268487320$, and $\ln(81\sqrt2/32) = 1.27528684215268487320$ — the unit's $1.2752868421526849$ is right, and the two closed forms agree because $3(9/8)^{3/2} = 81/(16\sqrt2) = 81\sqrt2/32$. The printed partial sums of $\sum a_n/(n+1)$ are exactly reproduced: $1$, $1.16666667$, $1.25$, $1.25925926$, $1.27175926$, $1.27244513$, $1.27467727$, $1.27473443$; the cumulative sum through $n = 11$ is $1.27526279$, which is the unit's "by $n=11$ ... $1.275263$".
  - Example 2. $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^n/(3n+1) = 0.83564884826472105$ by acceleration, and $\ln2/3 + \pi/(3\sqrt3) = 0.23104906018665 + 0.60459978807807 = 0.83564884826472105$ — the unit's $0.2310490602$, $0.6045997881$ and $0.8356488483$ are all right. The printed partial sums match to the last digit: $1$, $0.75$, $0.892857$, $0.792857$, $0.869780$, $0.807280$, $0.859912$, $0.814457$. The partial fractions were verified by clearing denominators: $(t^2-t+1) + (2-t)(1+t) = 3$, so $1/(1+t^3) = 1/(3(1+t)) + (2-t)/(3(t^2-t+1))$; the split $2-t = -\tfrac12(2t-1)+\tfrac32$ is correct; $\int_0^1(2t-1)dt/(t^2-t+1) = \ln1-\ln1 = 0$; $\tfrac12\int_0^1 dt/((t-\tfrac12)^2+\tfrac34) = \tfrac1{\sqrt3}\cdot\tfrac\pi3 = \pi/(3\sqrt3)$. $\int_0^1 dt/(1+t^3)$ by quadrature agrees with the closed form to 25 digits.
  - Proof arithmetic: $\delta_n^2 \le (n-1)/\binom n2 = 2/n$ (step 1) checked symbolically; $(n+1)^{1/n} \to 1$ via $n^{1/n}\le(n+1)^{1/n}\le 2^{1/n}n^{1/n}$ checked; the step-3 bound $\bigl|f(w)-f(w_0)-(w-w_0)g(w_0)\bigr| \le \tfrac12K|w-w_0|^2$ re-derived from $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}j = \tfrac12 n(n-1)$, and tested numerically on $\sum z^n$ and on Example 1's coefficients at $\rho = 1.5$ — the inequality holds with the printed constant.
  - Application: for Airy's equation $y''=xy$ the recursion is indeed $(n+2)(n+3)a_{n+3} = a_n$ (matching powers of $x^n$), giving $\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}=0$ and $R=\infty$; for Legendre's equation $R = 1$. Both as stated.
