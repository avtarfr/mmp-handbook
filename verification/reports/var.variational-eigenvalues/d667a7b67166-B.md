## Findings
1. [minor] idea block (line 54), `src` and content. The unit's sign convention for $q$ is Strauss's
   and Gelfand and Fomin's — $-(pu')' + qu = \lambda w u$ and $D[u]=\int(pu'^2+qu^2)$ — but the same
   block also cites Riley, Hobson and Bence, whose (22.24) on p. 791 is
   $\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(p\frac{dy}{dx}\bigr)+qy+\lambda\rho y=0$, i.e. $-(py')'-qy=\lambda\rho y$, with
   $I=\int(py'^2-qy^2)$. Their $q$ is minus ours. Nothing the unit says is wrong — everything it
   draws from them (the quotient at an eigenfunction equals the eigenvalue; the eigenfunction
   expansion making the quotient a weighted average) is independent of that sign — but a reader
   opening pp. 791–793 sees an equation that looks different, and `docs/STYLE_GUIDE.md` asks for the
   convention to be stated along with which books use which. Fix: one clause in the idea block or a
   short conventions note, e.g. "(Riley, Hobson and Bence write $+q$ on the other side, so their $q$
   is minus ours.)"
2. [minor] statement 3 (line 51), the "Equivalently" clause. This is Strauss's maximin principle,
   §11.6 Theorem 2, pp. 324–325 — which the frontmatter lists but the statement block's `src=` does
   not (it names only §11.1 Thms 1–2 and §11.2 Thm 1). Since the clause is explicitly flagged as
   "stated here without proof", it is the one place in the unit that most needs its own locator.
   Also, Strauss takes the maximum over $n-1$ *piecewise continuous* functions, whereas the unit says
   *continuous*; both give $\lambda_n$ (the upper bound holds for any class and the maximum is
   attained at the eigenfunctions, which are continuous), so the unit's version is true, but it is
   narrower than the source it paraphrases. Fix: add
   `strauss-2008:§11.6 Thm 2, pp. 324-325` to the block's `src`, and either say "piecewise
   continuous" or note that the class is immaterial.

## Checked
- Counterexample search: attacked each part where it could fail.
  (i) The boundary condition in part 1. The unit's own counterexample is right: for $-u''=\lambda u$
  on $[0,\pi]$ with Dirichlet data $\lambda_1=1$, while $u\equiv1$ gives $\mathcal{R}=0$ — so the
  requirement $u(a)=u(b)=0$ on trial functions cannot be dropped, and step 1's end terms are exactly
  where it enters.
  (ii) Part 2 without the orthogonality. This is the sharpest trap in the unit, and the unit walks
  into it deliberately in the example: $\mathcal{R}[\sin 2\pi x]=26.319 < \lambda_2=26.465$. I
  verified both numbers (below), so the "warning about higher eigenvalues" is a genuine and correct
  counterexample to the careless version of part 2, and the explanation (not $w$-orthogonal to
  $\varphi_1$) is the right one.
  (iii) The comparison claims. I checked each: increasing $p$ or $q$ raises $D[u]$ pointwise, hence
  $\mathcal{R}$, hence each min-max; shrinking the interval works because a trial function on a
  subinterval extended by zero is still continuous and piecewise $C^1$ with the right end values —
  which is precisely why the unit's trial class is piecewise $C^1$ rather than Strauss's $C^2$, so
  the argument is sound as the unit sets things up. Decreasing $w$ needs $D[u]>0$, and the unit's
  counterexample for $q<0$ checks out exactly: $-u''-100u=\lambda wu$ on $[0,\pi]$ with Dirichlet
  data has $u=\sin nx$ and $\lambda=(n^2-100)/w$ for constant $w$, so $\lambda_1=-99$ at $w=1$ and
  $\lambda_1=-198$ at $w=\tfrac12$ — halving $w$ does lower $\lambda_1$, as claimed.
  (iv) Equality cases: step 5 needs $\lambda_{n+1}>\lambda_n$, and the remark says so. For a
  degenerate eigenvalue the equality claims in parts 1–2 would fail (any element of the eigenspace
  attains the bound), and the definition block's "real and simple" rules that out.
  No counterexample found to any statement as written.
- Calculations (sympy, numpy/scipy shooting, `.venv/bin/python`):
  - Example, two-sided bounds. $1 \le 1+x \le 2$ on $[0,1]$ gives
    $\int u^2 \le \int(1+x)u^2 \le 2\int u^2$, hence
    $\tfrac12\mathcal{R}_1 \le \mathcal{R} \le \mathcal{R}_1$ — the direction of each inequality is
    right (the weight is in the denominator). $\tfrac12\pi^2 = 4.93480$ and $\pi^2=9.86960$, so the
    printed $4.935 \le \lambda_1 \le 9.870$ is correct.
  - Example, one-trial-function bound. sympy: $\int_0^1(\pi\cos\pi x)^2dx=\pi^2/2$,
    $\int_0^1 x\sin^2\pi x\,dx = 1/4$ exactly, so $\int_0^1(1+x)\sin^2\pi x\,dx = 3/4$ — all three
    printed values confirmed — and $\mathcal{R}[\sin\pi x] = (\pi^2/2)/(3/4) = 2\pi^2/3
    = 6.5797363$, printed as $6.5797$.
  - Exact eigenvalues, independently. I reproduced the unit's shooting computation: integrate
    $u''=-\lambda(1+x)u$ from $x=0$ with $u(0)=0$, $u'(0)=1$ (rtol $10^{-12}$) and bracket the zeros
    of $u(1)$ in $\lambda$. This gives $\lambda_1 = 6.548395$, $\lambda_2 = 26.464937$ (and
    $\lambda_3 = 59.674174$). The printed $\lambda_1 \approx 6.5484$ and $\lambda_2 \approx 26.465$
    are both right, the min-max bracket $4.935 \le 6.5484 \le 9.870$ holds, and the stated error of
    the trial bound is $(6.5797363-6.548395)/6.548395 = 0.479\%$ — "0.5% high" is fair.
  - Example, the warning. $\int_0^1(2\pi\cos2\pi x)^2dx = 2\pi^2$ and
    $\int_0^1(1+x)\sin^2 2\pi x\,dx = 3/4$, so $\mathcal{R}[\sin2\pi x] = 8\pi^2/3 = 26.318945$,
    printed as $26.319$ — and it really is below $\lambda_2 = 26.464937$ while above
    $\lambda_1$, so the unit's reading of the situation is exactly right. For reference the min-max
    bracket for $n=2$ is $\tfrac12\cdot4\pi^2 = 19.74 \le 26.465 \le 4\pi^2 = 39.48$, consistent.
  - Proof, by hand, step by step. Step 1: the piecewise integration by parts is right, and the
    cancellation of interior terms needs $u$ and $p\varphi_n'$ continuous, which is what the unit
    says. Step 2: $\kappa$ exists because $w$ has a positive minimum, and
    $D_\kappa[u,\varphi_n] = (\lambda_n+\kappa)(u,\varphi_n)_w$, so step 1 does carry over. Step 3:
    expanding $D_\kappa[u-u_N]$ gives $D_\kappa[u] - 2\sum(\lambda_n+\kappa)c_n^2 +
    \sum(\lambda_n+\kappa)c_n^2$, which is the printed identity. Step 4: with $c_k=0$ for $k<n$ and
    $\lambda_k \ge \lambda_n$ for $k\ge n$ the inequality $D_\kappa[u] \ge (\lambda_n+\kappa)
    \sum_{k\le N}c_k^2$ follows for either sign of $\lambda_n+\kappa$, and Parseval closes it;
    note $\lambda_n+\kappa\ge0$ anyway, since $D_\kappa[\varphi_n]=\lambda_n+\kappa\ge0$. Step 5: I
    reproduced the split — $\sum_{k\le N}(\lambda_k+\kappa)c_k^2 \ge (\lambda_n+\kappa)
    \sum_{k\le N}c_k^2 + (\lambda_{n+1}-\lambda_n)\sum_{n<k\le N}c_k^2$ using
    $\lambda_k \ge \lambda_{n+1}$ for $k>n$ — and the printed inequality is exactly that, so
    $c_k=0$ for $k>n$ in the limit. Step 6: $n-1$ homogeneous conditions on an $n$-dimensional space
    always have a nonzero solution, and the compactness argument for the maximum is correct
    ($\|\cdot\|_w$ is a norm on $V$ because $w>0$ and elements of $V$ are continuous; $D$ restricted
    to $V$ is a quadratic form, hence continuous). Step 7 is immediate from step 1's
    $D[\varphi_m,\varphi_n]=\lambda_n\delta_{mn}$.
  - Robin claim in the remark, which is ours and uncited: integrating by parts with $u(a)=v(a)=0$
    and $p(b)u'(b)=\sigma u(b)$ gives $\int(pu'v'+quv) - \sigma u(b)v(b) = \int v(-(pu')'+qu)$, so
    the energy form really does acquire $-\sigma u(b)^2$, with the sign as printed.
  - Application: for $wu_t=(pu_x)_x-qu$, separating gives $-(p\varphi')'+q\varphi=\lambda w\varphi$
    with time factor $e^{-\lambda t}$, so the slowest decay rate is $\lambda_1$. Correct. And
    "stiffening does not lower any frequency / adding mass does not raise any" is the $q\ge0$ case of
    the comparison paragraph, read in the right direction.

    quotient; Theorem 1 (minimum principle for the first eigenvalue) is on p. 301 and Theorem 2
    (minimum over functions orthogonal to the earlier eigenfunctions) on pp. 302–303 — both stated
    there *assuming the minimum exists*, which the unit's idea block explicitly declines to assume
    ("To avoid the question of existence, the proof goes the other way"), so the unit's parts 1–2 are
    the inequality form and are proved here rather than taken over. That is a strengthening, honestly
    flagged, not a misreport. §11.2 Theorem 1 (Minimax Principle) and its proof are on pp. 307–308,
    and the proof route — pick a combination of the $n$ trial functions orthogonal to the first $n-1$
    eigenfunctions, apply the minimum principle, then exhibit the span of the first $n$
    eigenfunctions — is exactly the unit's steps 6–7. (Strauss phrases it as "$n$ trial functions"
    and tacitly needs them independent, since otherwise his $w=\sum c_jw_j$ could be zero; the unit's
    "$\dim V = n$" formulation is cleaner and avoids the gap.) §11.3: Theorem 1 on p. 310 is the
    Neumann/"free condition" result, whose proof on pp. 310–311 derives the Neumann condition from
