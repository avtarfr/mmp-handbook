## Findings

1. [blocking] remark, bullet "Positive definiteness is what the inequalities need" (line 107): "Every part above uses $(x,x)>0$ for $x\ne 0$" is false. Take the positive *semi*-definite hermitian form $(x,y) = x_1\overline{y_1}$ on $\mathbb{F}^2$, so $\lVert x\rVert = \lvert x_1\rvert$ and $(x,x)=0$ for every $x=(0,t)$. Parts 1, 4, 5 and 6 are then still exactly true (they are algebraic identities in a hermitian sesquilinear form), and the *inequalities* of parts 2 and 3 still hold. 5000 random pairs in this form gave 0 violations of parts 1, 5, 6, Cauchy-Schwarz and the triangle inequality; e.g. $x=(1,0)$, $y=(0,7)$ gives $(x,y)=0$, $\lVert x+y\rVert^2 = 1 = \lVert x\rVert^2+\lVert y\rVert^2$. Definiteness is used only by: the *equality* characterisation in part 2 (in this form $x=(1,0)$, $y=(1,5)$ give $\lvert(x,y)\rvert = 1 = \lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert$ with $x$ not a multiple of $y$), the equality characterisation in part 3, and the clause "$\lVert\cdot\rVert$ is a norm" in part 3 (here $\lVert(0,1)\rVert = 0$). The bullet's first clause is also misattributed: a nonzero null vector does not break Cauchy-Schwarz, as just shown; what breaks it is *indefiniteness*. In the pseudo-Euclidean form $(x,y)=x_1y_1-x_2y_2$, $x=(1,\tfrac12)$, $y=(1,-\tfrac12)$ give $(x,y)=1.25 > 0.75 = \sqrt{(x,x)(y,y)}$. :cite[Riley, Hobson and Bence]{work=rhb-2006} state Schwarz and the triangle inequality under $\langle a|a\rangle\ge 0$ alone (§8.1.3, p. 246), which contradicts the bullet directly. Fix: say that $(x,x)\ge 0$ is what makes $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ real and is all parts 1, 4, 5, 6 and the two inequalities need; that strict definiteness is needed for $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ to be a norm and for the equality cases in parts 2 and 3; and that what fails in the pseudo-Euclidean case is positivity of $(x,x)$, which costs Cauchy-Schwarz itself (give the Minkowski pair above or cite Hassani's footnote 8, p. 31, for the null vector separately).

2. [blocking] remark, bullet "Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz" (line 106): "The condition in part 3 is genuinely *not* symmetric, which is why it keeps its one-sided form" is false, and it contradicts the page it cites. Equality in $\lVert x+y\rVert\le\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert$ is a symmetric assertion, because $x+y=y+x$ and the right side is symmetric, so *any* correct characterisation of it is a symmetric condition. The condition as written is symmetric: if $y=0$ then $y=0\cdot x$; if $x=cy$ with $c>0$ then $y=c^{-1}x$ with $c^{-1}>0$; if $x=cy$ with $c=0$ then $x=0$, which is the "$y=0$" clause with the roles swapped. Checked on 4000 constructed pairs in $\mathbb{C}^3$ (including $c\in\{0,\tfrac12,1,2,-1,i,-0.3\}$, $x$ or $y$ zero, and unrelated pairs): the condition matched equality in every case and was never satisfied by $(x,y)$ while failing for $(y,x)$. :cite[Kreyszig]{work=kreyszig-1978} says so explicitly in footnote 2 on p. 137 — that the condition for equality in Lemma 3.2-1(b) is "perfectly symmetric" in $x$ and $y$, since $x=0$ is included in $x=cy$ with $c=0$ and $y=kx$ with $k=1/c$ for $c>0$. Fix: keep the one-sided *phrasing* but say it is symmetric for the reason Kreyszig gives (cite the footnote), and drop the contrast with part 2; or delete the sentence.

3. [minor] definition "The standard inner products" (line 78): the parenthetical "(for instance polynomials, or $C([a,b],\mathbb{F})$ with $-\infty<a<b<\infty$)" does not follow from the stated weight conditions. $w(t) = 1/(t-a)$ is real-valued, continuous and strictly positive on $(a,b)$, yet $\int_a^b w(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = \infty$, so $(f,g)$ is undefined for the constant $f=g=1$ in $C([a,b],\mathbb{F})$; polynomials on $(0,\infty)$ with $w\equiv 1$ fail likewise. The governing clause "the space is small enough that the integral exists for all its members" is the right condition, so this is only the illustration being wrong; it is minor rather than blocking also because :cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013} puts the existence condition in the same loose place (Example 2.2.3, pp. 31–32, "for which the integral exists"). Fix: add the condition on the weight, e.g. "polynomials with a weight for which every $\int_a^b w\,t^k\,\mathrm{d}t$ converges, or $C([a,b],\mathbb{F})$ with $-\infty<a<b<\infty$ and $w$ integrable on $[a,b]$".
