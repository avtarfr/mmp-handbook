## Findings
1. [blocking] proof, "Riemann's criterion", *Sufficiency* (line 101): "One exceptional interior point obeying just this limit condition does not spoil Cauchy's integral formula (:ref[]{to=complex.cauchy-integral-formula})" is the crux of the whole unit, and the result it appeals to is in neither prerequisite. `complex.cauchy-integral-formula` states the formula only for "$f$ analytic on and inside $C$" with $z_0$ an interior point (its statement block), and its lemma covers a continuous density on the contour, not an exceptional point *inside*; `complex.cauchy-theorem` has nothing of the kind either (its statement needs analyticity on all of $\Omega$). Ahlfors may assert this because his Theorems 3 and 5 (pp. 109-113) carry exceptional points through Cauchy's theorem from the start, and he says so explicitly on p. 124 ("Cauchy's integral formula remains valid in the presence of a finite number of exceptional points"); that machinery does not exist in this handbook, so as written the step is unjustified and Parts 1, 2 and 4, which all rest on it, inherit the gap. Fix: prove sufficiency from Laurent's theorem, which *is* a listed prerequisite. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, the hypothesis supplies $r$ with $\lvert f(\zeta)\rvert \le \varepsilon/r$ on $\lvert \zeta - z_0\rvert = r$; the Laurent coefficient formula then gives $\lvert a_{-n}\rvert \le r^{n}\sup_{\lvert\zeta-z_0\rvert=r}\lvert f\rvert \le \varepsilon r^{\,n-1}$ for every $n \ge 1$, and since the $a_{-n}$ do not depend on $r$ this forces $a_{-n} = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$, so the regular part is the analytic extension. Alternatively, add the exceptional-point version of Cauchy's formula to `complex.cauchy-integral-formula` and cite that.
2. [minor] remark, second bullet (line 137): "It requires only $(z-z_0)f(z)\to 0$, which boundedness implies but which also allows mild unboundedness such as $\lvert f\rvert \sim \log\lvert z-z_0\rvert^{-1}$." No analytic function on a punctured disc behaves this way: if $\lvert f\rvert \sim \log\lvert z-z_0\rvert^{-1}$ then $(z-z_0)f \to 0$, so the theorem in this very unit makes $f$ extend analytically and hence be bounded. The sentence is therefore vacuous as a statement about the functions the theorem applies to, and invites the reader to think log-growth isolated singularities are a real case of the criterion. Fix: say instead that the criterion is a weaker *hypothesis* to verify -- one need not exhibit a bound, only the limit -- and that boundedness then follows a posteriori.
3. [minor] proof, "No fractional orders" (line 124): "If the extension is not identically zero it has a zero of some finite order $k \ge 1$" leaves the identically-zero case unhandled; the parenthetical at the end covers only the absence of a suitable $\alpha$. The case is in fact excluded by the statement's hypothesis that some $\alpha$ gives limit $\infty$, which rules out $f \equiv 0$. Fix: say so in one clause.

6. [minor] example "Classifying every singularity of one function", at $z = \infty$ (line 165): "multiplying by $w^4/(1-2w)$ cannot cancel them all" is asserted rather than argued, and it is the only step of that classification that is not a computation.
