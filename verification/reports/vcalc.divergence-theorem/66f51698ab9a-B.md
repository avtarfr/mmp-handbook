## Findings
1. [blocking] definition "Simple region" (line 39): "A box, a tetrahedron, a parallelepiped and a convex polyhedron are simple regions" is false for all but specially placed cases, because the definition one line above demands $\psi_1 \le \psi_2$ **of class $C^1$ on $D$**, and a polyhedron whose top or bottom is made of more than one face has a crease there.

   Counterexample, airtight because $D$, $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are forced by $V$: take the regular octahedron $V = \{|x|+|y|+|z| \le 1\}$, a convex polyhedron. Its $z$-extent over $(x,y)$ is $|z| \le 1-|x|-|y|$, so necessarily $D = \{|x|+|y|\le 1\}$ and $\psi_2 = 1-|x|-|y|$. At the interior point $(0,\tfrac12)$ of $D$, $\partial\psi_2/\partial x$ jumps from $+1$ to $-1$, so $\psi_2 \notin C^1(D)$ and $V$ is not $z$-simple; by symmetry it is not $x$- or $y$-simple either. The same failure hits a generic tetrahedron — e.g. vertices $(0,0,0)$, $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$, $(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,1)$, whose shadow is the triangle $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$ and whose upper boundary is three planar faces meeting at creases above the segments from $(\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$ to the three corners — and a generic parallelepiped, e.g. a cube resting on one vertex, whose top is three faces. Only an axis-aligned box, and the special tetrahedra and parallelepipeds with one face up and one face down in each coordinate direction, satisfy the definition. (The unit's own Example 1 tetrahedron, $\{x,y,z\ge0,\;x+y+z\le2\}$, is one of the special ones: $\psi_1 = 0$ and $\psi_2 = 2-x-y$ are affine, so "This $V$ is a simple region" at line 150 is correct.)

   This matters beyond the sentence, because the remark at line 145 and Step 3 both lean on readers knowing which solids are simple, and because the honest statement is the one Step 5 already supplies. Fix: replace the sentence with something like "An axis-aligned box is a simple region, and so is a tetrahedron or parallelepiped placed with one face up and one face down in each coordinate direction; a general convex polyhedron is not — its top may be several faces meeting at a crease — but it is a finite union of simple regions, which is what Step 5 needs." Also drop or rephrase the clause "so is the solid between two $C^1$ graphs when it is also simple the other two ways", which is fine as it stands.

2. [minor] proof (line 83): the block's `src` cites `rhb-2006:§11.3, pp. 384–385`, but the frontmatter `rhb-2006` entry declares `pages: "401, 403-405"` and its `loc` never mentions §11.3. The cited pages do exist and do support the block — §11.3 "Green's theorem in a plane" on pp. 384–385 is precisely the iterated-integral-plus-FTC argument that Steps 1–3 generalize — so this is a bookkeeping gap, not a bad citation. Fix: add 384–385 to the declared pages and name §11.3 in the `loc`.

3. [minor] remark, third bullet (line 143): "Simple connectivity is not required. Nothing in the proof uses it" is correct and is Riley, Hobson and Bence's explicit position (p. 401), and Strauss and Evans require nothing of the kind. But Arfken, cited in this same unit for the companion identities and the tetrahedron check, states Gauss's theorem for "a simply connected region of $\mathbb{R}^3$" (p. 164) and says in so many words that a region with a hole is excluded. A reader who follows the unit's own citations will meet the clash. Fix: one clause in that bullet noting that Arfken carries a simple-connectivity hypothesis that the other three sources do not, and that we follow the others. (I have recorded Arfken's hypothesis as a source discrepancy; the fix asked for here is only the cross-reference, not a change of substance.)
