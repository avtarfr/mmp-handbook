## Checked
- Counterexample search: probed the $\varphi_1 \ne 0$ hypothesis. For $y''+y = 0$ with $\varphi_1 = \sin x$ the formula is valid on $(0,\pi)$ and the answer $-\cos x$ happens to extend to $\mathbb{R}$, but the inner integral $\int\csc^2$ diverges at every zero of $\sin$ -- confirming the remark. Checked the remark's claim about the behaviour near a simple zero: with $\varphi_1 = \sin x$, $x_1 = 0$, $\int\csc^2 = -\cot x \sim -1/x$ and $\varphi_2 = \sin x(-\cot x) \to -1$, a finite nonzero limit, exactly as stated. Checked that a solution of a normalised second-order equation cannot have a double zero (if $\varphi(x_1)=\varphi'(x_1)=0$ then $\varphi\equiv0$), which is what makes the remark's "higher-order zero only at a singular point" true. Verified the general-order reduced equation $(\mathrm{R})$: from $b_i = \sum_{m\ge i}\binom{m}{i}a_{n-m}\varphi_1^{(m-i)}$ with $a_0 = 1$, the coefficient of $v$ is $b_1 = \sum_{m\ge1} m\,a_{n-m}\varphi_1^{(m-1)} = n\varphi_1^{(n-1)} + (n-1)a_1\varphi_1^{(n-2)} + \cdots + a_{n-1}\varphi_1$ and the leading coefficient is $b_n = \varphi_1$ -- both as displayed. For $n=2$ this gives $\varphi_1 v' + (2\varphi_1'+a_1\varphi_1)v = 0$, and multiplying by $\varphi_1 e^{A}$ makes the left side $\frac{d}{dx}(\varphi_1^2e^{A}v)$ -- the integrating factor claimed at line 180 is correct.
- Calculations (by hand and symbolically):
  - Example 1 (Legendre, $\ell=1$, $I = (0,1)$, $x_0 = \tfrac12$): $a_1 = -2x/(1-x^2)$, $A(s) = [\ln(1-t^2)]_{1/2}^{s} = \ln\frac{1-s^2}{3/4}$, $e^{-A(s)} = \tfrac34(1-s^2)^{-1}$. Partial fractions: $\frac{1}{s^2(1-s^2)} = \frac{1}{s^2}+\frac{1}{1-s^2}$ (checked: numerator $(1-s^2)+s^2 = 1$), so $\int = -\frac1s + \frac12\ln\frac{1+s}{1-s}$. Hence $\varphi_2 = x(-\tfrac1x + \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}) = \tfrac{x}{2}\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x} - 1$, and sympy confirms this solves $(1-x^2)y''-2xy'+2y=0$ with $W(x,\varphi_2) = 1/(1-x^2)$, consistent with step 5's bookkeeping ($e^{-A} = \tfrac34(1-x^2)^{-1}$ for the normalised $\varphi_2$, the factor $\tfrac34$ removed). $\varphi_2(0) = -1$, as step 6 says.
  - Example 2 ($xy''-(x+2)y'+2y=0$ on $x>0$): $\varphi_1 = e^x$ gives $e^x(x-x-2+2) = 0$. Normalised $a_1 = -1-2/x$; $A(s) = -(s-1)-2\ln s$; $e^{-A(s)} = e^{s-1}s^2$; integrand $e^{-1}s^2e^{-s}$; $\int_1^{x}s^2e^{-s}ds = [-(s^2+2s+2)e^{-s}]_1^{x} = 5e^{-1}-(x^2+2x+2)e^{-x}$ (I verified $\frac{d}{ds}[-(s^2+2s+2)e^{-s}] = s^2e^{-s}$ and the value $5e^{-1}$ at $s=1$). So $\varphi_2 = 5e^{x-2}-e^{-1}(x^2+2x+2)$, and after dropping the multiple of $\varphi_1$ and the constant, $\varphi_2 = x^2+2x+2$. Substitution: $2x-(x+2)(2x+2)+2(x^2+2x+2) = 2x-2x^2-6x-4+2x^2+4x+4 = 0$ -- matches the printed expansion term for term. $W(e^x, x^2+2x+2) = e^x(2x+2-x^2-2x-2) = -x^2e^x$, which is $-e$ times $e^{-A(x)} = x^2e^{x-1}$, so the "constant multiple" claim is right (the constant is $-e$).
  - Critical damping in the application: $\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\gamma^2x = 0$, $a_1 = 2\gamma$, $e^{-A} = e^{-2\gamma t}$, $\varphi_1^{-2} = e^{2\gamma t}$, integrand $1$, $\varphi_2 = te^{-\gamma t}$ -- correct.
  - Euler/Frobenius claim in the application: $\int\varphi_1^{-2}e^{-A}$ reduces to $\int ds/s$ exactly when the exponent is $-1$; for $x^2y''+axy'+by=0$ with $\varphi_1 = x^{r_1}$ this is $-2r_1-a = -1$, i.e. $r_1 = (1-a)/2$, the double root -- consistent with the Euler unit.
