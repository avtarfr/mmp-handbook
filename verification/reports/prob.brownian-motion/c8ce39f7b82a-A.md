## Findings
1. [blocking] idea (line 109): "A distribution that is an $n$-fold convolution of identical pieces for every $n$, with finite variance, has to be normal" is false. Infinite divisibility plus finite variance does not force normality: $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ is the $n$-fold convolution of $n$ i.i.d. $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda/n)$ variables for every $n$ and has variance $\lambda < \infty$, and the same holds for every gamma and every compound-Poisson law with a square-integrable jump size. What the triangular-array central limit theorem actually needs in addition is a uniform asymptotic negligibility (Lindeberg) condition on the $n$ pieces, and that is exactly what fails for the Poisson pieces. The sentence is in an `original` block, so the handbook owns it. Fix: state it as Folland does - the subdivision argument *suggests* normality but does not prove it - or add the negligibility hypothesis explicitly and say that it is an extra assumption.

2. [blocking] definition (line 47), and the same claim in the opening note (line 27): "Normality and the proportionality of the variance to $t-s$ are then *deduced* rather than assumed, by the subdivision argument" misreports Folland. On p. 331 Folland writes that, after the subdivision, "the central limit theorem *suggests* that the distribution of $X_t - X_s$ should be normal, and this conclusion is also supported by experimental evidence", and that the equal-variance identity "strongly indicates" proportionality; he then *postulates* normality with variance $C(t-s)$ as his third assumption (10.22). Saying the source deduces what it assumes weakens/garbles its hypothesis structure, and it also contradicts this unit's own preceding sentence, which calls (10.20)-(10.22) "physical postulates", and the note at line 27 ("forced on us ... says the increments must be normal"). Fix: say the subdivision argument motivates postulate (10.22), which Folland still takes as an assumption.

3. [blocking] prereqs, and the two proof blocks that use them (lines 137 and 209): two named results are used that no listed prerequisite supplies. (a) Step 3 of the scaling-limit proof invokes Slutsky's theorem ("a summand tending to $0$ in probability leaves a limit in distribution unchanged") as the justification for passing from $a_nY_n$ to $\sqrt{d_j}Y_n$; naming it is not proving it, and the scope of `prob.convergence-random-variables` (the four modes, the implications, Borel-Cantelli) does not contain it. (b) Step 3 of the quadratic-variation proof applies Chebyshev's inequality with an explicit `:ref` to `prob.probability-inequalities`, which is not in `prereqs` (it appears only transitively, as a prerequisite of `prob.convergence-random-variables`). Fix: add `prob.probability-inequalities` to `prereqs`, and either add the Slutsky step to a listed prerequisite or avoid it - here it is avoidable, since $\mathbb{E}(e^{icD_{n,j}}) = \mathbb{E}(e^{i(ca_n)Y_n})$ and the characteristic function of $Y_n$ converges uniformly on compact sets of its argument, which gives the displayed limit directly.

4. [minor] statement "Path properties" part 2 (line 96) and proof step 4 (line 215): the statement puts "almost surely" outside "on every interval $[a,b]$", but the proof establishes only, for each fixed interval, that the total variation is infinite almost surely ("the argument on a general $[a,b]$ is the same with the origin shifted"). The stronger order of quantifiers needs one more line. Fix: run the argument on the countably many intervals with rational endpoints, intersect those full-probability events, and note that total variation is monotone in the interval, so every $[a,b]$ contains a rational subinterval on which it is already infinite.

5. [minor] proof "Covariance, and the scaling limit", step 3 (line 133): the step opens "Let $d_j = t_j - t_{j-1} > 0$", but the claim (line 75) allows $t_1 = 0$, and with the convention $t_0 = 0$ introduced in step 2 this gives $d_1 = 0$, so $m_n = 0$ for all $n$ and "$m_n \to \infty$" fails. The conclusion is still true ($D_{n,1} \equiv 0$), but the case is not covered. Fix: say at the start of step 3 that if $t_1 = 0$ then $W_n(t_1) = W(t_1) = 0$ for every $n$ and that index may be dropped, so one may assume $d_j > 0$ throughout.

6. [minor] statement "Path properties" part 3 (line 101): "part 2 ... already rules out differentiability in any averaged sense" is too vague to check, and the obvious reading of it is false - infinite total variation does not by itself rule out differentiability at every point, as $f(x) = x^{2}\sin(1/x^{2})$ (with $f(0) = 0$) is differentiable everywhere on $[0,1]$ and yet has unbounded variation there. Fix: say precisely what part 2 does rule out, for example that no sample path can be continuously differentiable, or have a bounded derivative, on any interval.

7. [source] proof "Existence of a version with continuous paths", step 1 (lines 158-162): with the stated convention $X_0 \equiv 0$, the assertion "Since $X_j$ and $X_{j-1}$ agree at every point of $\mathcal{D}_{j-1}$, the function $Z_j$ vanishes there" fails at $j = 1$, because $Z_1(1) = X_1(1) - X_0(1) = X(1) \ne 0$ almost surely. Consequently $\sup_{[0,1]}|Z_1|$ is a maximum over the two points $1/2$ and $1$, not over the single odd point promised by the count $2^{j-1}$, and step 2 gives $\mathrm{var}(Z_1(1/2)) = \mathrm{var}(X(1/2)) = 1/2$ rather than $2^{-j-1} = 1/4$. This is copied faithfully from Grimmett and Stirzaker p. 588, who set $X_0(t) = 0$ and then compute $\mathrm{var}(Z_j(2^{-j}))$ as though $X_0$ were the linear interpolant of $X$ on $\mathcal{D}_0$; by the source-authority rule it is recorded, not failed. It is harmless (level $j = 1$ contributes one almost surely finite term to $\sum_j \sup|Z_j|$), and it would be removed by taking $X_0(t) = tX(1)$ or by starting the series at $j = 2$. Recorded with `tools/discrepancy.mjs` against grimmett-stirzaker-2020, "§13.3, proof of Theorem (13.3.19), p. 588".
