## Findings
1. [minor] frontmatter `summary` (line 6): "the real ones reduce to rotations and reflections" is
   accurate for the $2\times2$ case and for the canonical form read as a decomposition, but a reader
   can take it as "every real orthogonal matrix is a rotation or a reflection", which is false for
   $n \ge 3$: $-I_3$ is orthogonal with $\det = -1$ and has spectrum $(-1,-1,-1)$, so it is neither a
   rotation nor a reflection in a hyperplane (which has spectrum $(1,\dots,1,-1)$). Fix: "...and that
   in two dimensions the real ones are exactly the rotations and reflections, and in general reduce
   to a block sum of plane rotations and $\pm 1$."
2. [minor] statement "Rotations and reflections", part 3 (line 80): "A real orthogonal matrix
   therefore cannot in general be diagonalized over $\mathbb{R}$; this canonical form is as close as
   one can get" sits close to the phrasing of the cited Hassani theorem (p. 201: cannot in general be
   completely diagonalized / the closest it can get to a diagonal form). The displayed canonical form
   itself cannot be reworded and the block's structure is the unit's own, so I do not treat this as a
   close paraphrase, but one short gloss sentence is echoing the source and is cheap to reword.
   Fix: e.g. "Over $\mathbb{R}$ this block form, not a diagonal matrix, is the normal form: the
   $2\times2$ blocks cannot be split further without leaving $\mathbb{R}$."

## Checked
- Counterexample search (statements as written):
  - "Characterisation" 1-4: the equivalences were tested against the unit's own inner-product
    convention $(x,y) = y^\dagger x$ fixed in the definition block, which matters here. Both stated
    polarization identities hold in that convention and were verified on 2000 random complex pairs in
    dimensions 1-4 (max deviation $< 10^{-10}$): $2\operatorname{Re}(x,y) = \|x+y\|^2-\|x\|^2-\|y\|^2$
    and $2\operatorname{Im}(x,y) = \|x+iy\|^2-\|x\|^2-\|y\|^2$. I confirmed the unit's claim that the
    second is the first with $y \mapsto iy$: $\operatorname{Re}(x,iy) = \operatorname{Re}(-i(x,y))
    = \operatorname{Im}(x,y)$ in this convention. Under the opposite convention $(x,y)=x^\dagger y$ the
    imaginary identity would carry the other sign, so the unit is internally consistent only because
    the convention is stated — it is. Item 2's "and likewise for the rows" is sound because $U$ is
    square ($U^\dagger U = I \iff UU^\dagger = I$), as the definition block records.
  - Degenerate cases: $n=1$ (unitary $= $ unit modulus scalar), $U=I$, $U$ with a zero column (not
    unitary, correctly excluded), the singular $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ of the remark
    (sends $e_2 \mapsto e_1$, $e_1 \mapsto 0$ — verified), and $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$
    (determinant exactly $1$, $M^{\mathsf T}M = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} \ne I$ — verified),
    which is the unit's stated non-characterisation of part 1. No counterexample found.
  - "Rotations and reflections" part 2 (completeness of the $R(\theta)/F(\theta)$ dichotomy): drew 500
    random $2\times2$ real orthogonal matrices (QR of Gaussian matrices), split by sign of the
    determinant, and reconstructed each as $R(\theta)$ or $F(\theta)$ with
    $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(Q_{21},Q_{11})$. All 500 matched to machine precision. Symbolically,
    $F^{\mathsf T}F = I$, $\det F = -1$, $\det R = +1$; $F(\theta)$ fixes
    $(\cos\frac\theta2, \sin\frac\theta2)$ and negates $(-\sin\frac\theta2, \cos\frac\theta2)$ exactly
    (both residuals simplify to $0$), confirming "reflection in the line at angle $\theta/2$".
  - "Rotations and reflections" part 3: for 200 random real orthogonal matrices of sizes 2-6 the real
    Schur form is block diagonal with every $1\times1$ block $\pm1$ and every $2\times2$ block of the
    form $\begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}$, and the transforming matrix is orthogonal. No case
    needed a non-rotation $2\times2$ block, consistent with the stated form.
  - The non-uniqueness remark: $R(0) = I_2$ and $R(\pi) = \operatorname{diag}(-1,-1)$ confirmed
    symbolically, so the same $Q$ really does admit displays with different $N_\pm$ and $m$ when
    $\theta_j$ is only restricted to $[0,2\pi)$; eigenvalues of $R(\theta)$ are
    $\cos\theta \pm i|\sin\theta|$, non-real exactly when $\sin\theta \ne 0$, which is why
    $\theta_j \in (0,\pi)$ is the right restriction for uniqueness. The remark is correct and it is
    honest that the source states the form without it.
  - The "orthogonal columns is not orthogonal matrix" remark: $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = D$ with
    $D_{jj} = \|q_j\|^2 > 0$ and $\|Qe_j\| = \sqrt{D_{jj}}$ — correct as stated, including the
    zero-column degeneracy.
