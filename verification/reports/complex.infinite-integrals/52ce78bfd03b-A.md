## Findings
1. [minor] statement, Jordan's lemma (line 67): the parenthetical justification has a small gap. $f$ is assumed "analytic in the upper half-plane except at finitely many points", and the text concludes that for $R$ larger than the modulus of all of them "$f$ is then analytic, hence continuous, on $\Gamma_R$". But $\Gamma_R$ includes its endpoints $\pm R$, which lie on the real axis, and analyticity on the *open* half-plane says nothing there. Riley, Hobson and Bence's condition (i) on p. 862 is stated for $\operatorname{Im} z \ge 0$, which closes the gap. Fix: say "analytic on $\{\operatorname{Im}z \ge 0\}$ except at finitely many points", matching the convention the two evaluation statements already use ("analytic on $\mathbb{R}$ and in the upper half-plane").
2. [minor] method, steps 2-3 (lines 172-173): step 2 allows closing in the lower half-plane when no exponential is present, but step 3 then says "Compute $M(R)$", and $M(R)$ is reserved by the definition block for the *upper* arc, where the same block warns that a lower-half-plane semicircle "gets its own maximum, which is in general unrelated to $M(R)$". As written the recipe tells a reader who closed downwards to check the wrong quantity. Fix: step 3 should say "compute the maximum of $\lvert f\rvert$ on the arc you are using".
3. [minor] definition (line 39): "the argument of $z$ seen from an interior point decreases, from $0$ at $R$ through $-\pi/2$ at $-iR$ to $-\pi$ at $-R$". The arguments quoted are taken about the origin, which is a *boundary* point of the lower half-disc, not an interior one, so "seen from an interior point" misdescribes the computation it introduces. The orientation conclusion is correct and the preceding "region lies on the right" argument already establishes it. Fix: drop "seen from an interior point", or say "the argument of $z$ about the origin".
4. [minor] remark, third bullet (line 189): the numbers are introduced as values of $R\,M(R)$ ("numerically $R\,M(R) = 0.2171$ at $R = 100$") and then the closing parenthesis says "the $R\,M(R)$ values above are $1/\ln R$ with constant $1$", as if they were that function exactly. They are not: I computed the true maxima as $0.21713352$ at $R=100$ against $1/\ln 100 = 0.21714724$, and $0.054286810238$ at $R=10^8$ against $1/\ln 10^8 = 0.054286810238$. The quoted four digits are right either way, but the parenthesis overstates. Fix: "which agree with $1/\ln R$ to the digits shown".
5. [minor] statement, arc lemma (line 63): "For a rational $f = p/q$ this holds exactly when $\deg q \ge \deg p + 2$" needs $p$ and $q$ to have no common factor, otherwise the degrees are not the right ones to compare. Fix: "in lowest terms".

## Checked
Arc lemma: re-derived the $ML$ bound, confirming arc length $(\theta_2-\theta_1)R$ and $\max_C\lvert zf(z)\rvert = R\,M_C(R)$ since $\lvert z\rvert = R$ on the arc, so that the hypothesis and the conclusion are the same statement; and that for rational $f$, $\lvert zf\rvert \sim \lvert z\rvert^{\deg p + 1 - \deg q}$ gives the degree condition.

Jordan's lemma: re-derived $\lvert e^{iaz}\rvert = e^{-aR\sin\theta}$, the symmetry of $e^{-aR\sin\theta}$ about $\theta = \pi/2$, the chord bound $\sin\theta \ge 2\theta/\pi$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ from $(\sin)'' = -\sin \le 0$, and the explicit integral $\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-2aR\theta/\pi}d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2aR}(1-e^{-aR})$, hence $R\int_0^\pi e^{-aR\sin\theta}d\theta \le \frac{\pi}{a}(1-e^{-aR}) < \pi/a$ and the quantitative bound $\pi M(R)/a$. This is Arfken's own chain at Eq. (11.101) and the display after it, with the same constant, and the unit's note on where $a>0$ enters matches his.

Evaluation 2 (the rectangle): checked each side. $m(X) = \sup_{R\ge X}M(R)$ does equal $\sup\{\lvert f\rvert : \lvert z\rvert \ge X,\ \operatorname{Im}z \ge 0\}$, is finite once $X$ exceeds all singularity moduli, is non-increasing and tends to $0$ iff $M(R)\to0$; the right side satisfies $\lvert z\rvert \ge X_2$ and $\lvert e^{iaz}\rvert = e^{-ay}$, giving $\int_0^\infty m(X_2)e^{-ay}dy = m(X_2)/a$, which also shows the side integral still converges after $Y\to\infty$; the top side is bounded by $m(Y)e^{-aY}(X_1+X_2) \to 0$ for fixed $X_1,X_2$. So the final inequality and the independent limits are right. Compared with Ahlfors p. 157: he runs the same rectangle but under the stronger hypothesis "$\lvert zR(z)\rvert$ is bounded", getting a bound $\propto 1/X_2$ on the vertical side; the unit says plainly that the only hypothesis available here is $M(R)\to0$ and substitutes $m(X)$, which is an honest adaptation, not a misreport, and it is correct.

Orientation in the definition block: verified that running $[-R,R]$ then the arc through $-iR$ back to $-R$ is clockwise, so the sign on the residue sum is $-2\pi i$, and that the stated repair (run the axis from $R$ to $-R$) gives the positively oriented boundary.

Sources: Ahlfors p. 156, case 2, says the integral of a rational function "converges if and only if ... the degree of the denominator is at least two units higher than the degree of the numerator, and if no pole lies on the real axis" -- exactly what line 90 attributes to him; case 3 on pp. 156-157 is the simple-zero-at-infinity discussion and the rectangle, as attributed, including his explicit point that symmetric limits would not settle convergence. Riley, Hobson and Bence p. 862 condition (ii) is "$R$ times the maximum of $\lvert f\rvert$ on $\Gamma$ tends to zero" and condition (iii) is "$\int_{-\infty}^0 f$ and $\int_0^\infty f$ both exist"; p. 864 Jordan's condition (ii) is "the maximum of $\lvert f(z)\rvert \to 0$". So both comparisons in the remark block are accurate. Arfken p. 528 does carry the sentence that "for Jordan's lemma the upper and lower half-planes are not equivalent, because the condition $a>0$ ...", as the fourth remark bullet says; and Example 11.8.6 on p. 531 is indeed $\int_0^\infty dx/(x^3+1)$ on a circular sector of opening $2\pi/3$, which both step 6 and the last application bullet cite correctly. All three prereqs are used (residue theorem and its simple-pole quotient rule; isolated singularities; the $ML$ estimate from contour integrals) and nothing outside them is.

Numerically, with mpmath at 20-25 digits. The counterexample $f(z) = 1/((z+i)\operatorname{Log}(z+i))$: checked that $\operatorname{Im}(z+i)\ge1$ on the closed upper half-plane, so the principal cut and the zero of $\operatorname{Log}$ at $1$ are both avoided and $f$ is singularity-free there; computed the true maxima over the arc, $R\,M(R) = 0.21713$ at $R=100$ and $0.0542868$ at $R=10^8$, matching the unit's $0.2171$ and $0.05429$; computed $\lvert\int_{-R}^R f\rvert = 0.333272,\ 0.225010,\ 0.169529$ at $R = 10^4, 10^6, 10^8$ -- so the quoted $0.225$ at $10^6$ is right -- with products against $\ln R$ of $3.06955$, $3.10863$, $3.12284$, matching the unit's $3.0696$, $3.1086$, $3.1228$ and climbing towards $\pi$; and $\operatorname{Re}\int_1^X f = 1.6798$ at $X = 10^2$ and $3.28923$ at $X = 10^{10}$, a difference of $1.6094$ against $\ln\ln 10^{10} - \ln\ln 10^2 = 1.6094$, so the $\ln\ln$ growth and the divergence of $\int_1^\infty f$ are real. The example therefore does what the bullet claims: every hypothesis of part 1 holds, the symmetric limit is $0$, and $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f$ does not exist.

Example 1: verified the four poles $2e^{i(2k+1)\pi/4}$, that only $\sqrt2(1+i)$ and $\sqrt2(-1+i)$ are in the upper half-plane, the residue $z_j^2/(4z_j^3) = 1/(4z_j)$, the two values $(1-i)/(8\sqrt2)$ and $(-1-i)/(8\sqrt2)$, their sum $-i/(4\sqrt2)$, and $I = \sqrt2\pi/8$; quadrature gives $0.55536036726979578088$, equal to $\sqrt2\pi/8$ to 20 digits. Example 2: verified $M(R) = 1/(R^2-9)$ and $R/(R^2-9)$ are the exact maxima (attained at $z = iR$), so $R\,M(R)\to1\ne0$ for $J_2$ and Jordan's lemma really is the only route; the residues $e^{-6}/(6i)$ and $e^{-6}/2$ at $z = 3i$; and $J_1 = \pi e^{-6}/3$, $J_2 = \pi e^{-6}$, which oscillatory quadrature reproduces as $0.0025957432094282470$ and $0.0077872296282847409$, matching the printed ten digits. The by-products (the two odd integrals vanishing) are correct.
