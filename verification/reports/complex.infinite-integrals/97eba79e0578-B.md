## Findings
1. [blocking] statement "The resulting evaluations", part 1 (lines 74-78): "Let $f$ be analytic on $\mathbb{R}$ and in the upper half-plane except for finitely many isolated singularities, with $R\,M(R)\to0$. Then $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx$ **converges** and equals $2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}$." Under the ordinary meaning of an improper integral over $\mathbb{R}$ (both limits independent) this is false, and the unit's own part 2 shows it knows the distinction matters, since it spells the independent-limit reading out there. Counterexample: $f(z) = 1/\bigl((z+i)\operatorname{Log}(z+i)\bigr)$ with the principal branch. Its branch cut ($z + i \in (-\infty,0]$, i.e. the horizontal ray leftwards from $-i$) and the zero of the logarithm ($z = 1-i$) both lie in the lower half-plane, so $f$ is analytic on $\mathbb{R}$ and on the whole closed upper half-plane with no singularities at all; on the upper arc $\lvert f\rvert \approx 1/(R\log R)$, so $M(R) \to 0$ and $R\,M(R) \approx 1/\log R \to 0$, satisfying the hypothesis. But on the real axis $f(x) \approx 1/(x\log x)$ as $x\to+\infty$, so $\int_1^{X}f\,dx$ grows like $\log\log X$ and $\int_0^\infty f$ diverges. The symmetric limit $\lim_R\int_{-R}^{R}f$ does exist and equals $0 = 2\pi i\cdot 0$, which is all the proof establishes: the proof's one sentence about absolute convergence is explicitly restricted to "the rational case", while the statement's hypotheses are not. Fix: either restrict part 1 to rational $f$ with $\deg q \ge \deg p + 2$ (which is what Ahlfors p. 156 asserts, and then absolute convergence is genuine), or state the conclusion as "the symmetric limit $\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}f(x)\,dx$ exists and equals ...", adding that for rational $f$ the integral converges absolutely so the symmetric limit is the integral.
