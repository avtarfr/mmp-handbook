## Findings
1. [blocking] remark "Where the hypotheses are used...", second paragraph (line 220): the discussion of what fails without continuity at the origin runs two different objects together and gets both the intermediate claim and the conclusion wrong. The process it describes — one "whose $\rho$ jumps from $1$ at $t=0$ to $0$ for $t\ne 0$" — has constant mean and a covariance depending on the lag alone, so by the unit's own definition block it *is* weakly stationary, with finite variance $c(0)$; what fails for it is only the continuity hypothesis, which is the point the paragraph set out to make. So "The idealised white noise of physics is therefore not a weakly stationary process in the sense used here" does not follow and contradicts the definition given earlier in the unit. The intermediate claim is also wrong for that process: since $\rho$ vanishes at every $t\ne0$, its formal transform $(2\pi)^{-1}\int\rho(t)e^{-it\lambda}dt$ is $0$, not a positive constant. The flat spectrum of infinite total mass belongs to the delta-correlated object $c(t)=\sigma^2\delta(t)$, which is a different thing: it has $c(0)=\infty$ and so is not a second-order process at all. Fix: separate the two. Say that the discontinuous-$\rho$ process is weakly stationary but, $\rho$ being discontinuous at $0$, has no spectral representation at all (no $F$ exists, since characteristic functions are continuous); then say that physicists' white noise, with $c(t)=\sigma^2\delta(t)$, has infinite variance, is not a process in this sense, and is the one with the formally flat, non-integrable spectrum.
2. [minor] definition "Autocovariance, autocorrelation, spectral distribution and density" (line 75): "$c(0)\,dF(\lambda)$ is the variance itself per unit frequency" mixes a measure with a density. $c(0)\,dF(\lambda)$ is the variance carried by $[\lambda,\lambda+d\lambda]$; the variance *per unit frequency* is $c(0)f(\lambda)$ where the density exists. Fix: "$c(0)\,dF(\lambda)$ is the variance carried by the frequencies in $[\lambda,\lambda+d\lambda]$, so $c(0)f(\lambda)$ is the variance per unit frequency".
3. [minor] frontmatter `prereqs` (line 7): `fourier.fourier-inversion` is listed but never used or referenced. The proofs use Cauchy-Schwarz and the covariance algebra (`prob.expectation-variance`), the uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions (`prob.characteristic-functions`, correctly :ref'd), the handbook's transform convention (`fourier.fourier-transform`, correctly :ref'd), the Weierstrass M-test and orthogonality of $e^{in\lambda}$ on a period — the last is Fourier *series*, not the inversion theorem for the transform on the line. Fix: drop it, or cite it where the conventions table converts between $f$, $\hat\rho$ and $\hat c$.
4. [source] statement "The spectral theorem...", Inversion paragraph (lines 108-114): G&S Theorem (9.3.15)/(9.3.16), p. 442, holds the inversion formula "at every point $\lambda$ at which $f$ is differentiable", a condition imposed on the density rather than on $\widetilde F$, which the book itself never verifies when it applies the formula (Examples (9.3.19) and (9.3.23)). The unit reports the hypothesis exactly as printed and flags it in the remark, which is the right handling; an entry for this is already in `verification/discrepancies.md` under this unit, so I have not added a duplicate. Not a defect in our text.
