## Findings
1. [blocking] application "Densities and likelihood ratios in probability" (line 230): "Both are absolutely continuous with respect to $m$, hence equivalent to each other" is a non-sequitur, and the general rule it asserts is false: the uniform law on $(0,1)$ and the uniform law on $(1,2)$ are both $\ll m$ and are mutually singular. The conclusion happens to be true here, but only because both densities are strictly positive on $(0,\infty)$, which is what gives $m \ll P$ and $m \ll Q$ and hence $P \equiv Q$. As written the sentence also leaves the chain-rule step unsupported: part 2 of the calculus statement needs $Q \ll P$, which is exactly what the missing argument supplies. Fix: replace "hence equivalent to each other" by something like "and both densities are strictly positive on $(0,\infty)$, so each is equivalent to $m$ and hence to the other".
2. [minor] proof, Step 8 (line 125): "Apply Steps 1-7 ... this produces $B_j \subseteq X_j$ ... and a measurable $f_j \ge 0$ vanishing off $X_j$". Steps 1-7 do not produce either containment. $B_j = \{F_j = 1\}$ with $F_j$ determined only up to an $\alpha_j$-null set, and $\alpha_j$ vanishes off $X_j$, so a representative with $F_j \equiv 1$ off $X_j$ is admissible and would give $B_j \supseteq X \setminus X_j$. Both claims are load-bearing: $\mu(B_j) = 0$ (Steps 1-7 only give $\mu(B_j \cap X_j) = 0$), the disjointness of the $B_j$ that turns $\sum_j \nu(E \cap B_j)$ into $\nu(E \cap B)$, and the identification $\int_E f_j\,d\mu_j = \int_E f_j\,d\mu$ all use them. Fix: add the normalisation explicitly, as Folland does ("we may assume $f_j = 0$ on $A_j^c$", Thm 3.8 Case II, p. 90) — choose the representative $F_j = 0$ off $X_j$, which is legitimate because $\alpha_j(X \setminus X_j) = 0$.

[minor] definition (line 42): "When $\nu \ll \mu$ and such an $f$ exists it is unique up to $\mu$-a.e. equality (Step 9 below)" is stated with no finiteness hypothesis, but the definition block's standing assumptions are only "$(X,\mathcal{M})$ is a measurable space and all measures are defined on $\mathcal{M}$". Step 9 does assume the $\sigma$-finite partition. Fix: qualify with "for $\sigma$-finite $\mu$ and $\nu$", or say "under the hypotheses of the theorem below".

The passage to $\mu$-a.e. is immediate ($\mu \ll \lambda$, so a $\lambda$-null set is $\mu$-null), but it is not written. Fix: add that clause.
5. [minor] application (line 240): "the tower property is the chain rule in disguise". The tower property $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]\mid\mathcal{H}] = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{H}]$ for $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ is a statement about successive restrictions of the *same* measure to smaller $\sigma$-algebras, not an instance of $d\nu/d\lambda = (d\nu/d\mu)(d\mu/d\lambda)$, in which three different measures on one $\sigma$-algebra appear and the derivatives multiply. Fix: say it follows from the same uniqueness argument, or drop the identification.

[source] note "The version in the sources, and what we prove" (line 66): the unit's report of :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="Thms I.19 and I.20, p. 23"} is exact — both are stated for measures on a measure space with no $\sigma$-finiteness hypothesis, and I.20 asserts uniqueness.
