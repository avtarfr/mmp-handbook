## Findings
1. [minor] frontmatter `prereqs` (line 7): `complex.contour-integrals` is relied on but not listed. The definition block's substitution is precisely the parametrisation definition of a contour integral — "$\theta$ running from $0$ to $2\pi$ traces the unit circle once counterclockwise" and $d\theta = dz/(iz)$ — which is that unit's content, not the residue theorem's. Fix: add `complex.contour-integrals` to `prereqs` (the style guide asks for every prerequisite relied on to be named, not only the deepest one).
2. [minor] definition block (line 33): "a rational function of two variables, finite for all $(s,c)$ with $s^2+c^2=1$". For a rational function "finite at a point" is ambiguous where numerator and denominator vanish together: such a point is not in the function's domain, yet the function is finite near it. Fix: "whose denominator does not vanish at any $(s,c)$ with $s^2+c^2=1$", which is what the argument in the rest of the block and step 4 of the method actually use.
3. [minor] method, step 6 (line 71): "If $R \ge 0$ on the circle, the answer must also be positive" should be non-negative, or positive given that $R$ does not vanish identically. Fix: say "non-negative, and positive unless $R$ vanishes identically on the circle".

## Checked
Re-derived the substitution and all three examples by hand and then confirmed each closed form against numerical quadrature with `.venv/bin/python` (mpmath, 20 digits).

Definition and method: $\sin\theta=(z-z^{-1})/2i$, $\cos\theta=(z+z^{-1})/2$, $d\theta=dz/(iz)$ and the $k$-fold versions are right, and the argument that $F$ has no pole on $\lvert z\rvert=1$ is valid as given — on the circle the two arguments of $R$ are the real numbers $\sin\theta,\cos\theta$, which satisfy $s^2+c^2=1$, so the hypothesis applies there verbatim. Step 4's claim that a pole on the circle means the real integral diverges is correct for a rational $R$: near such a $\theta_0$ the integrand grows like $\lvert\theta-\theta_0\rvert^{-m}$ with $m\ge1$. The reciprocal-pair remark is correct, including the sharp dichotomy — $z_1z_2=1$ forces $\lvert z_1\rvert=1\iff\lvert z_2\rvert=1$, so either both roots are on the circle or exactly one is strictly inside.

Example 1: the reduction to $F=(z^2-1)^2/(2iz^2(z^2-4z+1))$ is right, including $z^2-2+z^{-2}=(z^2-1)^2/z^2$; $z_\pm=2\pm\sqrt3$ with $z_+z_-=1$, so $z_-=0.2679492$ inside and $z_+=3.7320508$ outside. Residue at the double pole: $N(0)=1$, $N'(0)=0$, $D(0)=1$, $D'(0)=-4$, so $(N/D)'(0)=4$ and the residue is $4/2i=-2i$. Residue at $z_-$: $z_-^2=7-4\sqrt3$, $(z_-^2-1)^2=84-48\sqrt3=12(7-4\sqrt3)$, $z_--z_+=-2\sqrt3$, and the $(7-4\sqrt3)$ cancels to leave $12/(-4i\sqrt3)=i\sqrt3$. Total $2\pi i(-2i+i\sqrt3)=2\pi(2-\sqrt3)$. Quadrature: $1.68357442895387$ against $2\pi(2-\sqrt3)=1.68357442895387$ — agreement to 20 digits, so the quoted $1.6835744$ is right.

Example 2: $F=-4iz/((z-z_+)^2(z-z_-)^2)$, with the $1/z$ from $d\theta$ genuinely cancelled, so no pole at the origin. The derivative simplifies to $-(z+z_+)/(z-z_+)^3$, giving $-4i\cdot(-4)/(-24\sqrt3)=-2i/(3\sqrt3)$ and $J=4\pi/(3\sqrt3)=4\sqrt3\pi/9$. Quadrature: $2.41839915231229$ against $4\sqrt3\pi/9=2.41839915231229$. The bracket is right ($(2-\cos\theta)^2$ lies in $[1,9]$, so $2\pi/9=0.69813<J<2\pi$), and the relation to Hassani's $\int_0^\pi d\theta/(a+\cos\theta)^2$ at $a=2$ is correct: $\int_0^{2\pi}(2-\cos\theta)^{-2}d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}(2+\cos\theta)^{-2}d\theta = 2\int_0^{\pi}(2+\cos\theta)^{-2}d\theta$, and the standard value $\pi a(a^2-1)^{-3/2}=2\pi/(3\sqrt3)$ doubles to our $J$.

Example 3: the two symmetries are correctly applied ($\sin^2\theta$ has period $\pi$ and is invariant under $\theta\mapsto\pi-\theta$, giving $\int_0^{2\pi}=4\int_0^{\pi/2}$); $4-\sin^2\theta=(z^4+14z^2+1)/(4z^2)$; $u=z^2$ gives $u=-7\pm4\sqrt3$ with product $1$, and since $(2\mp\sqrt3)^2=7\mp4\sqrt3$ the inside roots are $\pm i(2-\sqrt3)$ of modulus $0.2679492$ and the outside ones have modulus $2+\sqrt3$; the quotient rule gives $1/(i(z_j^2+7))$ with $z_j^2+7=4\sqrt3$, so each residue is $1/(4\sqrt3 i)$ and $K=\pi/(4\sqrt3)=\sqrt3\pi/12$. Quadrature: $0.453449841058554$ against $\sqrt3\pi/12=0.453449841058554$, and the full-period integral returns $1.81379936423422=4K$, matching the quoted $1.8137994$. The crude bracket $0.3927<K<0.5236$ is right.
