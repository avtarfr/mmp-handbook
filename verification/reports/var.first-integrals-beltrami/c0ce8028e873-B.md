## Findings
1. [minor] `statement`, item 2 (line 32): the identity is asserted "For any $C^2$ function $y$", but the block's preamble (line 29) assumes only that $F$ has continuous *first* partial derivatives. Under that hypothesis the term $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}$ on the right (and the matching term inside $\frac{d}{dx}(y'F_{y'})$ on the left) need not exist for an arbitrary $C^2$ curve. Concrete case: $F(x,y,p)=p|p|/2$ is $C^1$ on $[a,b]\times\mathbb{R}^2$ ($F_x=F_y=0$, $F_p=|p|$ continuous) but not $C^2$; take $y(x)=\frac12 x^2+x$, so $y'=x+1$ and shift to $F(p)=(p-1)|p-1|/2$, giving $F_{y'}=|x|$ along the curve, which is not differentiable at $x=0$ although $y'(0)=1\neq 0$. Neither side of the identity exists there. The identity is never *false* (whenever $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}$ exists, the algebra is correct), so the conclusion the unit actually uses is safe, but the quantifier as printed is wrong. Fix: say "for any $C^2$ function $y$ along which $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}$ exists (in particular for any extremal, by the Euler-Lagrange theorem)", or add $F \in C^2$ to item 2. The `proof` block already relies on exactly this, saying "the last derivative exists because $F_{y'}$ along an extremal is $C^1$".
2. [minor] `method`, step 1 (line 53): "If $F_{y'y'} \ne 0$ along the expected solution, the regularity part of the Euler-Lagrange theorem guarantees the $C^2$ smoothness needed" - that regularity part also requires $F$ to have continuous *second* partial derivatives, which the step does not mention. Fix: add "and $F$ is $C^2$".
