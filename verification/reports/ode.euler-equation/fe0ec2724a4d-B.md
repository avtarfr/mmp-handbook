## Findings
1. [blocking] remark "Where the hypotheses are used", last bullet (line 188): "if $k$ is a root of $q$ of multiplicity $m$, the trial $c\,x^{k}(\log x)^{m}$ works, with $c = m!/q^{(m)}(k)$". The constant is wrong by a factor $m!$; it should be $c = 1/q^{(m)}(k)$. The unit's own differentiated identity (Step 4 of the basis proof) gives $L\bigl(x^{k}(\log x)^{m}\bigr) = \sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}q^{(i)}(k)\,x^{k}(\log x)^{m-i}$, and with $q(k) = \cdots = q^{(m-1)}(k) = 0$ only $i = m$ survives, leaving $\binom{m}{m}q^{(m)}(k)\,x^{k} = q^{(m)}(k)x^{k}$ -- so $c\,q^{(m)}(k) = 1$. Verified symbolically on two cases: for the unit's own operator $L(y) = x^3y'''+xy'-y$ ($q = (r-1)^3$, $k=1$, $m=3$) sympy gives $L(x\log^3 x) = 6x$ while $q'''(1) = 6$, so $c = \tfrac16$, not $3!/6 = 1$; for $L(y) = x^2y''-xy'+y$ ($q = (r-1)^2$, $k=1$, $m=2$), $L(x\log^2 x) = 2x$ and $q''(1) = 2$, so $c = \tfrac12$, not $2!/2 = 1$. The formula happens to be right only for $m = 1$, which is the case Coddington's Ex. 6(b) (p. 150) asks for. Fix: $c = 1/q^{(m)}(k)$.
2. [minor] statement "The indicial polynomial" (line 57) and the remark's $x\ne0$ bullet (line 184): the remark says "The identity $x^{k}(x^{r})^{(k)} = r(r-1)\cdots x^{r}$ is fine at $x = 0$". For non-integer $r$ the right-hand side is not even defined at $x = 0$ (and $x^{r}$ is not $k$-times differentiable there), so the clause is misleading in the very sentence that then says so. Fix: drop the clause, or restrict it to integer $r \ge k$.
3. [minor] method, step 4(c) (line 195): the Wronskian $(r_2-r_1)|x|^{r_1+r_2-1}\operatorname{sgn} x$ is given without the hypothesis $r_1 \ne r_2$ that makes $\{|x|^{r_1},|x|^{r_2}\}$ a basis at all; for a double root the relevant pair is $|x|^{r_1}, |x|^{r_1}\log|x|$, whose Wronskian is $|x|^{2r_1-1}\operatorname{sgn} x$. Fix: add the double-root case, or say "for distinct roots".
4. [minor] definition (line 31): "the operator $L$ is then invariant under the scaling $x \mapsto cx$ for any $c > 0$". $L$ is invariant in the sense that $L$ commutes with the dilation, which is what is wanted; as written a reader may expect $L$ itself to be unchanged as a differential expression in the new variable (which it is), but "invariant" is doing two jobs. Fix: state it as "if $y(x)$ solves $L(y)=0$ then so does $y(cx)$".
