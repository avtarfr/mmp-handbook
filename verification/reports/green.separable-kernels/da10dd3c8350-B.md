## Findings
1. [minor] application, third paragraph "Where the alternative bites" (line ~236): the sentence
   "becomes, by the single-layer representation, a first-kind or second-kind boundary integral
   equation whose homogeneous version, and whose adjoint homogeneous version, both have the
   constants as solutions" conflates two different boundary formulations. For the single-layer
   representation of the interior Neumann problem the equation is $(\tfrac12 I+K^{*})\sigma=f$, and
   it is the *adjoint* homogeneous equation $(\tfrac12 I+K)v=0$ that has the constants; the
   homogeneous equation itself is solved by the equilibrium (natural-charge) density, which is
   constant only for special surfaces such as a sphere. The version in which both null spaces are
   the constants is the hypersingular first-kind equation $W\mu=f$, which comes from the
   *double*-layer representation ($W$ is self-adjoint with $\ker W$ the constants). Everything the
   paragraph goes on to use is right under either reading — $k=1$, the adjoint null space spanned
   by a nonzero constant $g$, the condition $\oint f\,dS=0$, the solution fixed only up to an added
   constant, and the well-made point that the condition is on the data and not on $g$ — so the
   conclusion is unaffected. Fix: drop "single-layer" and say "by a boundary-integral
   representation", or name the hypersingular equation, and claim the constants only for the
   adjoint homogeneous equation.
2. [source] rhb-2006, §23.4.1, p. 808: "if the separable kernel contains $n$ terms, as in (23.8),
   there will be $n$ such eigenvalues, although they may not all be different." A rank-$n$ separable
   kernel has at most $n$ characteristic values and may have none — $\det(\mathrm{I}-\lambda A)$ is a
   polynomial of degree at most $n$ with constant term $1$, and $A$ may be nilpotent or zero;
   Stakgold and Holst's own Example 1, p. 361, gives
   $\sin\pi x\sin2\pi\xi+\sin3\pi x\sin4\pi\xi$ on $[0,1]$, for which all four matrix entries
   vanish and there is no nonzero eigenvalue although $n=2$. The unit states the correct "at most
   $n$" and does not attribute the count to Riley, Hobson and Bence, so nothing in the unit is
   wrong. Recorded with `node tools/discrepancy.mjs add green.separable-kernels rhb-2006
   "§23.4.1, p. 808" ...`; not a reason to fail.
3. [minor] definition (line ~48): the Fourier expansion of $(2+\cos(x-t))^{-1}$ is displayed with no
   indication of where it comes from. It is correct (verified below), and the convergence argument
   that follows it is complete, but a reader cannot check the coefficient $1/\sqrt3$ without
   rederiving it. Fix: add half a line — the substitution $1+2r\cos\theta+r^{2}=2r(2+\cos\theta)$
   with $r=2-\sqrt3$ the root of $r^{2}-4r+1=0$ turns the left side into the Poisson kernel.

- Calculations (`.venv/bin/python`, sympy + numpy; scripts at
  <local scratch path> and
  .../sep2.py):
  - The Fourier claim in the definition. I rederived it: $1+2r\cos\theta+r^{2}=2r(2+\cos\theta)$
    requires $r^{2}-4r+1=0$, so $r=2-\sqrt3$, and the Poisson-kernel series gives
    $\frac{1}{2+\cos\theta}=\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}\sum_k(-r)^{|k|}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\theta}$ with
    $\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}=\frac{2-\sqrt3}{2\sqrt3-3}=\frac1{\sqrt3}$ — exactly the unit's $c_k$.
    Numerically (200 000-point trapezoid on $[0,2\pi]$) the coefficients come out
    $c_0=0.5773502692$, $c_1=-0.1547005384$, $c_2=0.0414518843$, $c_3=-0.0111069989$,
    $c_4=0.0029761114$, matching $(-1)^k(2-\sqrt3)^{|k|}/\sqrt3$ to 15 digits. Ratio
    $2-\sqrt3=0.267949$, so the unit's $0.2679$ and the absolute convergence claim are right, and
    $\mathcal{K}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kt}=2\pi c_k\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}$ with $c_k\neq0$ for every
    $k$ follows, giving infinite rank. (Sympy's symbolic evaluation of these integrals returns
    nonsense for $k\ge1$ — 2, $-8$, 30 — so the numerical check is the one to trust here.)
  - Example, step 2: $\phi_1\psi_1+\phi_2\psi_2=\tfrac12+\tfrac{3xt}{2}=K$; sympy gives
    $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, confirming all four entries ($A_{22}=\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac23=1$).
    The Legendre aside is right: $\delta(x-t)=\sum_n\frac{2n+1}{2}P_n(x)P_n(t)$ truncated after two
    terms is $\tfrac12+\tfrac32xt=K$.
  - Example, step 3: $\det(\mathrm{I}-\lambda\mathrm{I})=(1-\lambda)^2$, single root $\lambda=1$ with
    $\dim\ker=2$. Direct check reproduced: $\int_{-1}^{1}K(x,t)\,dt=1$ and
    $\int_{-1}^{1}K(x,t)\,t\,dt=x$ (sympy), so $\mathcal{K}$ fixes $1$ and $x$.
  - Example, first branch: $b=(\tfrac13,0)$, $c=(\tfrac23,0)$, $y=x^{2}+\tfrac13$; sympy gives
    $y-\bigl(f+\tfrac12\int_{-1}^{1}K(x,t)y(t)\,dt\bigr)=0$ exactly. The unit's hand check
    $\tfrac14(\tfrac23+\tfrac23)=\tfrac13$ is right.
  - Example, no-solution branch: $b_1=\tfrac13\neq0$ and $(x^{2},1)=\tfrac23\neq0$, both confirmed.
  - Example, infinitely-many branch: with $f=x^{2}-\tfrac13$, sympy gives
    $y-\bigl(f+\int_{-1}^{1}K(x,t)y(t)\,dt\bigr)=0$ identically in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ for
    $y=x^{2}-\tfrac13+\alpha+\beta x$. The two-parameter family is right, and $(f,1)=(f,x)=0$.
  - Proof, part 3: I redid the adjoint bookkeeping independently.
    $\overline{K(t,x)}=\sum_j\overline{\psi_j(x)}\,\overline{\phi_j(t)}$, so applying part 1 to it
    with $\tilde\phi_j=\overline{\psi_j}$, $\tilde\psi_j=\overline{\phi_j}$ gives
    $\tilde A_{ij}=\int\overline{\phi_i}\,\overline{\psi_j}=\overline{A_{ji}}=(A^{*})_{ij}$, and
    $(f,g)=\lambda\sum_j\overline{d_j}b_j=\lambda(b,d)$. Both displays in the unit are correct,
    including the factor $\lambda$ and the placement of the conjugates.
  - Method note on Hassani: $A_{11}=\int_a^b x^{2}dx=(b^{3}-a^{3})/3$ and the characteristic value
    $1/A_{11}=3/(b^{3}-a^{3})$; Hassani's Neumann condition $|\lambda|(b^{3}-a^{3})<3$ is
    $|\lambda|A_{11}<1$, so the unit's comparison is exact.
