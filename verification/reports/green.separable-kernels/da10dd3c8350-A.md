## Findings
1. [blocking] application, third bullet "Where the alternative bites" (line 237): "The potential problem $\nabla^{2}u=0$ in a region with prescribed normal derivative on the boundary becomes, by the single-layer representation, a first-kind or second-kind boundary integral equation whose homogeneous version, **and** whose adjoint homogeneous version, both have the constants as solutions." The second half of that is false. Represent $u$ by a single layer, $u(x)=\int_{\partial D}\sigma(y)\Phi(x,y)\,\mathrm dS_y$; taking the interior normal derivative gives the second-kind equation $(\pm\tfrac12 I+\mathcal K^{*})\sigma=f$, where $\mathcal K$ is the double-layer operator and $\mathcal K^{*}$ its adjoint (the normal derivative of a single layer carries the transposed kernel $\partial_{n(x)}\Phi(x,y)$). Its *adjoint* homogeneous equation $(\pm\tfrac12 I+\mathcal K)v=0$ is the one solved by the constants, because $\mathcal K\mathbf 1=\mp\tfrac12$. Its *own* homogeneous equation is solved by the equilibrium (capacity) density of $\partial D$, which is constant only for a ball or a disc; that density being non-constant is exactly the content of the classical equilibrium problem. Part 3 of this unit's own statement gives the two null spaces *equal dimension*, not equal elements, and the bullet has read the former as the latter. The same objection applies to the first-kind reading: the single-layer operator is self-adjoint, so there the two coincide, but its null space is $\{0\}$ in three dimensions and the equilibrium density (again not the constants) in the exceptional two-dimensional case. Nothing downstream is affected — the adjoint null space *is* spanned by a constant $g$, so $k=1$, the condition $(f,g)=0$ *is* $\oint f\,\mathrm dS=0$, and $u$ *is* determined up to an added constant, all as the bullet says. Fix: delete "whose homogeneous version, and", or write "whose adjoint homogeneous version has the constants as solutions (the homogeneous version itself has the equilibrium density, of the same dimension $k=1$ but not the same function)".
2. [minor] definition (lines 49–53): the Fourier expansion
   $\frac{1}{2+\cos(x-t)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}(2-\sqrt3)^{|k|}\mathrm e^{\mathrm ik(x-t)}$
   is displayed with no derivation and no citation, and it is the whole load of the counterexample. It is true — I checked the coefficients numerically to eight digits for $k=0,1,2,3,-2$, and it is the Poisson-kernel identity $\sum_k r^{|k|}\mathrm e^{\mathrm ik\theta}=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos\theta+r^{2}}$ at the root $r=-(2-\sqrt3)$ of $r^{2}+4r+1=0$ — but a reader cannot check it from what is written. Fix: name the Poisson kernel and the root, in one clause.
3. [minor] statement, part 2, closing parenthetical (line 79): "This null space is what Arfken, Weber and Harris isolate in their Exercise 21.2.16(b): a function orthogonal to every $\psi_{j}$ may be added to any solution without disturbing the equation." Arfken's Exercise 21.2.16 is about the equation of the **first** kind, $f(x)=\int_a^b K(x,t)\varphi(t)\,\mathrm dt$, and the claim is true only there. For the second-kind equation $(\ast)$ that this statement is about, adding $\psi\in\ker\mathcal K$ to a solution does *not* give another solution: $f+\lambda\mathcal K(y+\psi)=y\neq y+\psi$. As placed, the sentence invites the wrong reading. Fix: "…in their Exercise 21.2.16(b), which is about the first-kind equation $\mathcal Ky=f$: there a function orthogonal to every $\psi_j$ may be added to any solution."
4. [minor] application, second bullet (line 235): "the method above turns the integral equation into the $N\times N$ system $(\mathrm{I}-\lambda K)c=b$ — exactly what a Galerkin boundary-element code assembles". The matrix is right: writing the projected kernel as $\sum_i\chi_i(x)\bigl[\sum_jK_{ij}\overline{\chi_j(t)}\bigr]$ and using orthonormality gives $A_{il}=K_{il}$ (I checked this). But the method's own right-hand side is then $b_i=\sum_jK_{ij}(f,\chi_j)$ and its unknown is $c$, whereas a Galerkin code assembles $(\mathrm I-\lambda K)\hat y=\hat f$ for the coefficients $\hat y$ of the solution with $\hat f_i=(f,\chi_i)$. The two are the same system after the change of unknown, but "exactly what" overstates it. Fix: "the same $N\times N$ matrix a Galerkin boundary-element code assembles (its unknown is the coefficient vector of $y$ rather than $c$)".
5. [minor] application, first bullet (line 233): "A rank-$n$ separable potential also supports at most $n$ bound states in each partial wave" is true — the Birman–Schwinger count bounds the number of negative eigenvalues of $H_0+V$ by the rank of the negative part of $V$ — but the reason offered, "it is the size of that determinant … that limits how many energies can satisfy it", is not an argument: an $n\times n$ determinant depending on $E$ can vanish at more than $n$ energies. The bullet is careful to say the shape of the argument is what transfers, so this is presentation rather than error. Fix: either cite the rank bound as the reason or drop the "so it is the size of that determinant" clause.
