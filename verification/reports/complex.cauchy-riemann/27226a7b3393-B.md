## Findings

1. [minor] remark, fourth bullet ("Conjugacy is a relation, not a symmetry", line ~120): "the pair $(v,u)$ satisfies $v_x = u_y$, which is the wrong sign". As printed this asserts a false identity: from $u_y = -v_x$ the pair actually satisfies $v_x = -u_y$, and $v_x = u_y$ is what the Cauchy–Riemann equations for $(v,u)$ would *require*. The conclusion of the bullet ("$u$ is the conjugate of $-v$, not of $v$") is correct, and the concrete check with $z^2$ is correct, so this is a wording slip rather than a mathematical error — but the equation on the page is wrong. Fix: "the pair $(v,u)$ would have to satisfy $v_x = u_y$, whereas in fact $v_x = -u_y$."

2. [minor] frontmatter `rhb-2006` locator "§24.1–24.2, Eqs. (24.1)–(24.5), pp. 826–828" and the `definition` src "rhb-2006:§24.1, Eq. (24.1) and p. 826": Eq. (24.1), the definition of the complex derivative, is on p. 825 (§24.1 opens there), not in the quoted range. Eqs. (24.2)–(24.5) are on pp. 827–828 as claimed. Fix: `pages: "825-828"` and `loc: "§24.1–24.2, Eqs. (24.1)–(24.5), pp. 825–828"`.

3. [minor] statement part 3 last sentence: "$v$ is determined by $u$ up to an additive constant on a connected $\Omega$". The proof establishes this, but the statement does not say that it presupposes $\Omega$ connected in the earlier parts too (parts 1–2 are fine on any open set). Not wrong, just worth one clause so that the reader does not carry connectedness back into parts 1 and 2.

## Checked

- Counterexample search. Tested necessity and sufficiency separately. (a) The unit's own counterexample $f(z)=\bar z^{\,2}/z$, $f(0)=0$: in polar form $f = re^{-3i\theta}$, so $\lvert f\rvert=\lvert z\rvert$ and $f$ is continuous at $0$; I checked the two axis restrictions on *both* sides of the origin (for $x<0$, $f(x) = \lvert x\rvert e^{-3i\pi} = x$; for $y<0$, $f(iy) = \lvert y\rvert e^{3i\pi/2} = iy$), so $f(x)=x$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and $f(iy)=iy$ on all of $i\mathbb{R}$, giving $u_x(0)=1$, $v_x(0)=0$, $u_y(0)=0$, $v_y(0)=1$ and both Cauchy–Riemann equations at $0$; and $f(z)/z = e^{-4i\theta}$ takes the values $1$ and $-1$ along $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi/4$, so there is no derivative. The example is correct and does show that the equations at a single point do not suffice. (b) The off-origin claim: $\partial_r f = e^{-3i\theta}$ against $\partial_r z = e^{i\theta}$ gives quotient $e^{-4i\theta_0}$, and $\partial_\theta f = -3ire^{-3i\theta}$ against $\partial_\theta z = ire^{i\theta}$ gives $-3e^{-4i\theta_0}$; since $1 \ne -3$ the equations fail off the origin too, as the bullet says. (c) Tested the "test for non-analyticity" bullet: $\bar z$ ($u_x=1\ne-1=v_y$, nowhere), $\lvert z\rvert^2$ ($u_x=2x$, $v_y=0$, $u_y=2y$, $-v_x=0$, so only at the origin — and there the difference quotient is $\bar h\to0$, so it really is differentiable at $0$, which is what "except at the origin" says), $\operatorname{Re}z$ (nowhere). (d) Tested the combined form: $f_x = -if_y$ expands to exactly the pair $u_x=v_y$, $v_x=-u_y$, so the "equivalently" is an equivalence and not an implication. (e) Checked that part 3 needs the continuity of the second partials: the proof uses $v_{yx}=v_{xy}$, and says so.
