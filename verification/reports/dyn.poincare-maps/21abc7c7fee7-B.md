## Findings
1. [minor] :::definition (transversality paragraph): "all trajectories pierce $S$ in the same direction, the one in which $g$ increases." Transversality only gives $\nabla g\cdot f \ne 0$, so on each connected component of $S$ the crossing direction is constant but may be the one in which $g$ *decreases* (replace $g$ by $-g$ and nothing else in the unit changes); and $S$ as defined need not be connected, so even constancy across $S$ is not automatic. Fix: "cross in a single direction on each connected piece of $S$ -- the one in which $g$ has the sign of $\nabla g\cdot f$".
2. [minor] :::proof[Parts 2-4], the adapted-norm paragraph: "(For complex eigenvalues work over $\mathbb{C}$ and take the real part of the resulting norm; the bound is unchanged.)" A norm is already real, so "the real part of the resulting norm" does not parse. What is meant is to build the norm on $E\otimes\mathbb{C}$ and restrict it to the real subspace (or to use the real Jordan form with $2\times2$ rotation blocks). Fix: "restrict the resulting complex norm to $E$". (The automated check flags the 8-word run "and take the real part of the resulting" against evans-2010 p. 207, which is not a source of this unit; comparing the two, the coincidence is in a generic phrase and there is no provenance issue -- the sentence just needs rewording for correctness.)
3. [minor] :::example[A return map in closed form] is labelled `original`, and its content is ours -- the vector field $\dot r = -r\ln r$, the map $P(r) = r^{\kappa}$, the divergence cross-check and all the numerics are not in either source. But its skeleton is Strogatz's Example 8.7.1 on p. 278: polar coordinates with $\dot\theta = 1$, the positive $x$-axis as $S$, return time $2\pi$, a unique limit cycle at $r^* = 1$, and a cobweb to iterate $P$. I judge this to fall on the right side of the line -- $\dot\theta = 1$ with a radial section is essentially forced if one wants a return map in closed form, Strogatz's radial law $r(1-r^2)$ gives a completely different $P$ and a multiplier $e^{-4\pi}$ rather than our $e^{-2\pi}$, and the unit's second half (divergence check, numerics, Cartesian confirmation) has no counterpart there. Still, a one-clause acknowledgement ("Strogatz computes a return map the same way for $\dot r = r(1-r^2)$") would remove any doubt and costs nothing.
4. [minor] :::example[A forced oscillator], check (iii): "the two $e^{-2\pi}$-sized factors come from the mean growth rate $1$ and the mean of $-3x^2$ along the orbit". The arithmetic behind this is $\exp\bigl(\int_0^{2\pi}[(1+\cos t) - 3x^2]\,dt\bigr) = \exp(2\pi - 6\pi) = e^{-4\pi}$, i.e. a factor $e^{+2\pi}$ and a factor $e^{-6\pi}$, not "two $e^{-2\pi}$-sized factors". The underlying fact is exact and worth stating outright: integrating $\dot x/x = (1+\cos t) - x^2$ over a period gives $\int_0^{2\pi}x^2\,dt = 2\pi$, so the mean of $x^2$ along the periodic orbit is exactly $1$ -- which I confirmed numerically ($1.0000000$). Fix: say that, which explains the exactness of $e^{-4\pi}$ instead of gesturing at it.
