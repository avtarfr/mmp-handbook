## Findings
1. [blocking] remark, final bullet "The general hypothesis": "The counterexample above satisfies every hypothesis of the fixed-limit theorem except the boundedness of the $t$-interval, which is exactly the gap that domination fills." This is false, it contradicts the unit's own first remark bullet, and it misdiagnoses the counterexample. The counterexample's $t$-interval is $(0,\infty)$, which fails to be a compact $[a,b]$ in *two* ways — it is unbounded and it is open at $t = 0$ — and $f(x,t) = 2x^3/(x^2+t^2)^2$ cannot be extended continuously to $\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)$ at all, since $f$ has no limit at $(0,0)$. More importantly, the failure is caused by the open end, not by the infinite tail. The unit itself says two sentences earlier that "the mass of $\partial f/\partial x$ escapes to $t = 0$ as $x \to 0$", and indeed with $t = sx$ one gets $\partial f/\partial x = 2(3s^2-1)\big/\bigl(x^2(1+s^2)^3\bigr)$, concentrating at $t \sim x \to 0$. On the unbounded but closed strip $\mathbb{R}\times[1,\infty)$ the same $f$ satisfies the interchange: I computed $\int_1^\infty f(x,t)\,dt = \tfrac23 x^3 + O(x^5)$ numerically (ratio $F(x)/x^3 \to 0.66667$ at $x = 10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}$), so $F'(0) = 0 = \int_1^\infty \partial f/\partial x(0,t)\,dt$. So boundedness is not "exactly the gap". The first remark bullet's own heading — "the interval of integration must be bounded, and the continuity must hold on the closed strip" — is the accurate statement. Fix: say that the counterexample's strip is neither closed nor bounded, and that it is the failure at the open end $t = 0$ that breaks the interchange; domination fills the gap left by non-compactness of the $t$-interval in general, not by unboundedness alone.

2. [minor] note: "Continuity of $\partial f/\partial x$ on the closed strip is what makes the difference quotients in $x$ converge *uniformly* in $t$ — the hypothesis that lets a limit pass under a sign of integration at all". Uniform convergence is *a* sufficient condition, not the only one; the unit's own closing remark and the `:ref` to measure.dominated-convergence give the other. As phrased ("the hypothesis ... at all") the note overstates it and sits awkwardly beside the final bullet. Fix: "one standard hypothesis that lets a limit pass under a sign of integration".

3. [minor] method, step 2: "this step must instead produce a dominating function, so that dominated convergence applies on $I$" does not say what has to be dominated, which is the point of the step. The last remark bullet states it correctly ($\lvert\partial f/\partial x(x,t)\rvert \le g(t)$ for all $x$ in a neighbourhood, with $g$ integrable in $t$). Fix: state the bound on $\partial f/\partial x$ in step 2, and note that $f(x_0,\cdot)$ must itself be integrable for $F(x_0)$ to exist.

4. [minor] remark, bullet "A jump in the integrand across the diagonal": the integrand is written $\int_0^1 g(x,t)\,\rho(t)\,dt$, while Stakgold and Holst's integral (printed p. 64) is $\int_0^1 g(x,\xi) f(\xi)\,d\xi$ with the source called $f$ and the two pieces of $g$ named $r$ and $l$. The renaming is sensible here (the unit's own $f$ is the integrand), but the reader comparing with p. 64 has no signal. Fix: add "(their source function, written $f$ there)" or similar.
