## Findings
1. [blocking] example, Part A, Step 4 (line 184), and remark, third bullet (line 144): "The convergence is not uniform on $(0,\infty)$" is false — the convergence in Part A *is* uniform on $(0,\infty)$. With $f_n(x) = n\sin(x/n)/(x(1+x^2))$ and $f(x) = 1/(1+x^2)$,
   $$\lvert f_n(x) - f(x)\rvert = \frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\Bigl\lvert \frac{\sin(x/n)}{x/n} - 1 \Bigr\rvert \le \frac{\min\bigl(2,\ x^{2}/(6n^{2})\bigr)}{1+x^{2}} \le \frac{1}{6n^{2}},$$
   using $\lvert \sin u/u - 1\rvert \le u^2/6$ and $x^2/(1+x^2) \le 1$. I evaluated the exact supremum numerically: $0.10907$ at $n=1$, $0.0061021$ at $n=5$, $0.00040748$ at $n=20$, $1.6592\times10^{-5}$ at $n=100$ — within 10% of $1/(6n^2)$ and plainly tending to $0$. The accompanying justification is wrong too: "at $x = n$ the integrand is $\sin(1)/(1+n^{2})$ times $n$, while the limit there is $1/(1+n^{2})$, and their ratio is $n\sin 1 \ne 1$". The integrand at $x=n$ is $n\sin(n/n)/(n(1+n^2)) = \sin(1)/(1+n^{2})$ with no extra factor $n$, so the ratio is $\sin 1 = 0.8415$, and the *difference* there is $(1-\sin 1)/(1+n^2) \to 0$, which demonstrates nothing about uniformity. Both the claim and its evidence are our own material. Fix: delete the uniformity claim in both places and keep the point that actually holds — the interval is unbounded, so the uniform-convergence theorem (Stakgold and Holst's Thm 0.6.2, p. 45) does not apply however uniform the convergence is; or replace the example with one whose convergence really is non-uniform. The third remark bullet's sentence "Conversely, in the worked example below the convergence is not uniform on $(0,\infty)$ and the conclusion holds" must go with it, since it then states nothing.
2. [blocking] example, Part A, Step 2 (line 170): "For $u > 0$, $\sin u \le u$, so $0 < n\sin(x/n)/x \le 1$ for every $n \ge 1$ and $x > 0$". The strict lower bound is false: at $n=1$, $x=4$ the quantity is $\sin 4 / 4 = -0.1892 < 0$, and $\sin(x/n)$ is negative whenever $x/n \in (\pi, 2\pi) \bmod 2\pi$. Hypothesis (b) of the theorem requires $\lvert f_n \rvert \le g$, so the two-sided bound is what has to be proved, and $\sin u \le u$ alone does not give it. Fix: use $\lvert \sin u \rvert \le u$ for $u \ge 0$ (which is what makes $\lvert n\sin(x/n)/x \rvert \le 1$), and drop the claim of positivity.
3. [minor] proof "The series corollary and the parameter theorems", Differentiation, Domination bullet (line 133): the parenthetical "(separately to real and imaginary parts, each contributing its own intermediate point)" correctly flags that the mean value theorem does not hold for a complex-valued function of a real variable, but the inequality then asserted, $\lvert h_n(x)\rvert \le \sup_{t\in[a,b]}\lvert (\partial f/\partial t)(x,t)\rvert \le g(x)$, does not follow from it: two different intermediate points give only $\lvert h_n \rvert \le \lvert \operatorname{Re}h_n\rvert + \lvert \operatorname{Im}h_n\rvert \le 2g$ (or $\sqrt2\,g$). The conclusion is unaffected, since $2g \in L^1$. Folland p. 56 writes the same bound for complex $f$, so the gap is inherited; recorded with `tools/discrepancy.mjs` against folland-1999. Fix: either keep the parenthetical and write the bound as $2g$, or drop to real and imaginary parts first, as Step 0 of the first proof already does, and then the bound $g$ is correct as stated.
4. [minor] statement "Dominated convergence theorem" (lines 33–38) and proof, Step 0 (line 88): the conclusion "$f \in L^{1}(\mu)$" needs $f$ to be measurable, and on a measure space that is not complete this does not follow from the hypotheses, because $f$ is unconstrained on the exceptional null set. Step 0's "redefining all the $f_n$, $f$ and $g$ to be $0$ there changes no integral" already presupposes that $\int f$ is defined, which is the point at issue; what the argument actually produces is a measurable $\tilde f$ agreeing with $f$ a.e. Folland's Thm 2.24 has the same shape and repairs it in the proof by appeal to Props. 2.11 and 2.12 (the completion), so the statement is faithful to the source. Fix: say in Step 0 that the redefined $\tilde f$ is measurable and that $f$ itself is measurable when $\mu$ is complete, $f$ being an equivalence class otherwise.
5. [minor] remark, second bullet (line 143): "The smallest envelope is $\sup_n \lvert f_n\rvert(x) = 1/\lceil \max(\lvert x\rvert,1)\rceil$" is wrong at every non-zero integer. For $f_n = n^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{(-n,n)}$ the supremum is attained at the least $n$ with $n > \lvert x\rvert$, so $\sup_n \lvert f_n\rvert(x) = 1/(\lfloor \lvert x\rvert\rfloor + 1)$; at $x = 2$ that is $1/3$, while the printed formula gives $1/2$. The two agree off the integers and both fail to be integrable, so nothing downstream changes. Fix: write $1/(\lfloor\lvert x\rvert\rfloor+1)$.
6. [minor] example, Part B, Step 3 (line 209): "$F'/F = -t/2$ gives $F(t) = F(0)e^{-t^2/4}$" divides by $F(t)$, which has not been shown to be non-zero at this point in the argument (positivity of $F$ is a consequence of the formula, not an input). Fix: use the integrating factor — $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(F(t)e^{t^{2}/4}\bigr) = e^{t^2/4}\bigl(F'(t) + \tfrac t2 F(t)\bigr) = 0$, so $F(t)e^{t^2/4}$ is constant — which needs no division.
7. [minor] statement "Limits and derivatives in a parameter", part 2 (line 73): "$F$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$" should say that the derivative is one-sided at the two endpoints, since $t \mapsto f(x,t)$ is only defined on $[a,b]$ (the hypothesis "$\partial f/\partial t$ exists at every point of $X \times [a,b]$" has the same reading). Harmless, and the following paragraph's passage to an open $I$ removes it. Fix: "differentiable on $[a,b]$, one-sidedly at the endpoints".
