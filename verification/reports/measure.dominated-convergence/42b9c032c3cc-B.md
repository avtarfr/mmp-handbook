1. [blocking] example, Part A, step 4 (line 184): all four quadrature values are wrong at the precision printed. The integral has a closed form. Writing $a = 1/n$ and using $\frac{1}{x(1+x^2)} = \frac1x - \frac{x}{1+x^2}$ with $\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin ax}{x}dx = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\int_0^\infty \frac{x\sin ax}{1+x^2}dx = \frac{\pi}{2}e^{-a}$ gives
   $$\int_0^\infty \frac{n\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\,dx = \frac{n\pi}{2}\bigl(1 - e^{-1/n}\bigr),$$
   which I confirmed to 12 digits by oscillatory quadrature (mpmath `quadosc`, split at the zeros $x = k\pi n$). The correct values are $0.9929327$ ($n=1$), $1.4236853$ ($n=5$), $1.5551926$ ($n=50$), $1.5692266$ ($n=500$), against the unit's $0.9929404$, $1.4236880$, $1.5551929$, $1.5692265$; the first two are wrong in the sixth decimal. Fix: replace the four numbers, and consider quoting the closed form $\frac{n\pi}{2}(1-e^{-1/n})$, which makes the check exact and shows the limit $\pi/2$ directly.

The limit there is $1/(1+n^2) = 0.03846153846$, so the ratio is $\sin 1 = 0.8415$, a constant, not $n\sin 1$. Fix: "at $x = n$ the integrand is $\sin(1)/(1+n^{2})$ while the limit there is $1/(1+n^{2})$, a ratio of $\sin 1 \approx 0.841$ at every $n$" — but see finding 3, because that ratio does not show what the sentence wants it to show.

[blocking] example, Part A, step 4 (line 184), repeated in the remark, bullet "Uniform convergence is neither necessary nor sufficient" (line 144): "the convergence is not uniform on $(0,\infty)$" is false; the convergence in Part A *is* uniform on $(0,\infty)$. With $\operatorname{sinc}(u) = \sin u/u$ the error is $f_n(x) - f(x) = \bigl(\operatorname{sinc}(x/n) - 1\bigr)/(1+x^{2})$, and since $|\operatorname{sinc}(u) - 1| \le u^{2}/6$ always and $|\operatorname{sinc}(u)-1| \le 1.22$ always, splitting at $x = \sqrt n$ gives $\sup_{x>0}|f_n - f| \le \max\bigl(\tfrac{1}{6n}, \tfrac{1.22}{n}\bigr) \to 0$. Computed suprema: $0.1091$ ($n=1$), $0.006102$ ($n=5$), $6.607\times10^{-5}$ ($n=50$), $6.661\times10^{-7}$ ($n=500$). The constant ratio $\sin 1$ of finding 2 is itself the tell — a ratio bounded away from $1$ at a point where *both* functions are $O(n^{-2})$ says nothing about uniformity. Consequences: the clause "so a uniform-convergence theorem would not have served" is unsupported as argued (what actually rules that route out is that $(0,\infty)$ has infinite measure, not any failure of uniformity), and the remark's "Conversely, in the worked example below the convergence is not uniform on $(0,\infty)$ and the conclusion holds" is false, so the remark's claim that uniform convergence is not *necessary* has no witness in this unit. Fix: either replace Part A's sequence by one that really converges non-uniformly (for instance $f_n(x) = \frac{n x e^{-nx}}{1+x^2}$ on $(0,\infty)$, whose sup over $x$ is $\Theta(1)$ while $\int f_n \to 0$), or drop the uniformity discussion from step 4 and give the remark a different witness.

[minor] remark, bullet "An envelope that is not integrable is no envelope" (line 143): for $f_n = n^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{(-n,n)}$ the stated pointwise supremum $1/\lceil\max(|x|,1)\rceil$ is wrong at integer $|x| \ge 1$.
