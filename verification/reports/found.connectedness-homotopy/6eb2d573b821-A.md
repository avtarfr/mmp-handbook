1. [blocking] definition "Simply connected" (line 107): the claimed equivalence is false for one space, and the unit is the thing that makes it false. The block asserts "**Assume $X$ is path-connected.** Then each of the following is equivalent to $X$ being simply connected", followed by two bullets. Take $X = \emptyset$. The unit's own definition of path-connected (line 59, "some path in $X$ joins $p$ to $q$, whichever pair $p, q \in X$ is named") is satisfied vacuously, and the unit explicitly affirms this at line 118 ("$\emptyset$ is convex and is path-connected, vacuously, there being no pair of points to join"). Both bullets then hold vacuously as well: $\emptyset$ contains no loop, so "every loop in $X$ can be deformed ... to the stationary loop" is true, and it contains no paths, so "any two paths of $X$ that start together and finish together are path-homotopic" is true. But line 118 also asserts, correctly under the definition at line 105 (which demands a base point $q \in X$), that "$\emptyset$ is not simply connected". So the hypothesis of the equivalence holds, both bullets hold, and the conclusion fails. This is an internal contradiction in our own material, not a deficiency inherited from Lee: Lee's Exercise A.61 (p. 613) carries the same tacit gap, but Lee never asserts that $\emptyset$ is path-connected, whereas this unit does, and then relies on $\emptyset$ *not* being simply connected to justify the word "nonempty" in item 3. One of the two claims has to give.

Either (a) define path-connected to require $X \neq \emptyset$ (then line 118's argument must be rewritten, since the reason $\emptyset$ fails becomes the *first* half of the definition, not the second, which is the opposite of what item 3 now says), or (b) keep the vacuous reading of path-connected and strengthen the hypothesis of the equivalence to "Assume $X$ is nonempty and path-connected", and likewise qualify the base-point remark at line 101 ("Changing the base point inside a path-connected $X$ ... so one may speak of $\pi_1(X)$ there"), which also presupposes that a base point exists. 2. [minor] note (line 33): "The failure is measured by the fundamental group" overstates the relationship. For plane regions the two coincide, which is why the sentence reads plausibly. Fix: "A nontrivial fundamental group is what makes the failure possible; what measures it is homology", or simply "The obstruction is detected by the fundamental group".
