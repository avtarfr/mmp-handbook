The non-path-connectedness argument is valid as written - $\gamma^{-1}(A)$ is closed and does not contain $1$, so the largest $c$ with $\gamma(c) \in A$ exists; the first coordinate then takes every small positive value on $(c, c+\eta)$ by the intermediate value theorem, and on those values $\sin(1/x)$ attains both $1$ and $-1$, contradicting continuity at $c$. Verified numerically: for $\delta = 10^{-1}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-6}$ there are $x_\pm < \delta$ with $\sin(1/x_+) = +1.000000$ and $\sin(1/x_-) = -1.000000$ (e.g. $x_+ = 7.074\times10^{-2}$, $x_- = 5.787\times10^{-2}$ for $\delta = 0.1$). The path components are indeed $A$ and $B$, $A$ is closed in $X$ and $B$ is not ($\overline{B} \cap X = X$), and no neighbourhood of $(0,0)$ in $X$ is path-connected, so local path-connectedness is the hypothesis that fails. "No holes" item: $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{0\} \simeq S^2$ is simply connected while $\mathbb{R}^3$ minus a line $\simeq S^1 \times \mathbb{R}^2$ is not; the solid torus has $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$; the shell between two concentric spheres is homotopy equivalent to $S^2$, hence simply connected - all four claims correct. $S^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$ are both simply connected and not homeomorphic (one is compact), so the last item is right. Ahlfors's convention: the parallel strip has disconnected complement in $\mathbb{C}$ (two closed half-planes) and connected complement in $\mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}$ (they meet at $\infty$), and is simply connected - the unit's "essential" is justified, and it is Ahlfors's own example on p. 139.
