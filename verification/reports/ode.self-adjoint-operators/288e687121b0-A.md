## Findings
1. [blocking] method "Putting a second-order equation into self-adjoint form", step 4 (line 248): "For an eigenvalue problem $Ly = \lambda y$ with no weight in the original equation, take $\rho \equiv 1$, so that $w = h$" has the wrong sign, and the resulting weight is negative. The block's own template (line 228) is $a_2y''+a_1y'+a_0y+\lambda\rho y=0$, and $L$ in this unit is the operator $a_2D^2+a_1D+a_0$ of the first definition (line 40). So $Ly=\lambda y$ is the template with $\rho=-1$, and step 4's own formula $w=h\rho$ then gives $w=-h<0$ wherever $a_2>0$ — contradicting step 5 on the next line ("Check that $p>0$ and $w>0$"), contradicting the unit's own example (line 303), which writes the same kind of eigenvalue problem as $Lu=-\lambda u$ and gets $w=x=h>0$, and contradicting Hassani, whose corresponding equation (19.5), p. 569, is $L[u]+\lambda u=0$. The sentence reads as Arfken's statement (eq. (8.19), p. 386, where the eigenvalue equation is $L\psi=\lambda\psi$ and $w$ is the weight) imported without the sign flip that this unit's own remark (line 221) says Arfken's convention requires ("his $\lambda$ is $-\lambda$"); that is exactly the silent merge of two conventions the remark warns against. Fix: write the eigenvalue problem in the convention of the surrounding block, e.g. "For an eigenvalue problem $Ly+\lambda y=0$, that is $Ly=-\lambda y$, with no weight in the original equation, take $\rho\equiv1$, so that $w=h$"; alternatively keep $Ly=\lambda y$ and say that then $\rho=-1$ and the problem must be rewritten with $\lambda\mapsto-\lambda$ before step 5. While fixing it, define $L$ inside the method block (it occurs only in this sentence, and the sign depends on which operator it denotes).

2. [minor] definition "Symmetric boundary conditions and self-adjoint problems" (line 166): "a bounded jump in $q$ leaves his problem regular while ours is singular" is not what Strauss says. His standing hypotheses for the Sturm-Liouville equation (p. 314) are $p\in C^1$ and $q,m$ continuous, so an equation with a jump in $q$ falls outside his setup rather than being a regular problem inside it; his p. 316 list only says which problems are *called* singular. Fix: "Strauss's shorter list of three conditions does not mention a discontinuous $q$ — he assumes $q$ continuous throughout — whereas Hassani makes it one of his four."

3. [minor] method, step 1 (line 233): "If $a_2'=a_1$, the operator is already formally self-adjoint; skip to step 4." Step 4 is written in terms of $h$, which is defined only in step 2, so a reader who skips has no $h$. Fix: add that in this case $h\equiv1$ (the formula of step 2 gives $(1/a_2)\exp\int a_2'/a_2 = \text{const}$), hence $p=a_2$, $q=-a_0$, $w=\rho$.

4. [minor] definition "Symmetric boundary conditions and self-adjoint problems" (line 161): "**Separated** (**Robin**, **unmixed**)" offers "Robin" as a synonym for separated and then makes Dirichlet and Neumann special cases of it, whereas "Robin" is commonly reserved for the case in which neither $\alpha_i$ nor $\beta_i$ vanishes (Strauss uses it that way in §4.3 and as "Case 4" of the general separated condition in §5.3). Fix: "separated (Stakgold and Holst: *unmixed*) conditions, the general form of what Strauss calls the Robin case".

5. [source] method, step 2 and step 3 (lines 234–241), citing hassani-2013 Thm 14.5.4, p. 435: the theorem as printed asserts that every SOLDE "can be made self-adjoint" by $h=(1/p_2)\exp\int p_1/p_2$ with no hypothesis that $p_2$ be non-vanishing, although $h$ divides by $p_2$ and the resulting $p=hp_2$ degenerates where $p_2=0$. The unit is right to add "on an interval where $a_2$ does not vanish" (line 231); recorded with `tools/discrepancy.mjs`, not held against the unit.
