## Findings
1. [minor] statement[The Gaussian family sifts] (line 63): "let $f : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be bounded and continuous at $x = 0$". For the integrals $\int f\,\delta_\varepsilon$ that the statement asserts a limit for to exist at all, $f$ must also be measurable (or at least Riemann-integrable on compact intervals); boundedness plus continuity at one point does not give that, and Step 2 of the proof claims absolute convergence "because $f$ is bounded and $\delta_\varepsilon$ is integrable", which is the step that needs it. This is a one-word addition and nothing in the proof or the conclusion changes. Fix: "let $f : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be measurable, bounded, and continuous at $x = 0$", and in Step 2 say "because $f$ is measurable and bounded and $\delta_\varepsilon \in L^1$".
2. [minor] definition, first bullet (lines 37–45): a delta family is defined to consist of "integrable functions with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta_\varepsilon = 1$ for every $\varepsilon$", and then $D_T(x) = \sin Tx/\pi x$ is listed as one of "the standard three" — but $D_T$ is not absolutely integrable on $\mathbb{R}$, as the remark's second bullet itself points out ("not integrable in absolute value on $\mathbb{R}$"). On the Lebesgue reading of "integrable" the definition and its own third example contradict each other; the normalization $\int D_T = 1$ holds only as an improper integral. Fix: either say "integrable (for $D_T$, the normalization is an improper integral and $D_T \notin L^1$; see the remark)", or move $D_T$ out of the list of delta families and present it separately as the family behind rule 7.
3. [minor] definition, first bullet (line 37): "becoming taller and narrower as $\varepsilon \to 0$" is the only concentration requirement in the definition, and it is not a checkable condition — nothing in the definition as given makes a delta family sift, which is the property every subsequent statement relies on. Fix: one clause saying the definition is heuristic and that the two properties actually used (unit mass, mass escaping from every fixed neighbourhood of $0$) are what the proof below establishes for the Gaussian family; that is also what makes the remark's second bullet land.
4. [minor] `prereqs` (line 7) and proof[The Laplacian of $1/r$], Step 1 (line 147): the step cites `:ref[the radial Laplacian]{to=vcalc.divergence-curl}` for $\nabla^2F = F'' + (2/r)F'$, and the third example uses the same formula again, but `vcalc.divergence-curl` is not listed in `prereqs`. It precedes this unit in `content/map.yaml`, so adding it is free. Fix: add `vcalc.divergence-curl` to `prereqs`.
5. [minor] proof[The Laplacian of $1/r$], Step 3 (line 159): "On $r = R$ both $f$ and $\nabla f$ vanish, because $f \equiv 0$ on a neighbourhood of that sphere". $f$ is assumed only to vanish *outside* the ball $r < R$, i.e. on a one-sided neighbourhood; the conclusion is nonetheless right, because $f \in C^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$ and vanishes identically on $r \ge R$, so its first derivatives vanish on $r = R$ too. As written the reason given is stronger than the hypothesis supplied. Fix: either "because $f$ vanishes on $r \ge R$ and is $C^1$, so $f$ and $\nabla f$ vanish on $r = R$", or choose $R$ so that $f$ is supported in $r < R/2$, which makes the quoted reason true as stated.
