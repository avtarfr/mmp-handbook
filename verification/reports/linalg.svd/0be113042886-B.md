## Checked
- Counterexample search (statements as written, over 2000-5000 random cases each, dimensions 1-5,
  real and complex, with rank-deficient matrices forced in a large fraction of trials):
  - Definition: shapes, the $S_{ij}=0$ for $i\ne j$ condition, the ordering and $p = \min(m,n)$ are
    consistent with every later use. "For real $A$ one may take $U$ and $V$ real orthogonal" is
    correct.
  - Part 1: for 2000 random $m\times n$ matrices (including deliberately rank-deficient ones),
    $s_i^2$ equalled the $p$ largest eigenvalues of **both** $A^\dagger A$ and $AA^\dagger$ in
    decreasing order, and the remaining eigenvalues of the larger of the two were zero
    (max deviation $< 10^{-8}$). The degenerate shapes $m=1$, $n=1$ and $A = 0$ all behave as the
    statement says. The claim that "the columns of $U$ so obtained are an orthonormal eigenbasis of
    $AA^\dagger$" is right including the completion columns, since those span $\ker A^\dagger$ and are
    therefore eigenvectors for eigenvalue $0$ — no gap there.
  - Part 2: $Av_i = s_iu_i$ and $A^\dagger u_i = s_iv_i$ held for all $i \le p$, and $Av_i = 0$ for
    $p < i \le n$, $A^\dagger u_i = 0$ for $p < i \le m$ (1000 random cases, $<10^{-9}$). I checked
    the non-obvious half: for $i > p$ the corresponding eigenvalue of $A^\dagger A$ must vanish
    because $r \le p$, so $\|Av_i\|^2 = v_i^\dagger A^\dagger Av_i = 0$. Correct, and the statement is
    careful to range the two "$i > p$" clauses over whichever of $m, n$ is the larger.
  - Part 3: $r = \operatorname{rank}A$ matched `matrix_rank` in every case, and the dyadic sum
    $\sum_{i\le r}s_iu_iv_i^\dagger$ reproduced $A$ to $<10^{-9}$. The four basis claims (range of
    $A$, $\ker A$, range of $A^\dagger$) are correct and correctly indexed.
  - Part 4 (uniqueness): the "same $Q$ on both sides" constraint is right — see finding 2 for the
    numerical check. The zero-block clause is right too: the trailing columns contribute nothing to
    $\sum_{i\le r}s_iu_iv_i^\dagger$, so unrelated unitaries of sizes $m-r$ and $n-r$ are admissible,
    and those sizes genuinely differ when $m\ne n$. Edge cases: $r = 0$ ($A = 0$, both completions
    entirely free — covered by the zero clause), and all singular values distinct (every $W$ a
    singleton, so the freedom is the common phase — exactly what the statement says).
  - Part 5: correct. $|A| = (A^\dagger A)^{1/2}$ and "no simple relation to the eigenvalues of $A$" are
    both borne out by the trap remark's own example.
  - "Spectral norm and best low-rank approximation": $\|A\|_2 = s_1$ and
    $\|A\|_F = (\sum_i s_i^2)^{1/2}$ held exactly. For a random $5\times4$ matrix the truncation
    errors matched the predicted values to $10^{-10}$ (see Calculations), and a 20000-sample random
    search over rank-$k$ competitors never beat $A_k$ in either norm — consistent with optimality.
    The side conditions are all correct: $A_k$ has rank exactly $k$ for $k < r$; the Frobenius
    minimiser is unique exactly when $s_k > s_{k+1}$; the spectral minimiser need not be unique; for
    $k \ge r$ the minimum is $0$. The attributions to Eckart-Young (1936), Schmidt (1907) and Mirsky
    (1960) are each correct for what they are credited with (Mirsky is the unitarily-invariant-norm
    extension, which is indeed where the spectral case comes from).
  - "Pseudoinverse": over 1000 random cases (many rank-deficient) $x^{+} = A^{+}b$ minimised
    $\|Ax-b\|_2$ — no perturbation out of 500 per case did better — and adding any null-space vector
    left the residual unchanged while strictly increasing $\|x\|_2$, confirming "the unique one of
    smallest norm". $AA^{+}$ and $A^{+}A$ came out Hermitian and idempotent in every case, so both
    projection claims hold; the square-invertible and full-column-rank special cases check out.
  - Remark "Singular values are not eigenvalues": the necessity argument via Schur's inequality is
    sound and I tested its exact form: over 5000 random complex square matrices,
    $\sum_i s_i^2 = \|A\|_F^2 \ge \sum_k|\lambda_k|^2$ always, and equality held **iff** $A$ was
    normal (no exception at tolerance $10^{-7}$). The "coincide with no absolute values exactly for
    positive semidefinite $A$" claim is also right: coincidence forces the eigenvalues real and
    non-negative and forces equality in Schur, hence normality, hence positive semidefiniteness.
  - Remark "Full versus reduced": this is the one place where a careless statement would be easy, and
    the unit gets it exactly right. Verified directly: for $(m,n) = (5,3)$ the thin $U$ is $5\times3$
    with $U^\dagger U = I_3$ but $UU^\dagger \ne I_5$, while the thin $V$ is square and fully unitary;
    for $(3,5)$ the roles are exchanged; for $(4,4)$ both are unitary. That is precisely the
    "thin $U$ square exactly when $m \le n$ / thin $V$ square exactly when $n \le m$" dichotomy, and
    it confirms "at most one of the two can fail".
  - Remark "The pseudoinverse is not continuous": verified — $\operatorname{pinv}
    (\operatorname{diag}(1,10^{-12})) = \operatorname{diag}(1, 10^{12})$ while
    $\operatorname{pinv}(\operatorname{diag}(1,0)) = \operatorname{diag}(1,0)$.
  - Summary vs statements: the summary agrees with what the unit proves. (It says the singular values
    "are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^*A$", which over-counts by the zero eigenvalues
    when $m < n$; the statement's precise "the $i$-th largest eigenvalue" formulation is what the unit
    relies on, and the summary does not overstate anything.)

  - $\operatorname{diag}(i,1)$: singular values $(1,1)$, eigenvalues $i$ and $1$, both of modulus $1$

  - $A_\epsilon = \operatorname{diag}(1,\epsilon)$: $A_\epsilon^{+} = \operatorname{diag}(1,1/\epsilon)$,
    $A_0^{+} = \operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ — matches.
  - Truncation errors for a random $5\times4$ real $A$ with singular values
    $s = (s_1, 3.18883073, 2.02122352, 0.77486456)$:
    $k=1$: $\|A-A_1\|_2 = 3.18883073 = s_2$, $\|A-A_1\|_F = 3.85414077 = (\sum_{i>1}s_i^2)^{1/2}$;

    $k=3$: $\|A-A_3\|_2 = \|A-A_3\|_F = 0.77486456 = s_4$. All three agree with the stated formulas to
    $10^{-10}$, and the two norms coincide at $k = r-1$ as they must.
