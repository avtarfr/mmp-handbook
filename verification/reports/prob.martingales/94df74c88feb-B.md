[source] remark "Where the hypotheses are used: the doubling strategy" (lines 161-166): the unit follows Grimmett and Stirzaker's Example (12.1.3), p. 534, but silently fixes an index shift in the book. The book states the rule "if he loses the first $n$ plays, then he bets $2^n$ on the $(n+1)$th" and then gives the profit on winning at the $T$th bet as $2^T - (1+2+4+\cdots+2^{T-1})$; under the stated rule the stake on the $T$th bet is $2^{T-1}$, so the profit is $2^{T-1}-(1+\cdots+2^{T-2})$, which is what the unit writes. Both expressions equal 1, and the book's own mean-loss series on the same page, $\sum_n 2^{-n}(1+2+\cdots+2^{n-2})$, follows the rule rather than the printed profit formula, so nothing downstream is affected. The unit's convention is self-consistent and gives the book's values $\mathbb{P}(T=n)=2^{-n}$, $\mathbb{E}(T)=2$, $Y_T = 1$ and an infinite mean loss before the win. Recorded with `node tools/discrepancy.mjs add prob.martingales grimmett-stirzaker-2020 "Sec. 12.1, Example (12.1.3), p. 534" ...`. Not a reason to fail.
2. [minor] statement "Stopping a martingale", part A (line 57): the unit calls "$\mathbb{E}(Y_T)=\mathbb{E}(Y_0)$ for a bounded stopping time" the optional sampling theorem. Grimmett and Stirzaker's (12.4.11a), p. 552, is the stronger conditional statement $\mathbb{E}(Y_T\mid\mathcal{F}_0) \ge Y_0$ for a submartingale, hence $\mathbb{E}(Y_T\mid\mathcal{F}_0)=Y_0$ for a martingale. Nothing the unit says is false, and the sharper sampled-sequence form (12.4.11b) is given, but the name is attached to a weaker consequence than the theorem it names. Fix: state the conditional form and note that taking expectations gives the displayed identity.
3. [minor] statement "Stopping a martingale", part A (line 57): the sampled-sequence form is stated as "$(Y_{T_j}, \mathcal{F}_{T_j})$ again form a martingale"; the source's (12.4.11b) prepends $(Z_0, H_0) = (Y_0, \mathcal{F}_0)$ to the sampled sequence. Immaterial to the content, but the unit's version omits the initial term the book includes.
4. [minor] application (line 253): "Assume $q > 0$ wherever $p > 0$" is weaker and better than the source's "assume for neatness $q(x) > 0$ for all $x$" (Example (12.8.7), p. 568), and the unit's restricted integral $\int_{\{q>0\}}$ handles it correctly.
