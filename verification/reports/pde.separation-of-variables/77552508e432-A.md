## Findings

1. [minor] statement[The separation lemma] (line ~61): the lemma is stated for $F:A\to\mathbb{R}$, $G:B\to\mathbb{R}$, but it is applied twice to groups that the unit itself says may be complex-valued — method step 2, where Hassani's time constant $\alpha$ is "purely imaginary for the Schrödinger equation", and statement part 1, where $m^2$ and $\lambda$ are declared to be "complex numbers, subject to no further restriction" (if the three factors were real-valued, $\Phi''/\Phi=-m^2$ would force $m^2$ real). The two-step proof is codomain-agnostic and works verbatim, so nothing is actually wrong; the hypotheses are just narrower than the uses. Fix: state the lemma with $F:A\to\mathbb{C}$, $G:B\to\mathbb{C}$ and "$\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$" (no change to the proof).

2. [minor] proof, Step 6 (line ~257): "The function $Q_\ell^m$ diverges there, so a solution finite on the polar axis has $F=0$" does not follow from the divergence of $Q_\ell^m$ alone — if $P_\ell^m$ also diverges at an endpoint (which is the case for every non-integer $\ell$, and for every $m\neq0$), a combination $EP_\ell^m+FQ_\ell^m$ could in principle cancel the singularity. The clause that rules this out, integrality of $\ell$, is only supplied in the *next* half-sentence. The conclusion of the step is correct (the solution space of (6) finite at both $\mu=\pm1$ is nontrivial exactly when $\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}$, $|m|\le\ell$, and is then spanned by $P_\ell^m$), and the order of argument is Riley, Hobson and Bence's own (p. 733). Fix: reverse the two clauses — first "a nontrivial solution finite at $\mu=\pm1$ exists only for $\ell$ a non-negative integer with $0\le|m|\le\ell$ (:ref spec.associated-legendre)", then "and for those parameters $P_\ell^m$ is finite while $Q_\ell^m$ diverges, so $F=0$."

3. [minor] note[Catalogue of separated equations], spherical entry (line ~127): "finite polynomial solutions requiring $\ell$ a non-negative integer and $0\le|m|\le\ell$". $P_\ell^m$ is not a polynomial for odd $m$ (it carries $(1-\mu^2)^{m/2}$). The phrase "finite polynomial solutions" is the source's, but there it is said of *Legendre's* equation (rhb-2006, p. 733, $m=0$), not of the associated equation. Fix: "solutions finite at $\mu=\pm1$ requiring ...".

4. [minor] note[Catalogue], cylindrical entry (lines ~116–122): the azimuthal factor is given only as $C\cos m\varphi+D\sin m\varphi$ and the axial factor only as $Ee^{\alpha z}+Fe^{-\alpha z}$, so the degenerate cases $m=0$ (where $\Phi=C\varphi+D$) and $\alpha=0$ (where $Z=E+Fz$) are silently dropped, although the plane-polar entry does record its $n=0$ case and the spherical statement records both of its degeneracies explicitly. Fix: add the two parenthetical exceptions, as the plane-polar entry does.

5. [minor] application{provenance=original}, "Numerical methods" (line ~430): "the discrete Laplacian is diagonalized by the discrete analogues of the separated eigenfunctions, so a Poisson solve becomes a forward transform, a division by the eigenvalues $l^2+m^2+n^2$, and an inverse transform". The eigenvalues of the discrete Laplacian are not $l^2+m^2+n^2$; for the standard 7-point stencil on a uniform grid of spacing $h$ they are $\tfrac{4}{h^2}\bigl[\sin^2\tfrac{l\pi h}{2}+\sin^2\tfrac{m\pi h}{2}+\sin^2\tfrac{n\pi h}{2}\bigr]$, which only approach $l^2+m^2+n^2$ as $h\to0$ at fixed mode number. Fix: either say "the discrete eigenvalues, which are the discretization of $l^2+m^2+n^2$", or give the sine formula.
