## Findings

1. [blocking] definition "Small oscillations" (line 112): the order count justifying the
   disappearance of the $-\partial T/\partial q_i$ term is wrong, and inconsistent with the rest of
   the same sentence. With $T = \tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}M(q)\dot q$ we have
   $\partial T/\partial q_i = \tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}(\partial_i M(q))\dot q$, in which
   $\partial_i M$ is $O(1)$ at the equilibrium, so the term is of **second** order in
   $(q,\dot q)$, not third. (Counterexample to "third order": a particle in plane polar
   coordinates, $T = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2)$ about an equilibrium at $r = r_0 > 0$
   with $q_1 = r - r_0$, $q_2 = \theta$, gives $\partial T/\partial q_1 = m(r_0+q_1)\dot q_2^2
   \approx m r_0 \dot q_2^2$, which is second order and non-zero. Third order is the special case
   $\partial_i M(0) = 0$, which is what happens in the unit's own example because $M$ depends on
   $q$ only through $\cos(q_1-q_2)$.) The sentence is also self-contradictory: the terms that are
   kept, $M\ddot q$ and $Kq$, are of first order, so "one order beyond everything else kept" is
   second order, not third.
   Fix: say that $\partial T/\partial q_i$ carries two factors of $\dot q$ and so is of second
   order, one order beyond the first-order terms $M\ddot q$ and $Kq$ that are retained, hence
   dropped in the linearization; or, if the third-order phrasing is wanted, attach it to the
   neglected *term of $T$*, $\tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}(M(q)-M(0))\dot q = O(\|q\|\,\|\dot q\|^2)$,
   rather than to its $q$-derivative. The conclusion ("only $M\ddot q$ survives") is correct either
   way.

2. [minor] idea, before the pencil proof (line 54): notation clash. The sentence sets $y = B^{1/2}x$
   and then two clauses later writes "the $B$-inner product $y^\dagger Bx$", where $x$ and $y$ are
   two unrelated generic vectors; substituting the earlier definition into the later expression is
   meaningless. Fix: write the inner product with other letters, e.g. "the $B$-inner product
   $(u,v)_B = v^\dagger Bu$ becomes the ordinary one".

3. [minor] application, second paragraph (line 298): "a shape wrong by ten percent gives a frequency
   wrong by about one" and "quote the result as an upper bound on the fundamental frequency". The
   stationarity statement is quadratic in the error of the **quotient**, i.e. of
   $\lambda = \omega^2$; the frequency error is half of that, so a ten percent shape error gives
   about one percent in $\lambda_1$ and about half a percent in $\omega_1$ — the unit's own example
   (step 7) makes exactly this distinction (5.2% in $\lambda_1$, 2.5% in $\omega_1$). Likewise the
   quotient is an upper bound for $\omega_1^2$, and its square root for $\omega_1$. Fix: state the
   bound and the error estimate for $\lambda_1 = \omega_1^2$, then take the square root.

4. [minor] application, first paragraph (line 296): "a linear triatomic — the case of the remark
   above, CO$_2$ for instance — has $3\cdot 3 - 5 = 4$ vibrational frequencies". The remark's
   example (rhb-2006 §9.1, p. 321) is the one-dimensional three-coordinate chain, which has three
   modes, one of them $\omega^2 = 0$, and so exhibits two vibrations, not four; the count $3N-5$
   belongs to the full $3N$-coordinate model. Fix: say the remark's example is the axial subproblem
   of the full $3N$-coordinate calculation, or drop the cross-reference.

5. [minor] definition "Small oscillations" (line 99): the block's `src` locator is

6. [minor] proof of "Normal frequencies and normal modes", Part 2 and Part 3 (lines 169, 171): two
