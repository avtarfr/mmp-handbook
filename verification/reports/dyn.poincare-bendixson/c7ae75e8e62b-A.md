## Findings
1. [blocking] method, step 7 (line 185), and example[A trapping region for an autocatalytic reaction], step 3 (line 228): deleting an open **disc** $B$ around a repelling fixed point does not leave a positively invariant set, and the property offered as justification is the wrong one. "Every trajectory starting on the circle of radius $\rho$ leaves that disc in forward time" says nothing about trajectories *entering* $B$; positive invariance of $R\setminus B$ needs $f\cdot n > 0$ at every point of $\partial B$. For a Euclidean circle that is the condition that the symmetric part of the Jacobian be positive definite, which a repelling spiral need not satisfy — and in this very example it fails at every radius. At $(1,3+\rho)$ on the circle of radius $\rho$ about the fixed point $(1,3)$ the Brusselator field is $\dot x = 1 - 4 + (3+\rho) = \rho$, $\dot y = 3 - (3+\rho) = -\rho$, so with displacement $d = (0,\rho)$ one gets $f\cdot d = -\rho^2 < 0$: the field points strictly *into* the disc, for every $\rho > 0$. (Consistently, $\tfrac12(J+J^{\mathsf T}) = \begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $-3 < 0$.) The trajectory through $(1,3+\rho)$ therefore sits in $Q^\ast = Q\setminus B_\rho$ and enters $B_\rho$ immediately, so $Q^\ast$ is not positively invariant and hypothesis (4) of the theorem is not established; as written the worked example does not prove that a closed orbit exists. Fix: puncture with a negatively invariant *ellipse* instead. Since the eigenvalues of $J$ have positive real part, there is a positive definite $P$ with $J^{\mathsf T}P + PJ$ positive definite (solve the Lyapunov equation; for $J = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\-3&-1\end{pmatrix}$ any solution of $J^{\mathsf T}P+PJ = I$ will do), and for small $c$ the set $B = \{(x-p)^{\mathsf T}P(x-p) \le c\}$ has $f\cdot n > 0$ on its boundary, so $Q\setminus \operatorname{int}B$ is compact, positively invariant and fixed-point free. Step 7 of the method should be restated the same way: "a neighbourhood $B$ of the repeller on whose boundary the field points strictly outward, which exists in the norm adapted to the linearization, though in general not for a Euclidean disc".
2. [minor] proof[Three lemmas], Lemma 2 (line 117): "a short arc leaving $Y_1$ along the flow reaches the $Y_1$ side without crossing $\Sigma$" is hand-waved, and as written the arc follows the flow, which leaves $S$ altogether rather than reaching another part of it. The conclusion is right: in local coordinates with $S$ the $x$-axis and the flow crossing upward, the four rays at $Y_1$ in cyclic order are $S^{+}$, the outgoing flow direction, $T$, and the incoming trajectory direction, and $\Sigma$ is the corner formed by the last two, so $D$ is the sector between them and $S^{+}$ lies in the complement, while the same picture at $Y_0$ (using that $D$ lies just below $T$) puts $S^{-}$ inside $D$. Fix: give that two-sentence local argument instead of the arc.
3. [minor] proof[Three lemmas], Lemma 2 (line 117): $\Sigma = \mathcal A \cup T$ is a simple closed curve only if the trajectory arc from $Y_0$ to $Y_1$ does not meet $S$ in between, that is only if $Y_0$ and $Y_1$ are *successive* crossings. The lemma is stated for any three distinct crossings in time order. Fix: prove it for successive crossings and note that the general case follows by induction along the (monotone) list of successive crossings. Lemma 3 uses only successive-crossing triples in any case.
4. [minor] application (line 281): "A bounded trajectory of a planar system must converge to a fixed point, to a closed orbit, or to a set containing fixed points joined by homoclinic or heteroclinic connections" is the generalised Poincare-Bendixson theorem, which needs the equilibria in the limit set to be finite in number; otherwise the limit set can be a continuum of fixed points. Fix: add "when the system has finitely many equilibria".
5. [minor] definition (line 36): "such a segment exists because $f$ is continuous and $f(X_0)$ is perpendicular to the line" has the geometry the wrong way round — the line is defined to be perpendicular to $f(X_0)$, and what continuity supplies is that $f\cdot f(X_0) > 0$, hence transversality, on a short enough segment. Fix: say that.
