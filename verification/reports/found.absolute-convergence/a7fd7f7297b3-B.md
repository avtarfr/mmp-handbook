## Checked

- Counterexample search.
  - *Absolute convergence implies convergence.* True over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ (completeness);
    the stated converse failure is witnessed by the alternating harmonic series, which the unit itself
    uses. No boundary case: the hypothesis is on a non-negative series, so no conditional convergence
    can hide in it.
  - *Bound on the sum, equality clause.* The clause is the sharp one. If $S \neq 0$, put
    $\lambda = \bar S/\lvert S\rvert$; then $\sum \operatorname{Re}(\lambda a_n) = \sum \lvert a_n\rvert$
    forces $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda a_n) = \lvert \lambda a_n\rvert$, i.e. $\lambda a_n = \lvert a_n\rvert$,
    termwise. The degenerate case is handled too: if $S = 0$ and equality holds then every $a_n = 0$ and
    $\lambda = 1$ serves, which is why the "all non-zero terms have the same argument" gloss (vacuously
    true there) is equivalent and not merely sufficient. Tested on 20000 random complex tuples of
    length 1-5: equality of $\lvert \sum a_n\rvert$ and $\sum\lvert a_n\rvert$ to $10^{-12}$ agreed
    with the common-argument condition in every case, 0 mismatches. Spot cases: common argument
    $e^{0.7i}$, $\lvert S\rvert = 2.882819754163589 = \sum\lvert a_n\rvert$; all-zero, $0 = 0$;
    $\{1,-1,2\}$, $2 < 4$. The real-terms gloss ("all $\ge 0$ or all $\le 0$") follows from
    $\lambda = \pm 1$ and is right.
  - *Rearrangement under absolute convergence.* No counterexample; absolute convergence of the
    rearrangement is also correctly asserted, since $\sum \lvert a_{\sigma(n)}\rvert$ has the same
    supremum of partial sums.
  - *Riemann's theorem.* The hypothesis as written (real, convergent, not absolutely convergent) is
    exactly what the three conclusions need: it forces $a_n \to 0$ with both $\sum a_n^{+}$ and
    $\sum a_n^{-}$ divergent, so parts 1, 2 and 3 all hold. Part 3's "no limit in
    $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$" correctly covers both of RHB's "oscillate finitely or infinitely".
    I attacked the restriction to real terms rather than the statement: over $\mathbb{C}$ part 1 is
    false and the unit says so (see below), so the restriction is load-bearing and present.
  - *Cauchy product.* Absolute convergence of both factors gives
    $\sum_n \lvert c_n\rvert \le \sum_n \sum_k \lvert a_k\rvert\lvert b_{n-k}\rvert = (\sum\lvert a\rvert)(\sum\lvert b\rvert)$,
    so the asserted absolute convergence and the "any other order" extension both hold. Degenerate
    cases (one factor a finite sum; a factor with $a_0 = 1$ and the rest zero) give $c_n = b_n$ and are
    consistent with the statement, and also confirm the remark's claim that under Mertens' hypotheses
    the product need not converge absolutely.
  - *Index conventions.* The unit is $n \ge 0$ throughout; RHB is $n \ge 1$. Reindexing checked:
    $c_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k b_{n-k}$ equals RHB's $w_{n+1} = u_1 v_{n+1} + \dots + u_{n+1} v_1$ with
    $a_k = u_{k+1}$. No symbol is left undefined; $\mathbb{N}_0$ is introduced where it is first used.
  - *Summary vs statements.* The frontmatter summary restricts Riemann to real terms and claims nothing
    the statements do not contain. No overstatement.

- Calculations (`.venv/bin/python`, mpmath at 25-30 dps).
  - $\int_0^1 dt/\sqrt{t(1-t)}$: computed $3.14159265358979321850$ against
    $\pi = 3.14159265358979323846$, agreeing to 18 digits (the quadrature is split at $1/2$ because of
    the endpoint singularities). So the remark's value $\pi$ is right.
  - $\lvert c_n\rvert = \sum_{k=0}^{n} 1/\sqrt{(k+1)(n-k+1)}$ for $a_n = (-1)^n/\sqrt{n+1}$: computed
    $1.0$ ($n=0$), $1.41421356237$ ($n=1$), $1.65470053838$ ($n=2$), $2.29341822327$ ($n=10$),
    $2.85219738443$ ($n=100$), $3.04931739256$ ($n=1000$), $3.11238827586$ ($n=10^4$),
    $3.13235664650$ ($n=10^5$). Increasing, bounded by $\pi$, with deficit $\approx 2.9/\sqrt{n}$
    ($0.00924$ at $n=10^5$ against $2.9/316 = 0.0092$), consistent with the Riemann-sum limit $\pi$.
    Also confirms the remark's actual conclusion: $\lvert c_n\rvert \ge 1$ for all $n$, so $c_n \not\to 0$
    and the product series diverges. The substitution behind the Riemann sum is exact:
    $t_k = (k+1)/(n+2)$ gives $(k+1)(n-k+1) = (n+2)^2 t_k(1-t_k)$ and spacing $1/(n+2)$.
  - Sign of $c_n$: $\sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k a_{n-k} = (-1)^n \sum_k 1/\sqrt{(k+1)(n-k+1)}$, matching the
    displayed formula.
  - Complex counterexample $a_n = i(-1)^n/(n+1)$: summed to $0 + 0.69314718056\,i = i\ln 2$, and
    $\sum 1/(n+1)$ is harmonic, so the series converges and not absolutely, as claimed. Every term is
    purely imaginary, so the attainable sums lie in $i\mathbb{R}$; the unit's careful "no real
    $L \neq 0$ is attainable" is exactly right, since $L = 0$ *is* attainable (Levy-Steinitz gives the
    whole line $i\mathbb{R}$ here).
  - Arfken's rearrangement rule, reproduced from the unit's description (positive terms until the
    partial sum reaches $3/2$, then negative terms until it falls below, repeating), grouped partial
    sums: $1.5333, 1.0333, 1.5218, 1.2718, 1.5144, 1.3477, 1.5103, 1.3853, 1.5079, 1.4079$ against
    Arfken's printed $1.5333, 1.0333, 1.5218, 1.2718, 1.5143, 1.3476, 1.5103, 1.3853, 1.5078, 1.4078$
    (p. 15) - agreement to the last printed digit up to rounding. So the unit's paraphrase of the
    reordering is faithful, and $\ln 2 = 0.69315 \neq 3/2$ as the remark contrasts.
  - Levy-Steinitz bullet, checked by hand rather than numerically: the sum set is
    $s + \Gamma^{\perp}$ with $\Gamma = \{f : \sum \lvert f(a_n)\rvert < \infty\}$, so a single point
    means $\Gamma$ spans $(\mathbb{R}^2)^*$, which gives $\sum\lvert\operatorname{Re}a_n\rvert < \infty$
    and $\sum\lvert\operatorname{Im}a_n\rvert < \infty$, hence absolute convergence. The bullet's
    conclusions are therefore correct: a line or all of $\mathbb{C}$ in the non-absolute case, and over
    $\mathbb{R}$ the only proper subspace is $\{0\}$, so the theorem collapses to Riemann's. The
    stated justification ("unconditional convergence forces absolute convergence in finite
    dimensions") is a true fact but a shortcut for this implication; not worth a finding.
  - Terminology bullet: in $\mathbb{R}$, "convergence depends on the arrangement" and "not absolutely
    convergent" do pick out the same class, since unconditional and absolute convergence coincide in
    finite dimensions. Correct as written.
