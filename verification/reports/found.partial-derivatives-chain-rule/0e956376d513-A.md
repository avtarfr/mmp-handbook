## Findings
1. [blocking] definition[Partial derivative] (the paragraph beginning "In the subscript notation the opposite order is customary") and remark (bullet "Order of differentiation in the symbols"): the claim that the subscript notation runs opposite to the $\partial$ notation, i.e. that $f_{x_k x_j} = \partial^2 f/\partial x_j\,\partial x_k$, is stated as a flat fact about "the" subscript notation and is contradicted by one of the unit's own cited sources on exactly the page cited for notation. Riley, Hobson and Bence p. 152 define $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} = f_{xy}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x} = f_{yx}$; that is, their $f_{xy}$ differentiates with respect to $y$ first, the *same* direction as the $\partial$ notation, so for them $f_{xy} = \partial^2 f/\partial x\,\partial y$, the opposite of what this unit asserts. The unit's convention (left-to-right, $f_{xy}=(f_x)_y$) is indeed the one used in many calculus texts, so this is a genuine split between books, not a single correct answer; but as written the unit asserts one side as universal while citing a book that uses the other, and the remark bullet claiming "the two common notations run in opposite directions" is therefore wrong. Fix: state that the subscript convention is not uniform, give both ($f_{xy}=(f_x)_y=\partial^2 f/\partial y\,\partial x$ in Stewart-style calculus texts; $f_{xy}=\partial^2 f/\partial x\,\partial y$ in Riley, Hobson and Bence, §5.1, p. 152), say which convention this handbook follows, and move the "which books use which" information into the conventions remark as the phase file requires. The mixed-partial statement and the order-of-differentiation counterexample are unaffected, since there $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$ or only the $\partial$ notation is used.
