## Findings

1. [blocking] remark[Where the hypotheses are used], third bullet (line 241): "it excludes **exactly** the processes with a spectral atom, such as a deterministic sinusoid of random phase". The biconditional is false: absolute summability of $c$ also fails for a large and, for this unit's subject, central family of processes whose spectral measure is purely absolutely continuous and has no atom at all — the long-memory / power-law processes. Explicit counterexample, checked numerically: take the density
   $$f(\lambda) = \log 2 - \log\bigl\lvert 2\sin(\lambda/2)\bigr\rvert ,\qquad \lambda\in(-\pi,\pi] .$$
   It is non-negative (its minimum is $f(\pi)=0$, confirmed numerically over $2\times10^{5}$ points) and integrable, with total mass $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f = 4.3551722 = 2\pi\log 2$, so after normalisation it is a bona fide spectral density: the spectral measure is absolutely continuous and carries **no** atom. Its autocovariances, computed by quadrature, are $c(n) = \pi/\lvert n\rvert$ for $n\neq 0$ — I obtained $c(1)=3.14159265$, $c(2)=1.57079633$, $c(3)=1.04719755$, $c(5)=0.62831853$, $c(10)=0.31415927$, matching $\pi/n$ to eight digits — so $\sum_n\lvert c(n)\rvert$ diverges harmonically. The hypothesis therefore fails with no atom present, and a reader who believes the bullet will conclude that $1/f$ and other long-memory spectra satisfy (9.3.18), which is precisely the case where periodogram bias is worst. The claim is in an `original` block, so it is ours. Fix: replace "excludes exactly the processes with a spectral atom" with something like "excludes in particular every process with a spectral atom ... (it also excludes processes with a perfectly good but slowly decaying autocovariance, such as long-memory spectra with a $\lvert\lambda\rvert^{-\alpha}$ singularity at the origin)". Everything else in the bullet, including the linear-in-$N$ growth at a matching Fourier frequency, is correct.

2. [minor] statement[What the periodogram is, and what it is not], part 2 (line 107): "the full width of the main lobe of $\Phi_N$ between its first zeros is exactly $4\pi/N$, and **every** taper widens it". The $4\pi/N$ and the $8\pi/N$ for Hann are both right, and the claim is true — and provable — for tapers in the ordinary sense of the word, i.e. $w$ symmetric, non-negative and non-increasing away from the centre: such a $w$ is a superposition of centred rectangular windows of lengths $L\le N$ (layer-cake), each of whose Dirichlet kernels is strictly positive on $\lvert\theta\rvert<2\pi/L\le 2\pi/N$, so the superposition cannot vanish before $2\pi/N$. (Numerical spot check: over 300 random symmetric unimodal non-negative tapers at $N=25$ the smallest first null was $1.262$ bins, against $1.000$ for the rectangle.) But the unit's own definition one block earlier asks only for "weights $w_0,\dots,w_{N-1}$, not all zero", and under that definition there are counterexamples — the super-resolving windows. Checked: the Dolph–Chebyshev window of $N=33$ with $8$ dB sidelobes is strictly positive ($\min w = 0.0771$, $\max w = 1$) and its spectral window has its first zero at $0.729$ bins, i.e. a main lobe of full width $2.92\pi/N<4\pi/N$ (values of $\Phi^w/\Phi^w(0)$: $0.800$ at $0.2$ bins, $0.0560$ at $0.6$, $8.8\times10^{-8}$ at $0.729$, back up to $0.118$ at $1.0$); the three-point weight $(1,\tfrac12,1)$ does the same at $N=3$, with its zero at $0.871$ bins instead of $1$. Both are "anti-tapers", heavier at the ends than in the middle, which is why the sentence is defensible in spirit. Fix: either restrict the definition of a taper to symmetric non-increasing non-negative weights, or write "every taper that lowers the sidelobes widens it" / "every taper that is heaviest at the centre widens it".

3. [minor] example, Part B, last sentence (line 279): "gives $96.14\%$ in the two nearest bins and $99.93\%$ in the nearest four ... while the peak is spread a little wider". The two statements sit badly together, because the numbers just computed say the opposite for this record: the Hann taper concentrates the power more tightly than the rectangle at every width ($96.14\%$ vs $82.08\%$ in two bins, $99.93\%$ vs $92.18\%$ in four; I reproduced all four figures). The wider main lobe is real but shows up for a line *on* the grid, not for this half-bin-offset one, where the rectangle's main lobe straddles two bins and the Hann's covers them. Fix: say that the main lobe of the window is twice as wide, which costs resolution when two lines are close, rather than that "the peak is spread a little wider" in this example.

4. [minor] statement, part 1 heading (line 88): "it carries all of the sample **variance**", while the displayed sum rule gives the mean square, $\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_jI_N(\lambda_j) = \frac1N\sum_tx_t^2$. These agree only after the mean is removed (equivalently, only if the $j=0$ term is dropped), which is exactly the case part 1's first identity is restricted to and the case the example checks. Fix: "carries all of the mean square of the record" in the heading, or add "of the mean-removed record".
