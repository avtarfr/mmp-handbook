2. [blocking] :::remark, first bullet (line 307): "the dispersion relation (3) then has complex roots - the damping. Reading the correspondence the other way: ... an imaginary [mass term] makes them decay." This is wrong twice over, and both halves are our own gloss, not Strauss's. (i) Putting $\mu = i/2$ in (3) gives $\omega^2 = c^2k^2 - \tfrac14$, which is real: the roots are real for $ck > \tfrac12$ and purely imaginary for $ck < \tfrac12$, never genuinely complex. (ii) Where they are imaginary the two branches are $e^{+\lambda t}$ and $e^{-\lambda t}$ with $\lambda = \sqrt{\tfrac14 - c^2k^2}$, so an imaginary mass produces a *growing* long-wave mode as much as a decaying one — the tachyonic instability — not decay. The damping of the telegraph equation is the prefactor $e^{-t/2}$ of the substitution, which is uniform in $k$ and acts on the short waves too, where $\omega$ is real and $v$ does not decay at all; equivalently, the telegraph equation's own dispersion relation $\Omega = -\tfrac i2 \pm\sqrt{c^2k^2-\tfrac14}$ is the one with a constant imaginary part. Fix: say that an imaginary mass removes the frequency gap and replaces oscillation by exponential growth and decay for $ck < \lvert\mu\rvert$, and that the telegraph equation's damping is the restored factor $e^{-t/2}$, so that $\lvert\mu\rvert = \tfrac12$ is exactly the rate that keeps $u = e^{-t/2}v$ decaying for every $k$.
