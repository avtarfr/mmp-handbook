## Findings
1. [blocking] application (line 259): "To compute $I = \int_D f$, draw points uniformly in a region containing $D$ and average $f$ over them; the strong law says the average converges to $I$ times the volume". The limit is $I$ *divided* by the volume of the sampling region, not multiplied by it. With $R \supseteq D$ the sampling region and $g = f\mathbf{1}_D$, the strong law gives $n^{-1}\sum_i g(P_i) \to \mathbb{E}g = |R|^{-1}\int_R f\mathbf{1}_D = I/|R|$; equivalently it is $|R|$ times the average that converges to $I$. As written the recipe is wrong by a factor of $|R|^{2}$. Fix: "the average converges almost surely to $I$ divided by the volume of the sampling region, so the volume times the average converges to $I$".
2. [blocking] example, title (line 214) and step 3 (line 239): the example is headed "How many Monte Carlo samples does a three-decimal estimate of $\pi$ need?" and step 3 sets "$\epsilon = 0.01$ (three decimal places, i.e. an error below one part in three hundred)". An absolute error of $0.01$ on $\pi = 3.14159$ fixes two decimal places, not three (three decimal places means $\epsilon \approx 5\times10^{-4}$); the parenthetical's second clause, one part in three hundred, is right, so what is wrong is the words "three decimal places". The headline answer the example delivers, $2.70$ million samples, is therefore the answer to a different question from the one the title asks, and the figure for the promised accuracy is $100$ times larger ($2.70\times10^{8}$, which appears only as an aside in step 5). Fix: either retitle to "three significant figures"/"two decimal places" and reword step 3, or run the whole example at $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$.
3. [minor] proof, "What the sharp theorem costs" (line 180): the sketch is Grimmett and Stirzaker's proof of Theorem (7.5.1), pp. 374-376, which begins by assuming the $X_i$ non-negative and lifts that assumption only at the end, exactly as step 4 of this unit's own proof does. Both ingredients quoted presuppose it: $\sum_n \mathbb{P}(X_n \ge n) \le \mathbb{E}(X_1)$ is the non-negative tail-sum identity (for a general $X_1$ one needs $\mathbb{E}|X_1|$), and the conclusion $\alpha^{-1}\mu \le \liminf_m m^{-1}S'_m \le \limsup_m m^{-1}S'_m \le \alpha\mu$ requires $\mu \ge 0$. Fix: open the paragraph with "after the same reduction to non-negative summands as in step 4".
4. [minor] example, step 4 (line 247): $4\sigma z/\epsilon = 4\times0.410546\times2.57583/0.01 = 422.96$, whose square is $1.78897\times10^{5}$; the unit rounds to $423.0$ first and prints $(423.0)^2 = 1.7893\times10^{5}$, which is off in the fourth figure. The stated conclusions ("about 179 thousand", "a factor of $15.1$") are unaffected. Fix: print $1.7890\times10^{5}$, or keep $422.96$ in the display.
5. [minor] application (line 265): the random-walk recurrence result attributed to Grimmett and Stirzaker is their Example (5.10.16) and Theorem (5.10.17), pp. 220-221, which are not covered by the unit's `loc` or `pages` for that work (it lists §5.10 only at p. 217). The attribution is accurate -- the book does introduce it with "an application of the law of large numbers to the recurrence of random walks", uses $\mathbb{P}(|S_n| \le n\epsilon) \to 1$ and concludes that the mean number of visits $V_0$ diverges -- but the locator is missing. Fix: extend the `loc`/`pages` to pp. 220-221.
