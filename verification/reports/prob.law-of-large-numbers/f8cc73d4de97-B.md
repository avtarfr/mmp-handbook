An absolute error of $0.01$ in $\pi = 3.14159\dots$ is **two** decimal places, not three; and the two halves of the gloss contradict each other, since three decimal places would mean $\epsilon < 5\times10^{-4}$, i.e. about one part in six thousand, not one part in three hundred. Every computed number in the example is right for $\epsilon = 0.01$ (I reproduced all of them), so the arithmetic is sound and only the description of what it buys is wrong -- but the headline answer "2.70 million samples" is then attached to the wrong accuracy claim, and a reader who wants three decimals needs the $2.70\times10^8$ of step 5, a hundred times more. Fix: retitle as a two-decimal (or three-significant-figure) estimate and change the parenthesis to "two decimal places, i.e. an error below one part in three hundred"; step 5 already supplies the three-decimal figure and can be pointed at explicitly.
2. [blocking] :::application, "Monte Carlo integration" (line 259): "To compute $I = \int_D f$, draw points uniformly in a region containing $D$ and average $f$ over them; the strong law says the average converges to $I$ times the volume". This is inverted. If the enclosing region has volume $V$, the sample average of $f\mathbf{1}_D$ converges almost surely to $\mathbb{E}[f(U)\mathbf{1}_D(U)] = V^{-1}\int_D f = I/V$, so the limit is $I$ **divided** by the volume; equivalently, it is the average *times* the volume that converges to $I$. As written the claim is off by a factor $V^2$. Fix: "the average, multiplied by the volume of that region, converges to $I$ almost surely".
