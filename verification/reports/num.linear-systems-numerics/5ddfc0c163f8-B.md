## Findings
1. [blocking] :::example, Part 3 (conjugate gradients): "From $x^0 = 0$, so $r^0 = p^0 = b$ and $\|x - x^0\|_A = 1.5811$." This is wrong. With $x^0 = 0$, $\|x - x^0\|_A^2 = x^{\mathsf T}Ax = x^{\mathsf T}b = 1\cdot2 + 2\cdot4 + 3\cdot10 = 40$, so $\|x-x^0\|_A = \sqrt{40} = 6.3245553$. The value $1.5811 = \sqrt{2.5}$ is $\|x-x^1\|_A$, which the table's own last column already reports at $i=1$; the text has copied the wrong row. Fix: $\|x-x^0\|_A = \sqrt{40} = 6.3246$.
2. [blocking] :::example, Part 3, the closing paragraph. Because of finding 1 the whole paragraph is wrong, and it ends by asserting something false about the theorem. With the correct $\|x-x^0\|_A = 6.3246$, the part-4 bound at $i=2$ is $2(0.182676)^2(6.3246) = 0.42211$, and the achieved $\|x-x^2\|_A = 0.30576$ is comfortably **below** it. There is no near-violation, nothing to explain away, and the passage's resolution -- "The Chebyshev bound is an upper bound over a continuum and can be beaten or missed on a finite spectrum of three points at a single step" -- claims the bound may be *missed*, i.e. may fail, which is not true: (10.6.9) bounds the minimax over $[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]$, and the spectrum is contained in that interval, so the bound holds at every step for every spectrum. The paragraph also rhetorically asks "The bound is therefore violated?" and answers with a non-explanation. Fix: delete the paragraph and replace it with the correct one-line check -- achieved ratio $0.30576/6.3246 = 0.04834$ against the allowed $2\rho^2 = 0.0667$, the bound holding with room to spare because three eigenvalues do not fill the interval.
3. [blocking] :::example, Part 2: "Gauss-Seidel reaches $10^{-6}$ in $8$ sweeps where Jacobi needs $16$." I ran both iterations from $x^0 = 0$: the Jacobi max-norm error first drops below $10^{-6}$ at sweep **14** ($9.5367\times10^{-7}$; at sweep 13 it is $3.8147\times10^{-6}$), not 16. Gauss-Seidel at sweep 8 is $8.3447\times10^{-7}$, so that half is right. Fix: "where Jacobi needs $14$" (and the comparison is still the intended one -- roughly twice as many sweeps, as $\rho(C_{GS}) = \rho(C_J)^2$ predicts, but the parity of the Jacobi max-norm, which alternates between factors $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac12$, keeps it from being exactly double).
4. [minor] :::statement[Gaussian elimination] (i): "When it exists the factorization is unique." The proof's uniqueness argument assumes both $U_i$ nonsingular, and for singular $A$ an LU factorization need not be unique (e.g. $A = 0$ admits $L$ arbitrary unit lower triangular with $U = 0$). Fix: add "for nonsingular $A$".
5. [minor] :::application, least squares: "Fitting a degree-$12$ polynomial in the monomial basis on $[0,1]$ gives a Hilbert-like matrix with $\kappa \sim 10^{16}$." A degree-12 fit has 13 coefficients, and $\kappa_2$ of the $13\times13$ Hilbert matrix is $1.55\times10^{18}$ (the $12\times12$ is $1.81\times10^{16}$, the $11\times11$ is $5.23\times10^{14}$). The conclusion drawn -- that every digit is gone in double precision -- is right either way, and "Hilbert-like" plus "$\sim$" is doing some work, but the quoted exponent is two off for the stated degree. Fix: say $\kappa \sim 10^{18}$, or make it a degree-11 fit.
6. [minor] :::example, Part 3 table: $\|x-x^3\|_A$ is given as $8.9\times10^{-16}$; in my run the step-3 iterate was exact to the last bit and I got $0$. This is an arithmetic-order artefact, not an error, but a reader reproducing it will not match the digit.
