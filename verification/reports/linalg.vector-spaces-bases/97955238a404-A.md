Re-derived every step of the proof. Part 1: verified the induction is on *index* sets $J_k \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$ with $|J_k| = n-k$, which is what makes the bookkeeping sound even if some $w_i$ happens to coincide with some $v_j$; checked that "not every $b_j$ can vanish" is a valid dichotomy for any chosen expansion (if they all vanish, $w_{k+1} \in \operatorname{span}\{w_1,\dots,w_k\}$ with coefficient $1$ at $w_{k+1}$, which independence forbids); checked that $\operatorname{span} T \supseteq$ the old spanning set, hence $\operatorname{span} T = V$; and checked the $k = n$ step, including the degenerate $n = 0$ case, where $\emptyset$ spans, $V = \{0\}$ and the relation $1 \cdot w_1 = 0$ is the required dependency. Part 2: the collection of spanning subsets of a finite $S$ is finite and nonempty, a dependency lets one member be deleted while the span is preserved, and the $V = \{0\}$ case with $\operatorname{span}\varnothing = \{0\}$ is handled explicitly. Part 3: "no basis has more than $n$ elements" does give finiteness of every basis, and the symmetric argument gives equality; both $\dim\{0\} = 0$ directions are checked. Part 4: existence needs padding with zero coefficients, uniqueness needs the entries of an ordered basis to be distinct so that the tuple is independent, and the proof says both. Part 5: $v \notin A$ for $v \notin \operatorname{span} A$, the two-case coefficient argument for $A \cup \{v\}$, termination after at most $n - |A|$ steps, the existence of a largest independent subset of $W$, and the $\dim W = n \Rightarrow W = V$ step via part 3. Part 6: the collection of independent sets is nonempty (so the empty chain is covered), a chain's union is independent because a relation involves finitely many vectors all lying in the largest of finitely many chain members, and maximality plus the part-5 computation (which uses no finiteness) gives spanning.

Verified the two examples symbolically with sympy. For $B = (1+x,\, x+x^2,\, x^2+x^3,\, x^3)$ the coefficient system $a = p_0$, $a+b = p_1$, $b+c = p_2$, $c+d = p_3$ and its solution $a = p_0$, $b = p_1 - p_0$, $c = p_0 - p_1 + p_2$, $d = -p_0 + p_1 - p_2 + p_3$ are exactly what sympy returns; for $p = 2 - 3x + x^2 + 5x^3$ the components are $(2, -5, 6, -1)$ and the expansion back reproduces $p$ exactly; $q_4 - q_3 - q_5 = 0$ is exact and $q_1, \dots, q_4$ are independent. In the second example, $a(1+i) + b(1-i) = (a+b) + (a-b)i$ vanishes only for $a = b = 0$ over $\mathbb{R}$; $a = (x+y)/2$, $b = (x-y)/2$ spans; $4(1+i) - (1-i) = 3+5i$ and $-i(1+i) = 1-i$ both check.
