- Counterexample search: tested the classification against its own boundaries. Re-derived the induced equation from scratch: $x = 1/t$, $dy/dx = -t^2v'$, $d^2y/dx^2 = t^4v''+2t^3v'$, so $t^4v''+[2t^3-t^2P(1/t)]v'+Q(1/t)v = 0$ -- as printed. Then $t = 0$ is a regular singular point iff $t\cdot[2/t-P(1/t)/t^2]$ and $t^2\cdot Q(1/t)/t^4$ are analytic at $0$, i.e. iff $P(1/t)/t$ and $Q(1/t)/t^2$ are analytic, i.e. iff $P = \sum_{k\ge1}a_k/x^k$ and $Q = \sum_{k\ge2}b_k/x^k$ -- exactly the unit's criterion. Computed the induced indicial polynomial independently: $s_0 = 2-a_1$ (from $P(1/t)/t = a_1+a_2t+\cdots$) and $t_0 = b_2$, giving $I_\infty(\alpha) = \alpha(\alpha-1)+(2-a_1)\alpha+b_2$ -- as printed; and $v = t^{\alpha}(1+\cdots)$ gives $y = x^{-\alpha}(1+\cdots)$, so the sign convention the unit warns about is right. Tested the Riemann identity as an independent constraint: from the exponent form of the Riemann equation I computed $a_1 = 2-\lambda_1-\lambda_2-\mu_1-\mu_2$ and $b_2 = \nu_1\nu_2$, so $I_\infty(\alpha) = \alpha^2+(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu_1+\mu_2-1)\alpha+\nu_1\nu_2$, and this equals $(\alpha-\nu_1)(\alpha-\nu_2)$ precisely when the six exponents sum to $1$. So the displayed equation and the identity are mutually consistent, not independent assertions. Checked the hypergeometric exponent triple against the identity: $0+(1-\gamma)+0+(\gamma-\alpha-\beta)+\alpha+\beta = 1$.
- Calculations (by hand, re-derived independently):
  - Example 1, Chebyshev $(1-x^2)y''-xy'+\nu^2y = 0$: $P = -x/(1-x^2)$, $Q = \nu^2/(1-x^2)$, singular only at $\pm1$. At $x=1$: $s = (x-1)P = x/(1+x)$ (the two sign changes in $(x-1)/(1-x^2) = -1/(1+x)$ cancel against the $-x$), $s_0 = \tfrac12$; $t = (x-1)^2Q = -\nu^2(x-1)/(1+x)$, $t_0 = 0$; indicial $\sigma(\sigma-\tfrac12)$, exponents $(0,\tfrac12)$. At $x=-1$: $s = -x/(1-x)$ so $s_0 = \tfrac12$ and $t_0 = 0$ again, exponents $(0,\tfrac12)$. At infinity: $P = x/(x^2-1) = 1/x+1/x^3+\cdots$ so $a_1 = 1$; $Q = -\nu^2/(x^2-1) = -\nu^2/x^2-\nu^2/x^4-\cdots$ so $b_2 = -\nu^2$; $I_\infty(\alpha) = \alpha(\alpha-1)+\alpha-\nu^2 = \alpha^2-\nu^2$, exponents $\pm\nu$. Riemann check: $0+\tfrac12+0+\tfrac12+\nu-\nu = 1$ for every $\nu$. All as printed.
  - Step 7's check: $T_2 = 2x^2-1$ gives $(1-x^2)\cdot4-x\cdot4x+4(2x^2-1) = 4-4x^2-4x^2+8x^2-4 = 0$ -- the printed expansion is right term for term, and $T_2 \sim 2x^2$ matches the exponent $\alpha = -2$ under $y\sim x^{-\alpha}$.
  - Example 2.1, Hermite $y''-2xy'+2\nu y=0$: $P = -2x$, $Q = 2\nu$, both entire, no finite singular point. $2/t-P(1/t)/t^2 = 2/t+2/t^3$, whose product with $t$ is $2+2/t^2$, not analytic at $0$ -- so infinity is irregular, as printed. (The $Q$ side gives the same verdict: $t^2Q(1/t)/t^4 = 2\nu/t^2$.)
  - Example 2.2, Bessel $x^2y''+xy'+(x^2-\nu^2)y=0$: $P = 1/x$, $Q = 1-\nu^2/x^2$; at $0$, $xP = 1$ and $x^2Q = x^2-\nu^2$ are analytic, $s_0 = 1$, $t_0 = -\nu^2$, indicial $\sigma^2-\nu^2$, exponents $\pm\nu$; at infinity $Q \to 1 \ne 0$ so $Q$ is not a series starting at $1/x^2$ and the point is irregular. All as printed.
