## Findings
1. [blocking] definition (lines 33-39): the block opens "Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be random variables with partial sums $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$", assumes only a common mean $\mu$ and a common finite variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, and then asserts that $U_n = (S_n - n\mu)/\sqrt{n\sigma^2}$ "has mean $0$ and variance $1$ for every $n$". The mean is $0$ by linearity, but $\operatorname{var}(U_n) = \operatorname{var}(S_n)/(n\sigma^2)$ equals $1$ only when $\operatorname{var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2$, which needs the $X_i$ to be uncorrelated. For dependent summands it is false: with $X_i = X$ for every $i$ (common mean $\mu$, common variance $\sigma^2 > 0$) one gets $\operatorname{var}(S_n) = n^2\sigma^2$ and $\operatorname{var}(U_n) = n$. The statement block does assume independence, so nothing downstream is wrong, but the hypothesis is missing where the quantitative claim is made. Fix: add "independent" (or "uncorrelated") to the standing assumption in the definition block, or attach it to the sentence that computes the variance.
2. [minor] remark (line 145): "if $\sigma^2 = \infty$ the expansion stops at first order and the theorem is false as stated". With an infinite variance the normalisation $\sqrt{n\sigma^2}$ is not defined, so the theorem is not false but unformulated in this normalisation; what is true is that no normalisation of $S_n$ by a power of $n$ need have a normal limit, as the Cauchy paragraph immediately below demonstrates. Fix: "the expansion stops at first order and the standardisation of the statement is not even defined; nor does any other normalisation give a normal limit in general -- see the Cauchy case below".

[source] rhb-2006, §30.10, p. 1195 (and the derivation on p. 1196): Riley, Hobson and Bence state the theorem for independent $X_i$ with individual densities $f_i$ requiring only that each "possess formal means and variances", with no Lindeberg- or Lyapunov-type uniformity condition, and derive it by expanding each moment generating function (which presupposes the MGFs exist near $0$, a strictly stronger demand than a finite mean and variance).
