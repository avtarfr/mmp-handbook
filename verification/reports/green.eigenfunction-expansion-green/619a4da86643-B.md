1. [blocking] :::remark[Conventions] (line 272): "Hassani's $G_\lambda$ with our integral is wrong
   by a factor $w$" is false, and it contradicts the sentence immediately above it ("All four
   produce the same bilinear series ... with eigenfunctions normalised in the inner product that
   goes with the weight"). Hassani's $L_x$ is *not* our $\mathcal{L}$: by his (20.24), p. 614, it is
   $L_x = \frac1w\bigl[(pu')' + q\bigr]$, which is why his eigenvalue problem is $L_xu = \lambda u$
   as the table itself records. Multiplying his $(L_x-\lambda)G_\lambda = \delta/w$ through by $w$
   gives $(pG_\lambda')' + qG_\lambda - \lambda wG_\lambda = \delta$ — the same *unweighted* delta
   the other three rows carry. So his kernel is our kernel, not ours divided by $w$. Concretely,
   with $p = 1$, $q = 0$, $w = s = 2$ on $(0,1)$ with Dirichlet data and $\lambda = 0$: ours solves
   $-G'' = \delta$, giving $G = x_<(1-x_>)$ (jump $-1 = -1/p$); his solves
   $\tfrac12 G_0'' = \delta/2$, giving $G_0 = -x_<(1-x_>)$. The two differ by the *sign* convention
   of $\mathcal{L}$ (his $+(pu')'$ against our $-(pu')'$), with no factor $w$ anywhere. What really
   carries the $w$ is the *source*: his $u = \int wG f\,dy$ solves $(L_x-\lambda)u = f$, i.e.
   $(\mathcal{L}-\lambda s)u = wf$ in our normalisation, so his $f$ is our $wf$. Fix: state that
   the four kernels agree (up to the sign convention of finding 2) and that the mismatch to avoid
   is in the source — using $\int wGf$ when you wanted the solution for forcing $f$ overshoots by
   $w$.
2. [minor] :::remark[Conventions], last sentence (line 272): "the sign of every $\lambda_n$ differs
   accordingly" between Arfken's $L = (pu')' + qu$ and the unit's $\mathcal{L} = -(pu')' + qu$ holds
   only for $q \equiv 0$. The two are negatives of one another only then; with $q \ne 0$,
   $-L = -(pu')' - qu \ne \mathcal{L}$ and the two spectra are not related by a sign (only their
   directions, $\to-\infty$ and $\to+\infty$, are opposite, and that part of the sentence is
   right). The example has $q=0$, so nothing downstream is affected. The same sign caveat applies
   to Hassani's $L_x$, whose (20.24) has Arfken's sign, but the remark gives it only for Arfken.
   Fix: say the two conventions make the spectrum run in opposite directions, and that for $q=0$
   the eigenvalues are exact negatives.
3. [minor] :::example item 7 (line 322): "they differ from the exact initial-value normalisations
   of part (ii) by constant factors" — the factors are not constants. Part (ii) with
   $\alpha=\beta=0$, $a=1$, $b=e$ requires $v_t(0) = 1$ and $z_t(1) = -e$, so
   $v_{(ii)} = \sin(kt)/k$ and $z_{(ii)} = (e/k)\sin\bigl(k(1-t)\bigr)$: the factors are $k$ and
   $k/e$, functions of $\lambda$. The conclusion drawn is nonetheless sound — rescaling by any
   $c(\lambda), d(\lambda)$ leaves $G$ unchanged and leaves the residue unchanged, because
   $\tilde C = cdC$ gives $\tilde C'(\lambda_n) = c_nd_nC'(\lambda_n)$ once $C(\lambda_n) = 0$, and
   the $c_n, d_n$ cancel against $\tilde k_n = (c_n/d_n)k_n$ and $\tilde z_n = d_nz_n$. But the
   $\lambda$-dependence is exactly why the example's $C(\lambda) = -k\sin k$ carries the extra zero
   at $\lambda = 0$ that part (ii) ("the zeros of $C$ are exactly the eigenvalues") and method
   step 4 ("find the eigenvalues as the zeros of $C$") would read as a spurious eigenvalue, and
   which item 7 then has to explain away. With part (ii)'s own normalisation
   $C(\lambda) = -e\sin(k)/k$, which is $-e$ at $\lambda=0$ and whose zeros are exactly
   $\lambda = n^2\pi^2$. Fix: say the factors are $\lambda$-dependent, note that $G$ and the
   residue are insensitive to that, and point out that part (ii)'s normalisation is what removes
   the spurious zero.
4. [minor] :::remark (line 257): "the series converges absolutely and uniformly, with $G$
   continuous" is asserted from $\lambda_n \sim cn^2$ alone, but it also needs the normalised
   eigenfunctions to be uniformly bounded — true for a regular problem, and not stated. Fix: name
   the bound ($\sup_n\lVert u_n\rVert_\infty < \infty$ for the regular problem) where it is used.
