:::definition[Hermite polynomials]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§18.1, eq. (18.2), p. 872, and eq. (18.8), p. 874; hassani-2013:§8.4.1, the standardisation $K_n=(-1)^n$ and eq. (8.13), p. 248; rhb-2006:§18.9.1, eq. (18.130), p. 626; kreyszig-1978:§3.7-2, eq. (7b), p. 182"}
The **Hermite polynomials** $H_{0},H_{1},H_{2},\dots$ are the coefficients in the expansion of
$$
g(x,t) \;=\; \mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}+2tx} \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} H_{n}(x)\,\frac{t^{n}}{n!} ,
$$
which converges for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and every $t\in\mathbb{C}$, because $t\mapsto g(x,t)$ is entire. Equivalently, $H_{n}(x)=\partial_{t}^{n}g(x,t)\big|_{t=0}$.

Arfken, Weber and Harris take this generating function as the definition, so that nothing has to be imported from the general theory; Hassani, Riley, Hobson and Bence, and Kreyszig instead start from the **Rodrigues formula**
$$
H_{n}(x) \;=\; (-1)^{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}} ,
$$
and the two agree: the statement below derives each from the other. In the language of :ref[]{to=spec.orthogonal-polynomials-general}, $H_n$ is the classical family belonging to the weight $w(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}$ on $(-\infty,\infty)$, with the scale fixed by the choice Hassani writes as $K_{n}=(-1)^{n}$. That scale is a convention and nothing more; it makes the leading coefficient of $H_{n}$ equal to $2^{n}$ rather than $1$.

The first few are
$$
\begin{aligned}
H_{0}&=1, & H_{1}&=2x, & H_{2}&=4x^{2}-2,\\
H_{3}&=8x^{3}-12x, & H_{4}&=16x^{4}-48x^{2}+12, & H_{5}&=32x^{5}-160x^{3}+120x .
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

:::definition[Hermite functions]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§18.1, eqs. (18.12)–(18.13), pp. 875–876; kreyszig-1978:§3.7-2, eq. (7a), p. 181; reed-simon-1980:Appendix to §V.3, the display defining $\phi_n$ and the eigenvalue equation below it, p. 142"}
The **Hermite functions** are the Hermite polynomials with half of the weight absorbed into them. Arfken, Weber and Harris write the unnormalised version
$$
\varphi_{n}(x) \;=\; \mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}H_{n}(x) ,
$$
which is no longer a polynomial. Kreyszig and Reed and Simon use the normalised version, which we write
$$
\psi_{n}(x) \;=\; \bigl(2^{n}\,n!\,\sqrt{\pi}\,\bigr)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}H_{n}(x) ,
\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots
$$
Kreyszig obtains exactly these functions by running Gram-Schmidt (:ref[]{to=linalg.gram-schmidt}) on the sequence $t^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2}$ in the real space $L^{2}(-\infty,\infty)$; Reed and Simon obtain them instead by repeated application of $A^{\dagger}=2^{-1/2}(x-\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x)$ to the Gaussian $\psi_{0}=\pi^{-1/4}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}$, as $\psi_{n}=(n!)^{-1/2}(A^{\dagger})^{n}\psi_{0}$. The three descriptions give the same functions, with the same signs.
:::

:::statement[What the Hermite polynomials satisfy]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§18.1, eqs. (18.1), (18.3)–(18.9), (18.11), (18.13) and (18.15), pp. 871–876; rhb-2006:§18.9, eqs. (18.126) and (18.129), pp. 624–625, and §18.9.1, eqs. (18.130), (18.132)–(18.135), pp. 626–628; hassani-2013:§8.4.1, eqs. (8.13)–(8.15) and the values $k_n^{(n)}=2^{n}$ and $h_n=\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!$, pp. 248–249; kreyszig-1978:§3.7-2, eqs. (7a)–(7c), the orthonormality (8) and the Hermite equation (9), pp. 181–184; reed-simon-1980:Appendix to §V.3, Lemma 3 and the eigenvalue equation above it, p. 142"}
Let $H_{n}$ be defined by the generating function above and let $\psi_{n}$ be the normalised Hermite functions. Write $H_{-1}\equiv0$.

1. **Explicit form.** For $n\ge0$,
   $$
   H_{n}(x) \;=\; \sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{s}\,n!}{(n-2s)!\;s!}\,(2x)^{n-2s} .
   $$
   In particular $H_{n}$ is a polynomial of degree exactly $n$ with leading coefficient $2^{n}$, the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ vanishes, and
   $$
   H_{n}(-x)=(-1)^{n}H_{n}(x),
   \qquad
   H_{2m}(0)=(-1)^{m}\frac{(2m)!}{m!},
   \qquad
   H_{2m+1}(0)=0 .
   $$
2. **Rodrigues formula.** $\displaystyle H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}$.
3. **Recurrences.** For $n\ge0$,
   $$
   H_{n+1}(x) = 2x\,H_{n}(x) - 2n\,H_{n-1}(x),
   \qquad
   H_{n}'(x) = 2n\,H_{n-1}(x) .
   $$
4. **Hermite's equation.** $H_{n}$ solves
   $$
   y'' - 2x\,y' + 2n\,y = 0 .
   $$
   Riley, Hobson and Bence state this equation with a general real parameter $\nu$ in place of $n$; it is exactly when $\nu$ is a non-negative integer that it has a polynomial solution.
5. **Orthogonality and norm.** For all $m,n\ge0$,
   $$
   \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{m}(x)H_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \;=\; 2^{n}\,n!\,\sqrt{\pi}\;\delta_{mn} .
   $$
   Equivalently the $\psi_{n}$ are orthonormal in $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$ with unit weight.
6. **The eigenvalue problem solved.** Each $\psi_{n}$ satisfies
   $$
   -\psi_{n}''(x) + x^{2}\psi_{n}(x) \;=\; (2n+1)\,\psi_{n}(x) ,
   $$
   which is the self-adjoint form Arfken, Weber and Harris write as $\varphi_{n}''+(2n+1-x^{2})\varphi_{n}=0$ and Reed and Simon as $(-\mathrm{d}^{2}/\mathrm{d}x^{2}+x^{2})\phi_{n}=(2n+1)\phi_{n}$.
7. **Completeness.** $\{\psi_{n}\}_{n\ge0}$ is an orthonormal **basis** of $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$: this is Lemma 3 of the appendix to Reed and Simon's §V.3, and it is the totality statement Kreyszig records (and uses to conclude that $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$ is separable). We state it here on their authority and do not prove it; items 1–6 are proved below. Consequently every $f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$ has the mean-square convergent expansion $f=\sum_{n}(f,\psi_{n})\psi_{n}$, which Riley, Hobson and Bence write in the unnormalised form $f=\sum_{n}a_{n}H_{n}$ with
   $$
   a_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)H_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x .
   $$
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
Everything follows from one algebraic accident. The exponent $-t^{2}+2tx$ completes the square two different ways: as $x^{2}-(t-x)^{2}$, and as a product of two exponentials $\mathrm{e}^{2tx}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}}$ whose separate series are known.

The first completion makes $t$ and $x$ interchangeable up to a sign, because $\partial_{t}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)^{2}}=-\partial_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)^{2}}$; differentiating $n$ times in $t$ and setting $t=0$ therefore turns into differentiating $n$ times in $x$, which is the Rodrigues formula. The second completion multiplies out to the explicit coefficient sum, and the sum shows at a glance that $H_{n}$ has degree $n$, which powers it contains and what it is at the origin.

The recurrences come from the two first-order partial differential equations $\partial_{t}g=(2x-2t)g$ and $\partial_{x}g=2tg$ that the exponential satisfies, read coefficient by coefficient; eliminating $H_{n-1}$ between them and differentiating once gives Hermite's equation. Orthogonality is the same trick applied twice: multiply two copies of the generating function, in variables $s$ and $t$, by the weight $\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}$, and the $x$-integral is a Gaussian that evaluates to $\sqrt{\pi}\,\mathrm{e}^{2st}$. A function of the product $st$ alone has no $s^{m}t^{n}$ term with $m\neq n$, so orthogonality and the value of the norm drop out of the same line.
:::

:::proof{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§18.1, the derivations surrounding eqs. (18.3)–(18.4), p. 872, eq. (18.8), p. 874, and eqs. (18.14)–(18.15), p. 876; rhb-2006:§18.9.1, the worked derivation of the recurrences and the normalisation, pp. 626–628; hassani-2013:§8.7, Problem 8.13, p. 260"}
**Item 1, the explicit sum.** Multiply the two absolutely convergent exponential series
$$
\mathrm{e}^{2tx}=\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{(2x)^{j}}{j!}t^{j},
\qquad
\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}}=\sum_{s\ge0}\frac{(-1)^{s}}{s!}t^{2s},
$$
and collect the terms with $t^{n}$, which are those with $j+2s=n$, that is $0\le s\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $j=n-2s$:
$$
g(x,t)=\sum_{n\ge0}t^{n}\sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{s}(2x)^{n-2s}}{(n-2s)!\,s!} .
$$
Comparing with $\sum_n H_n t^{n}/n!$ and multiplying by $n!$ gives the stated sum. The rearrangement is legitimate because both series converge absolutely for every $x$ and $t$.

Reading the sum: the $s=0$ term contributes $(2x)^{n}$ and no other term reaches degree $n$, so the degree is $n$ and the leading coefficient $2^{n}$; every exponent $n-2s$ has the parity of $n$, which gives both the absence of an $x^{n-1}$ term and the parity relation $H_{n}(-x)=(-1)^{n}H_{n}(x)$; and at $x=0$ the only surviving term is the one with $n-2s=0$, which exists only for even $n=2m$ and equals $(-1)^{m}(2m)!/m!$.

We also record a bound for later use. Replacing every term of the double sum by its absolute value and running the computation backwards,
$$
\sum_{n\ge0}\bigl|H_{n}(x)\bigr|\frac{|t|^{n}}{n!}
\;\le\;\sum_{j,s\ge0}\frac{(2|x|)^{j}|t|^{j}}{j!}\frac{|t|^{2s}}{s!}
\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{2|x||t|+t^{2}} .
\tag{$\ast$}
$$

**Item 2, the Rodrigues formula.** Complete the square in the exponent the other way:
$$
-t^{2}+2tx = x^{2}-(t-x)^{2},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
g(x,t)=\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)^{2}} .
$$
The function $\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)^{2}}$ depends on $t$ and $x$ only through $t-x$, so $\partial_{t}$ acting on it equals $-\partial_{x}$ acting on it. Applying this $n$ times, and noting that $\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}$ is a constant for $\partial_{t}$,
$$
H_{n}(x)=\partial_{t}^{n}g\big|_{t=0}
=\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}\,\partial_{t}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)^{2}}\Big|_{t=0}
=(-1)^{n}\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}\,\partial_{x}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)^{2}}\Big|_{t=0}
=(-1)^{n}\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}} ,
$$
where the last step sets $t=0$ before differentiating in $x$, which is allowed because $\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)^2}$ is jointly smooth and the $t$-derivatives have already been taken.

**Item 3, the recurrences.** The exponential satisfies
$$
\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}=(2x-2t)\,g ,
\qquad
\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=2t\,g ,
$$
by direct differentiation. Insert the series into the first identity. On the left, $\partial_{t}\sum_{n}H_{n}t^{n}/n!=\sum_{n\ge0}H_{n+1}t^{n}/n!$ after shifting the index. On the right,
$$
2x\sum_{n\ge0}H_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}-2\sum_{n\ge0}H_{n}\frac{t^{n+1}}{n!}
=2x\sum_{n\ge0}H_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}-2\sum_{n\ge1}n\,H_{n-1}\frac{t^{n}}{n!} ,
$$
where the second sum was re-indexed using $t^{n+1}/n!=n'\,t^{n'}/n'!$ with $n'=n+1$. Term-by-term differentiation and re-indexing are legitimate because the series is a power series with infinite radius of convergence. Two power series in $t$ agree only if their coefficients agree, so $H_{n+1}=2xH_{n}-2nH_{n-1}$ for $n\ge1$; for $n=0$ the second sum is empty and the identity reads $H_{1}=2xH_{0}$, which is the same statement with the convention $H_{-1}=0$.

The second identity gives, in the same way,
$$
\sum_{n\ge0}H_{n}'\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=2\sum_{n\ge1}n\,H_{n-1}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},
$$
hence $H_{n}'=2nH_{n-1}$ for $n\ge1$; at $n=0$ the right-hand sum is empty and the identity reads $H_{0}'=0$, which is again the stated relation with the convention $H_{-1}=0$. Both recurrences therefore hold for every $n\ge0$, as item 3 asserts.

**Item 4, Hermite's equation.** Use $2nH_{n-1}=H_{n}'$ to eliminate $H_{n-1}$ from the three-term recurrence:
$$
H_{n+1}=2xH_{n}-H_{n}' .
$$
Differentiate this in $x$:
$$
H_{n+1}'=2H_{n}+2xH_{n}'-H_{n}'' .
$$
On the left, the derivative recurrence at index $n+1$ says $H_{n+1}'=2(n+1)H_{n}$. Equating and cancelling $2H_{n}$ from both sides leaves
$$
2n\,H_{n}=2x\,H_{n}'-H_{n}'' ,
$$
which is Hermite's equation. Each hypothesis used here is one of the two recurrences, so this holds for every $n\ge0$.

**Item 5, orthogonality and the norm.** Take two copies of the generating function, in variables $s$ and $t$, multiply by the weight, and integrate:
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}\,g(x,s)\,g(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}-s^{2}+2sx-t^{2}+2tx}\,\mathrm{d}x .
$$
The exponent is $-\bigl(x-s-t\bigr)^{2}+2st$, as one checks by expanding the square. So the integral is $\mathrm{e}^{2st}$ times a shifted Gaussian integral, and
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}g(x,s)g(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\pi}\,\mathrm{e}^{2st}
=\sqrt{\pi}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{2^{k}s^{k}t^{k}}{k!} .
$$
Now expand the left-hand side instead. The double series may be integrated term by term because, by the bound $(\ast)$,
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}\sum_{m,n}\bigl|H_{m}(x)H_{n}(x)\bigr|\frac{|s|^{m}|t|^{n}}{m!\,n!}\,\mathrm{d}x
\;\le\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{2|x|(|s|+|t|)+s^{2}+t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x
\;<\;\infty ,
$$
the last integral being finite because the Gaussian beats the exponential; Tonelli's theorem then licenses the interchange (:ref[]{to=measure.fubini-tonelli}). Therefore
$$
\sum_{m,n\ge0}\frac{s^{m}t^{n}}{m!\,n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{m}H_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x
=\sqrt{\pi}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{2^{k}}{k!}s^{k}t^{k} .
$$
Both sides are power series in the two variables $s$ and $t$, convergent on a neighbourhood of the origin, so their coefficients match one by one. The right-hand side has no $s^{m}t^{n}$ term with $m\neq n$, which gives orthogonality; and matching the $s^{n}t^{n}$ terms gives
$$
\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{n}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,2^{n}}{n!},
\qquad\text{that is}\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{n}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi} .
$$
Dividing by the two normalising constants shows $(\psi_{m},\psi_{n})=\delta_{mn}$.

**Item 6, the eigenvalue equation.** Put $\psi_{n}=c_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}H_{n}$ with $c_{n}$ the constant above. Then
$$
\psi_{n}'=c_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\bigl(H_{n}'-xH_{n}\bigr),
\qquad
\psi_{n}''=c_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\bigl(H_{n}''-2xH_{n}'+(x^{2}-1)H_{n}\bigr).
$$
By item 4, $H_{n}''-2xH_{n}'=-2nH_{n}$, so $\psi_{n}''=c_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\bigl(x^{2}-1-2n\bigr)H_{n}=(x^{2}-2n-1)\psi_{n}$, which rearranges to $-\psi_{n}''+x^{2}\psi_{n}=(2n+1)\psi_{n}$. $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used]{provenance=original}
**$n$ must be a non-negative integer.** Riley, Hobson and Bence write Hermite's equation with a real parameter $\nu$ and substitute a power series $y=\sum_{m}a_{m}x^{m}$, which forces
$$
a_{m+2}=-\frac{2(\nu-m)}{(m+1)(m+2)}\,a_{m} .
$$
If $\nu=n$ is a non-negative integer the chain of even or odd coefficients starting below $n$ terminates at $m=n$, and that branch is the polynomial $H_{n}$. If $\nu$ is not a non-negative integer, no branch terminates, and for large $m$ the ratio behaves like $a_{m+2}/a_{m}\sim 2/m$ — which is exactly the ratio of consecutive coefficients of $\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}=\sum_{j}x^{2j}/j!$, whose $x^{m}$ coefficient $1/(m/2)!$ satisfies $c_{m+2}/c_{m}=1/(m/2+1)\sim2/m$. So the solution grows like $\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}}$ and the Hermite function $\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}y$ like $\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}/2}$: it is not in $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$, and item 6 produces no eigenfunction. This is the whole content of energy quantisation in the application below: integrality of $n$ is not assumed for convenience, it is forced by square-integrability.

**The interval must be the whole line, and the weight must be the Gaussian.** Orthogonality in item 5 came from the Gaussian integral over $(-\infty,\infty)$; the half-line version $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{m}H_{n}$ is not $\delta_{mn}$ times anything, as $\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{0}H_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x=2\int_{0}^{\infty}x\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=1\neq0$ shows at once. In Sturm-Liouville language — Riley, Hobson and Bence note that Hermite's equation has the form with $p=\rho=\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}$, $q=0$, $\lambda=2n$ — the operator is self-adjoint on this interval precisely because $p$ decays fast enough to kill the boundary terms at $\pm\infty$ for all polynomial data (:ref[]{to=ode.self-adjoint-operators}). Cut the interval at $0$ and the boundary term at the cut survives.

**Absolute convergence is what licenses the term-by-term work.** Two places in the proof interchange a limit with an operation: the rearrangement into the explicit sum, and the term-by-term integration in item 5. The first is safe because both exponential series converge absolutely for all arguments. The second needs a genuine estimate, which is why the bound $(\ast)$ was recorded; without an integrable dominating function the manipulation would only be formal, which is how the derivation is usually presented.

**Completeness is a separate fact.** Items 1–6 say nothing about whether the $\psi_{n}$ span $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$. Orthonormality alone never implies completeness, and here it genuinely needs an argument — Reed and Simon refer it to problems in two later chapters, and Kreyszig states it without proof. A reader who needs only expansions of smooth, rapidly decaying functions can get by with items 1–6; a reader who wants Parseval for all of $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$ needs item 7.
:::

:::remark[Physicists' $H_{n}$ and probabilists' $He_{n}$]{provenance=adapted src="kreyszig-1978:§3.7-2, the Warning following eq. (9), p. 184"}
Two different families are called the Hermite polynomials, and both are sometimes written $H_{n}$. Kreyszig flags this explicitly, calling the terminology in the literature not unique, and defines the competing family by
$$
He_{0}(x)=1,
\qquad
He_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}\mathrm{e}^{x^{2}/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2},
\qquad n=1,2,\dots
$$
These are the **probabilists'** polynomials: the weight is the standard normal density up to a constant, $\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}$, rather than $\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}$. Everything in this unit is stated for the **physicists'** polynomials $H_{n}$, which is the convention of all five of our sources' main development.

The two families are the same objects at different scales. Comparing the Rodrigues formulas,
$$
He_{n}(x)=2^{-n/2}\,H_{n}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right),
\qquad
H_{n}(x)=2^{n/2}\,He_{n}\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,x\bigr),
$$
and correspondingly
$$
He_{n+1}(x)=x\,He_{n}(x)-n\,He_{n-1}(x),
\qquad
He_{n}'(x)=n\,He_{n-1}(x),
$$
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}He_{m}(x)He_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{2\pi}\,n!\,\delta_{mn} .
$$
The first few are $He_{0}=1$, $He_{1}=x$, $He_{2}=x^{2}-1$, $He_{3}=x^{3}-3x$, $He_{4}=x^{4}-6x^{2}+3$: note that they are monic, which is the main reason probabilists prefer them. (All three displays were checked symbolically against the definitions.)

**How to tell which one you are reading.** Look at $H_{1}$ or at the coefficient of the highest power: physicists' $H_{1}=2x$ and leading coefficient $2^{n}$; probabilists' $He_{1}=x$ and leading coefficient $1$. Or look at the weight in the orthogonality relation: $\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}$ against $\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}$. Mixing the two silently is the commonest error in this corner of the subject; a formula taken from a statistics text and dropped into an oscillator calculation will be wrong by powers of $\sqrt2$.
:::

:::method[Working with Hermite polynomials]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§18.1, the use of eq. (18.3) to construct $H_n$ from $H_0$ and $H_1$, p. 873; rhb-2006:§18.9.1, the expansion $f=\sum a_n H_n$ and its coefficient formula, p. 627; hassani-2013:§8.7, Problems 8.7 and 8.13, pp. 259–260"}
**To evaluate $H_{n}$ at a point.** Do not use the explicit sum: its terms alternate in sign and grow enormously before they cancel, so it loses precision badly for moderate $n$. Start from $H_{0}(x)=1$, $H_{1}(x)=2x$ and iterate
$$
H_{k+1}(x)=2x\,H_{k}(x)-2k\,H_{k-1}(x), \qquad k=1,\dots,n-1 .
$$
For $|x|$ of order $\sqrt{n}$ or larger the values overflow; carry $\psi_{k}$ instead, using the same recurrence rewritten for the normalised functions,
$$
\psi_{k+1}(x)=x\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{k+1}}\,\psi_{k}(x)-\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{k+1}}\,\psi_{k-1}(x),
\qquad
\psi_{0}(x)=\pi^{-1/4}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2},
$$
which keeps every quantity of size $O(1)$.

**To expand a function.** Given $f$ with $\int\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}f^{2}<\infty$:

1. Compute $a_{n}=\bigl(2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}\bigr)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)H_{n}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x$, so that $f=\sum_{n}a_{n}H_{n}$ in mean square (item 7).
2. If $f$ is even, all odd $a_{n}$ vanish, and conversely: use the parity relation to halve the work.
3. If $f$ is itself a polynomial of degree $N$, the expansion terminates at $n=N$; the fastest route is then not integration but repeated use of
   $$
   x\,H_{n}(x)=\tfrac12 H_{n+1}(x)+n\,H_{n-1}(x),
   $$
   which is the three-term recurrence solved for $xH_{n}$. Starting from $H_{0}=1$ and multiplying by $x$ one factor at a time expands $x^{N}$ exactly, with rational coefficients and no integrals at all.

**To compute moments and matrix elements.** Any integral of the form $\int\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{m}\,x^{k}\,H_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x$ is evaluated by applying $x\,H_{n}=\tfrac12H_{n+1}+nH_{n-1}$ exactly $k$ times to turn $x^{k}H_{n}$ into a finite combination $\sum_{j}c_{j}H_{j}$, and then reading off the answer as $c_{m}\cdot2^{m}m!\sqrt{\pi}$ by item 5. Only one coefficient of the combination is ever needed, and the index shifts by at most one per step, so $\int\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{m}x^{k}H_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x=0$ whenever $|m-n|>k$ or $m-n+k$ is odd.
:::

:::example[The spread of the third oscillator state]{provenance=original}
Take the dimensionless quantum oscillator state $\psi_{2}$, that is $n=2$, and compute $\langle x^{2}\rangle=\int x^{2}\psi_{2}^{2}$ and $\langle x^{4}\rangle=\int x^{4}\psi_{2}^{2}$ using nothing but the recurrence and item 5. Write $N_{2}=2^{2}\cdot2!\cdot\sqrt{\pi}=8\sqrt{\pi}$ for the norm constant of $H_{2}$, so that $\psi_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{2}^{2}/N_{2}$.

**Step 1: push the powers of $x$ through.** Apply $x\,H_{n}=\tfrac12H_{n+1}+nH_{n-1}$ repeatedly, starting from $H_{2}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
xH_{2} &= \tfrac12 H_{3}+2H_{1},\\
x^{2}H_{2} &= \tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac12H_{4}+3H_{2}\bigr)+2\bigl(\tfrac12H_{2}+H_{0}\bigr)
= \tfrac14H_{4}+\tfrac52H_{2}+2H_{0},\\
x^{3}H_{2} &= \tfrac14\bigl(\tfrac12H_{5}+4H_{3}\bigr)+\tfrac52\bigl(\tfrac12H_{3}+2H_{1}\bigr)+2\bigl(\tfrac12H_{1}\bigr)
= \tfrac18H_{5}+\tfrac94H_{3}+6H_{1},\\
x^{4}H_{2} &= \tfrac18\bigl(\tfrac12H_{6}+5H_{4}\bigr)+\tfrac94\bigl(\tfrac12H_{4}+3H_{2}\bigr)+6\bigl(\tfrac12H_{2}+H_{0}\bigr)\\
&= \tfrac{1}{16}H_{6}+\tfrac74H_{4}+\tfrac{39}{4}H_{2}+6H_{0} .
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2: read off the integrals.** By item 5, $\int\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{2}H_{j}=0$ unless $j=2$, when it equals $N_{2}=8\sqrt{\pi}$. So only the coefficient of $H_{2}$ in each line survives:
$$
\langle x^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{2}}\cdot\frac52\,N_{2}=\frac52 ,
\qquad
\langle x^{4}\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{2}}\cdot\frac{39}{4}\,N_{2}=\frac{39}{4}=9.75 .
$$
The odd moments $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle x^{3}\rangle$ vanish, because the expansions of $xH_{2}$ and $x^{3}H_{2}$ contain only odd-index Hermite polynomials and $H_{2}$ is orthogonal to all of them.

**Step 3: check.** Direct symbolic integration of $x^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{2}(x)^{2}/(8\sqrt{\pi})$ and of $x^{4}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{2}(x)^{2}/(8\sqrt{\pi})$ over $\mathbb{R}$ returns $5/2$ and $39/4$. The general pattern, obtained the same way for $n=0,1,2,3$, is
$$
\langle x^{2}\rangle_{n}=n+\tfrac12 ,
\qquad
\langle x^{4}\rangle_{n}=\tfrac34\bigl(2n^{2}+2n+1\bigr),
$$
giving $(\tfrac12,\tfrac34)$, $(\tfrac32,\tfrac{15}{4})$, $(\tfrac52,\tfrac{39}{4})$, $(\tfrac72,\tfrac{75}{4})$ for $n=0,1,2,3$; all eight numbers were verified symbolically.

**What the numbers mean.** $\langle x^{2}\rangle_{n}=n+\tfrac12$ is the virial theorem in this problem: in the units of item 6 the energy is $\tfrac12(-\mathrm{d}^{2}/\mathrm{d}x^{2}+x^{2})$ with eigenvalue $n+\tfrac12$, and the mean potential energy $\tfrac12\langle x^{2}\rangle=\tfrac12\bigl(n+\tfrac12\bigr)$ is exactly half of it, so kinetic and potential energies share the total equally. And the kurtosis of the position distribution,
$$
\frac{\langle x^{4}\rangle}{\langle x^{2}\rangle^{2}}=\frac{39/4}{25/4}=\frac{39}{25}=1.56 ,
$$
is below the Gaussian value $3$: the $n=2$ state, with its two nodes and its probability piled up near the classical turning points, is far flatter-topped than a Gaussian. For $n=0$ the same ratio is $(3/4)/(1/4)=3$, as it must be, since $\psi_{0}^{2}$ *is* a Gaussian.
:::

:::application{provenance=original}
**The quantum harmonic oscillator.** This is the reason Hermite polynomials are in a physics curriculum at all. Arfken, Weber and Harris start from $-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\psi''+\frac{k}{2}z^{2}\psi=E\psi$ and rescale $z=b\,x$ with
$$
b=\frac{\hbar^{1/2}}{(km)^{1/4}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}},
\qquad
\omega=\sqrt{k/m} ,
$$
which reduces the equation to $-\tfrac12\varphi''+\tfrac12x^{2}\varphi=\lambda\varphi$ — item 6 with $\lambda=n+\tfrac12$. Undoing the substitution,
$$
\psi_{n}(z)=\bigl(b\,2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}\bigr)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(z/b)^{2}/2}\,H_{n}(z/b),
\qquad
E_{n}=\bigl(n+\tfrac12\bigr)\hbar\omega=\bigl(n+\tfrac12\bigr)\hbar\sqrt{k/m} .
$$
The argument of $H_{n}$ is $z/b$ and not $bz$: the quantity $b$ is a length, so $z/b$ is the only dimensionless combination available. (Arfken, Weber and Harris print this eigenfunction in eq. (18.19) as $N_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-(\alpha z)^{2}/2}H_{n}(\alpha z)$ with $\alpha=\hbar^{1/2}/(km)^{1/4}$, which is our $b$ rather than $1/b$; the argument needs the reciprocal of the $\alpha$ they print, and their own normalisation constant $N_{n}=[\alpha/(2^{n}\sqrt{\pi}\,n!)]^{1/2}$ of eq. (18.20) agrees with the reciprocal reading, since it is $(b\,2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi})^{-1/2}$ exactly when $\alpha=1/b$. We follow the substitution $z=\hbar^{1/2}x/(km)^{1/4}$ that they make on the preceding page, which fixes the reading unambiguously. That the $\psi_{n}$ displayed above solve the dimensional equation with the energies quoted, and are normalised, was checked symbolically for $n\le3$.)

Two features are worth isolating. The spectrum is *equally spaced*, which is what makes the oscillator the model for a mode of a field and the $n$ a photon or phonon number; and the lowest energy is $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$, not zero, the zero-point energy, which is forced by the fact that a normalisable wave function has finite spatial extent and so cannot have zero kinetic energy. Reed and Simon's route — raising from the ground state with $A^{\dagger}$, which is the ladder-operator argument Arfken, Weber and Harris also give — produces the same functions and explains the equal spacing structurally. In the dimensionless variables of item 6, where $H=\tfrac12\bigl(-\mathrm{d}^{2}/\mathrm{d}x^{2}+x^{2}\bigr)=N+\tfrac12$ with $N=A^{\dagger}A$, the commutator is $[H,A^{\dagger}]=[N,A^{\dagger}]=A^{\dagger}$: applying $A^{\dagger}$ moves one rung up, of height $1$ in $\lambda$. Restoring units multiplies the ladder spacing by $\hbar\omega$, so for the dimensional Hamiltonian the same relation reads $[H,A^{\dagger}]=\hbar\omega A^{\dagger}$. The ladder has a bottom because $\|A\psi\|^{2}\ge0$.

**Molecular vibration and infrared spectra.** Near the bottom of a diatomic potential well the interatomic potential is quadratic to leading order, so the vibrational levels start out equally spaced and the wave functions are Hermite functions in the bond-length displacement. The selection rule $\Delta n=\pm1$ for infrared absorption is the statement that $\int\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}H_{m}\,x\,H_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x$ vanishes unless $|m-n|=1$, which is the $k=1$ case of the matrix-element rule in the method above. Anharmonicity — the cubic and quartic corrections to the potential — is then handled by perturbation theory (:ref[]{to=asym.regular-perturbation}) with exactly the matrix elements the method computes, and it is what makes real vibrational ladders converge rather than stay equally spaced.

**Hermite-Gauss beams in optics.** The paraxial wave equation in a rectangular geometry separates into two copies of the oscillator equation, so the transverse modes of a laser cavity with rectangular symmetry are products $\psi_{m}(x/w)\psi_{n}(y/w)$ of Hermite functions, labelled $\mathrm{TEM}_{mn}$. The $m$ and $n$ nodal lines are directly visible in a beam profile, and the orthogonality of item 5 is why the modes of a cavity do not exchange power.

**Gauss-Hermite quadrature.** Because $H_{n}$ has $n$ real simple zeros (:ref[]{to=spec.orthogonal-polynomials-general}), there are weights $w_{1},\dots,w_{n}$ making
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})
$$
exact for every polynomial $f$ of degree at most $2n-1$ (:ref[]{to=num.quadrature}). This is the standard way to integrate against a Gaussian in any dimension where the integrand is smooth — expectation values under a normal distribution, thermal averages, and the moment-matching step of a Gaussian-quadrature filter in data assimilation (:ref[]{to=stats.data-assimilation}).

**Series in the probabilists' polynomials.** With the $He_{n}$ of the convention remark, the Gram-Charlier and Edgeworth expansions write a near-Gaussian probability density as $\phi(x)\sum_{n}c_{n}He_{n}(x)$ with $\phi$ the standard normal density, the coefficients being combinations of the cumulants: $c_{3}$ carries the skewness, $c_{4}$ the excess kurtosis. These are the standard corrections to the central limit theorem (:ref[]{to=prob.central-limit-theorem}) at finite sample size, and the reason the probabilists' normalisation exists at all: with the weight equal to the normal density, $c_{n}=\mathbb{E}[He_{n}(X)]/n!$ with no stray powers of two.
