:::statement[Small argument]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§14.3, Eq. (14.58) and Eq. (14.62), p. 668, and Eq. (14.66), p. 670; arfken-2013:§14.4, Eqs. (14.79)-(14.82), p. 675; arfken-2013:§14.5, Eq. (14.100), p. 681, and Eqs. (14.110)-(14.111), p. 683"}
As $x \to 0^{+}$, with $\gamma$ the Euler-Mascheroni constant, the leading behaviour of each function is the leading term of its series (or, where the series is logarithmic, the logarithm):

$$
J_\nu(x) \to \frac{1}{\Gamma(1+\nu)}\Bigl(\frac{x}{2}\Bigr)^{\nu} ,
\qquad
J_\nu'(x) \to \frac{\nu}{2\Gamma(1+\nu)}\Bigl(\frac{x}{2}\Bigr)^{\nu-1} ,
$$

$$
Y_\nu(x) = -\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{\pi}\Bigl(\frac{2}{x}\Bigr)^{\nu} + \cdots \quad(\nu > 0) ,
\qquad
Y_0(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\Bigl[\gamma + \ln\frac{x}{2}\Bigr] + \cdots ,
$$

$$
H^{(1)}_0(x) \approx 1 + i\frac{2}{\pi}\bigl(\ln x + \gamma - \ln 2\bigr) ,
\qquad
H^{(1)}_\nu(x) \approx -i\,\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{\pi}\Bigl(\frac{2}{x}\Bigr)^{\nu} \quad(\nu > 0) ,
$$

with $H^{(2)}_\nu$ the complex conjugate of these for real $x$, and

$$
I_\nu(x) = \frac{x^{\nu}}{2^{\nu}\Gamma(\nu+1)} + \cdots ,
\qquad
K_0(x) = -\ln x - \gamma + \ln 2 + \cdots ,
\qquad
K_\nu(x) = 2^{\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu)\,x^{-\nu} + \cdots \quad(\nu > 0) .
$$

The form of $Y_\nu$ given above is derived for non-integer $\nu$; for integer $n > 0$ the series contains a term $\tfrac{2}{\pi}J_n(x)\ln(x/2)$ as well, but the most divergent term is the same :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.3, after Eq. (14.61), p. 668"}.

The pattern is uniform: in each pair exactly one member is regular at the origin ($J_\nu$ and $I_\nu$, vanishing like $x^{\nu}$ for $\nu>0$ and tending to $1$ for $\nu = 0$) and one is singular ($Y_\nu$ and $K_\nu$, diverging like $x^{-\nu}$ for $\nu>0$ and like a logarithm at $\nu = 0$). This is what makes "bounded at the origin" a usable boundary condition.
:::

:::statement[Large argument]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§14.6, Eqs. (14.133)-(14.143), pp. 692-693; strauss-2008:§10.5, Eq. (5), p. 283, and Eqs. (14)-(15), p. 285"}
Write $\mu = 4\nu^{2}$ and define the two formal series

$$
P_\nu(z) \sim 1 - \frac{(\mu-1)(\mu-9)}{2!\,(8z)^{2}} + \frac{(\mu-1)(\mu-9)(\mu-25)(\mu-49)}{4!\,(8z)^{4}} - \cdots ,
$$

$$
Q_\nu(z) \sim \frac{\mu-1}{1!\,(8z)} - \frac{(\mu-1)(\mu-9)(\mu-25)}{3!\,(8z)^{3}} + \cdots ,
$$

and abbreviate the phase $\chi = z - \bigl(\nu+\tfrac12\bigr)\tfrac{\pi}{2} = z - \tfrac{\nu\pi}{2} - \tfrac{\pi}{4}$. Then, as $|z| \to \infty$:

$$
H^{(1)}_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\;e^{i\chi}\bigl[P_\nu(z) + iQ_\nu(z)\bigr] , \qquad -\pi < \arg z < 2\pi ,
$$

$$
H^{(2)}_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\;e^{-i\chi}\bigl[P_\nu(z) - iQ_\nu(z)\bigr] , \qquad -2\pi < \arg z < \pi ,
$$

$$
J_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\Bigl[P_\nu(z)\cos\chi - Q_\nu(z)\sin\chi\Bigr] ,
\qquad
Y_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\Bigl[P_\nu(z)\sin\chi + Q_\nu(z)\cos\chi\Bigr] ,
$$

both for $-\pi < \arg z < \pi$, and for the modified functions

$$
K_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}\;e^{-z}\bigl[P_\nu(iz) + iQ_\nu(iz)\bigr] ,
\qquad
I_\nu(z) = \frac{e^{z}}{\sqrt{2\pi z}}\bigl[P_\nu(iz) - iQ_\nu(iz)\bigr] ,
$$

the latter for $-\pi/2 < \arg z < \pi/2$. The $K_\nu$ expansion holds for $-3\pi/2 < \arg z < 3\pi/2$ and reads, written out,

$$
K_\nu(z) \sim \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}\;e^{-z}\left[1 + \frac{4\nu^{2}-1^{2}}{1!\,8z} + \frac{(4\nu^{2}-1^{2})(4\nu^{2}-3^{2})}{2!\,(8z)^{2}} + \cdots\right] ,
$$

in which every sign is $+$: the alternating signs written into $P_\nu$ and $Q_\nu$ are cancelled by the rotation of the argument from $z$ to $iz$, so the series for $P_\nu(iz)$ and $iQ_\nu(iz)$ carry all positive signs :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, the remark after Eq. (14.136), p. 692"}. (The *terms* are positive only when every factor $4\nu^{2}-(2k-1)^{2}$ is; for $\nu$ of moderate size the early terms alternate.) Equivalently, in closed form,

$$
K_\nu(z) \sim \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}\;e^{-z}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\nu+r+\tfrac12\bigr)}{r!\,\Gamma\bigl(\nu-r+\tfrac12\bigr)}\,(2z)^{-r} .
$$

**These series diverge.** Each is an :ref[asymptotic series]{to=asym.asymptotic-series} in Poincaré's sense: truncating after $N$ terms gives an error of the order of the first omitted term, so for large enough $z$ a few terms give high accuracy, but the sum over all terms does not converge for any $z$. The symbol $\sim$ is used accordingly, and the equalities above are to be read term by term in $1/z$.

Keeping only the leading term, and recording the error order, gives the statement most often used:

$$
J_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\cos\Bigl(z - \frac{\nu\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4}\Bigr) + O\bigl(z^{-3/2}\bigr) ,
\qquad
Y_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\sin\Bigl(z - \frac{\nu\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4}\Bigr) + O\bigl(z^{-3/2}\bigr) ,
$$

$$
H^{(1),(2)}_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\;e^{\pm i(z - \nu\pi/2 - \pi/4)} + O\bigl(z^{-3/2}\bigr) ,
\qquad
K_\nu(z) \sim \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}e^{-z} ,
\qquad
I_\nu(z) \sim \frac{e^{z}}{\sqrt{2\pi z}} ,
$$

the first three as stated by :cite[Strauss]{work=strauss-2008 loc="§10.5, Eqs. (5), (14) and (15), pp. 283, 285"}.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
**Why $x^{-1/2}$ times an exponential, before any computation.** Substituting $y = x^{-1/2}v$ into Bessel's equation removes the first-derivative term and leaves

$$
v'' + \Bigl(1 - \frac{4\nu^{2}-1}{4x^{2}}\Bigr)v = 0
$$

:cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="Example 14.4.13, p. 432"}. At large $x$ the bracket is $1 + O(x^{-2})$, so $v$ behaves like $\cos x$ or $\sin x$, and $y = x^{-1/2}v$ like $x^{-1/2}$ times a sinusoid. The same substitution in the modified equation gives $v'' - (1 + \cdots)v = 0$ and hence $x^{-1/2}e^{\pm x}$. That is the whole shape of the answer. What this argument cannot supply is the *connection*: the constant in front and the phase $-\nu\pi/2-\pi/4$ depend on which solution one started from at the origin, and a local analysis at infinity cannot see the origin.

**How the connection is made.** This is the reason the derivation goes through an integral representation rather than through the differential equation. A contour integral representing $H^{(1)}_\nu$ is a single formula valid at both ends: it defines the function unambiguously (so the normalisation is not lost), and its large-$x$ behaviour can be extracted by :ref[steepest descent]{to=asym.steepest-descent}. The exponent $\tfrac{x}{2}(z - z^{-1})$ has saddle points at $z = \pm i$; the contour for $H^{(1)}$ can be pushed over the one at $z = +i$, where the exponent takes the value $ix$ — and there is the $e^{ix}$. The phase $-\nu\pi/2 - \pi/4$ is assembled from three small factors: the $z^{-\nu-1}$ in the integrand evaluated at $z = i$, the $1/\pi i$ in front, and the direction $3\pi/4$ in which the path crosses the saddle.

**Why a divergent series rather than a convergent one.** The point $z = \infty$ is an irregular singular point of Bessel's equation, so there is no theorem guaranteeing a convergent series in $1/z$ there, and in fact there is none. The mechanism is visible in the derivation of the $K_\nu$ expansion: one writes $K_\nu$ as $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^{\nu-1/2}(1+t/2z)^{\nu-1/2}dt$ times an elementary factor, expands the last bracket by the binomial theorem, and integrates term by term. The binomial series converges only for $t < 2|z|$, but the integral runs to $t = \infty$; the exponential $e^{-t}$ keeps each individual term finite, but the interchange is illegitimate and the price is a series that is asymptotic and not convergent :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, the paragraph after Eq. (14.133), p. 692"}. This is the same mechanism as in :ref[Watson's lemma]{to=asym.watson-lemma}.

**Why one computation suffices for six functions.** $K_\nu$ is defined from $H^{(1)}_\nu$ at a rotated argument, $J_\nu$ and $Y_\nu$ are the real and imaginary parts of $H^{(1)}_\nu$ on the real axis, $H^{(2)}_\nu$ is its conjugate, and $I_\nu$ is $i^{-\nu}J_\nu(ix)$. So one expansion — the one for $K_\nu$, which is the easiest because its integral representation has no oscillation in it — produces all the others by substitution.
:::

:::proof[The leading Hankel asymptotics by steepest descent]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§14.6, Eqs. (14.119)-(14.126), pp. 688-689"}
**Step 1 (the representation).** Take as given the contour representation of :ref[]{to=spec.bessel-integral-representations},

$$
H^{(1)}_\nu(t) = \frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{C_1}e^{(t/2)(z - 1/z)}\,z^{-\nu-1}\,dz ,
$$

with $C_1$ a contour running from $z = 0$ out to $\infty e^{i\pi}$; the second Hankel function has the same integrand on the reflected contour $C_2$ :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, Eqs. (14.119)-(14.120), p. 688"}. Take $t > 0$ large.

**Step 2 (locate the saddle).** Split the integrand into a slowly varying factor $g(z) = z^{-\nu-1}$ and an exponential $e^{w}$ with

$$
w(z) = \frac{t}{2}\Bigl(z - \frac1z\Bigr) ,
\qquad
w'(z) = \frac{t}{2}\Bigl(1 + \frac{1}{z^{2}}\Bigr) ,
$$

so $w'(z) = 0$ at $z = \pm i$. The contour $C_1$ can be deformed through $z_0 = +i$ and not through $-i$. At that saddle,

$$
w(i) = \frac{t}{2}\Bigl(i - \frac1i\Bigr) = \frac{t}{2}(i + i) = it ,
\qquad
w''(z) = -\frac{t}{z^{3}} ,
\qquad
w''(i) = -\frac{t}{i^{3}} = -it .
$$

**Step 3 (choose the direction of descent).** The saddle-point formula of :ref[]{to=asym.steepest-descent} gives

$$
\int_{C}g(z)e^{w(z)}\,dz \;\approx\; g(z_0)\,e^{w(z_0)}\,e^{i\theta}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|w''(z_0)|}} ,
\qquad
\theta = -\frac{\arg w''(z_0)}{2} + \frac{\pi}{2}\ \text{ or }\ \frac{3\pi}{2} .
$$

Here $\arg w''(i) = \arg(-it) = -\pi/2$, so $\theta = \pi/4 + \pi/2 = 3\pi/4$ or $\theta = \pi/4 + 3\pi/2 = 7\pi/4$. The value $7\pi/4 \equiv -\pi/4$ is not available: the contour cannot be brought to cross the saddle in that direction without first passing through a region where $|e^{w}|$ exceeds its value at the saddle, which would make the saddle not dominant. So $\theta = 3\pi/4$.

**Step 4 (assemble).** With $g(i) = i^{-\nu-1} = e^{i(\pi/2)(-\nu-1)}$ and $|w''(i)| = t$,

$$
H^{(1)}_\nu(t) \approx \frac{1}{\pi i}\;e^{i(\pi/2)(-\nu-1)}\;e^{it}\;e^{3i\pi/4}\;\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t}} .
$$

Collect the modulus: $\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{2\pi/t} = \sqrt{2/\pi t}$. Collect the phases, using $1/i = e^{-i\pi/2}$:

$$
-\frac{\pi}{2} \;-\; \frac{(\nu+1)\pi}{2} \;+\; t \;+\; \frac{3\pi}{4}
\;=\; t - \frac{\nu\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{3\pi}{4}
\;=\; t - \frac{\nu\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4} .
$$

Hence

$$
H^{(1)}_\nu(t) \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi t}}\;e^{i(t - \nu\pi/2 - \pi/4)} ,
$$

and the saddle at $z = -i$ gives, by the same computation with the conjugate phases,

$$
H^{(2)}_\nu(t) \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi t}}\;e^{-i(t - \nu\pi/2 - \pi/4)} .
$$

Taking real and imaginary parts for real $t$ and real $\nu$ gives the leading forms of $J_\nu$ and $Y_\nu$. $\square$
:::

:::proof[The full expansion, from an integral representation for $K_\nu$]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§14.6, Eqs. (14.128)-(14.143), pp. 690-693"}
**Step 1 (the representation).** For $\nu > -\tfrac12$ and $\operatorname{Re}z > 0$,

$$
K_\nu(z) = \frac{\pi^{1/2}}{\Gamma\bigl(\nu+\tfrac12\bigr)}\Bigl(\frac{z}{2}\Bigr)^{\nu}\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-zx}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{\nu-1/2}\,dx
$$

:cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, Eq. (14.128), p. 690"}. (That this integral solves the modified Bessel equation, has the right behaviour at the origin and decays at infinity are the three checks that identify it with $K_\nu$; Arfken carries them out on pp. 690-692, the third of them being a by-product of this proof.)

**Step 2 (move the exponential outside).** Substitute $x = 1 + t/z$, so $dx = dt/z$ and $x$ runs from $1$ to $\infty$ as $t$ runs from $0$ to $\infty$. Then $e^{-zx} = e^{-z}e^{-t}$ and

$$
x^{2}-1 = \frac{t^{2}}{z^{2}} + \frac{2t}{z} = \frac{2t}{z}\Bigl(1 + \frac{t}{2z}\Bigr) ,
$$

so $(x^{2}-1)^{\nu-1/2} = (2t/z)^{\nu-1/2}(1+t/2z)^{\nu-1/2}$. Collecting the powers of $z$ — the prefactor supplies $z^{\nu}2^{-\nu}$, the bracket $2^{\nu-1/2}z^{-\nu+1/2}$, the measure $z^{-1}$ — everything combines to $2^{-1/2}z^{-1/2}$, and

$$
K_\nu(z) = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}\;\frac{e^{-z}}{\Gamma\bigl(\nu+\tfrac12\bigr)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\,t^{\nu-1/2}\Bigl(1 + \frac{t}{2z}\Bigr)^{\nu-1/2}dt .
$$

The exponential decay $e^{-z}$ is now explicit and outside the integral, which is the point of the substitution.

**Step 3 (expand and integrate term by term).** Apply the binomial series to the last factor,

$$
\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{2z}\Bigr)^{\nu-1/2} = \sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\binom{\nu-\tfrac12}{r}\Bigl(\frac{t}{2z}\Bigr)^{r} ,
\qquad
\binom{\nu-\tfrac12}{r} = \frac{\Gamma\bigl(\nu+\tfrac12\bigr)}{r!\,\Gamma\bigl(\nu-r+\tfrac12\bigr)} ,
$$

and integrate each term against $e^{-t}t^{\nu-1/2}$ using $\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{\nu+r-1/2}\,dt = \Gamma\bigl(\nu+r+\tfrac12\bigr)$, the Euler integral for the :ref[Gamma function]{to=spec.gamma-function}. The factor $\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac12)$ cancels and

$$
K_\nu(z) \sim \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}\;e^{-z}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\nu+r+\tfrac12\bigr)}{r!\,\Gamma\bigl(\nu-r+\tfrac12\bigr)}(2z)^{-r} .
$$

**Step 4 (why $\sim$ and not $=$).** The binomial series converges only for $t < 2|z|$, but the integration runs to $t = \infty$. The interchange of sum and integral is therefore not justified, and the resulting series diverges for every $z$. What survives is the asymptotic property: the $e^{-t}$ weight concentrates the integral near $t = O(1)$, where the expansion is valid for large $|z|$, so truncating after $N$ terms leaves an error of the size of the next term.

**Step 5 (write it with $P_\nu$ and $Q_\nu$).** Expanding the ratio of Gamma functions, $\Gamma(\nu+r+\tfrac12)/\Gamma(\nu-r+\tfrac12)$ is the product of the $2r$ factors $(\nu+r-\tfrac12)(\nu+r-\tfrac32)\cdots(\nu-r+\tfrac12)$, which pairs off as $\prod_{k=1}^{r}\bigl(\nu^{2}-(k-\tfrac12)^{2}\bigr) = 4^{-r}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\bigl(\mu - (2k-1)^{2}\bigr)$ with $\mu = 4\nu^{2}$. Hence the $r$-th term is $\prod_{k=1}^{r}\bigl(\mu-(2k-1)^{2}\bigr)\big/\bigl(r!\,(8z)^{r}\bigr)$, which is the written-out form in the statement. Splitting it into even $r$ (real when $z$ is replaced by $iz$) and odd $r$ (imaginary) is exactly the split into $P_\nu(iz)$ and $iQ_\nu(iz)$ defined in the statement.

**Step 6 (the other five functions).** Rearranging the definition $K_\nu(x) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}i^{\nu+1}H^{(1)}_\nu(ix)$ of :ref[]{to=spec.modified-bessel} gives

$$
H^{(1)}_\nu(z) = \frac{2}{\pi}\,e^{-(i\pi/2)(\nu+1)}\,K_\nu(-iz) ,
$$

and substituting the expansion of Step 5, with $-i$ for the argument, produces the stated $H^{(1)}_\nu$ expansion; analytic continuation widens the sector in which it holds to $-\pi < \arg z < 2\pi$. Then $H^{(2)}_\nu$ is the complex conjugate for real argument, $J_\nu$ and $Y_\nu$ are the real and imaginary parts of $H^{(1)}_\nu$ for real $z$ and real $\nu$, which gives the $\cos\chi$, $\sin\chi$ combinations, and $I_\nu(z) = i^{-\nu}J_\nu(iz)$ gives the last one. $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used, and what the sources differ about]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§14.6, the sector statements with Eqs. (14.138)-(14.143), pp. 692-693, and the properties of the asymptotic forms, including the accuracy criterion Eq. (14.145) and the termination at half-integer order, pp. 693-694; hassani-2013:§16.2.1, pp. 504-505"}
**The sectors matter.** Each expansion is quoted with a range of $\arg z$, and they are not the same range. $H^{(1)}_\nu$ is valid on $-\pi < \arg z < 2\pi$ and $H^{(2)}_\nu$ on $-2\pi < \arg z < \pi$; $J_\nu$ and $Y_\nu$ only on $-\pi < \arg z < \pi$; $I_\nu$ only on the right half-plane $|\arg z| < \pi/2$; $K_\nu$ on $-3\pi/2 < \arg z < 3\pi/2$, wider than the strip $|\arg z| < \pi/2$ on which the integral of Step 1 converges, because the finished series continues analytically. Using one outside its sector is the Stokes phenomenon in practice: a subdominant exponential that was invisible becomes dominant, and the formula silently loses its leading term. The narrow sector for $I_\nu$ is the clearest case — $I_\nu$ grows like $e^{z}$ on the positive axis but oscillates on the imaginary axis, where it is a Bessel function in disguise.

**$\nu > -\tfrac12$ in the integral representation.** Step 1 needs it for convergence of $\int_1^{\infty}(x^{2}-1)^{\nu-1/2}dx$ near $x = 1$. The *conclusion*, however, holds for all $\nu$, because $P_\nu$ and $Q_\nu$ depend on $\nu$ only through $\mu = 4\nu^{2}$ and $K_{-\nu} = K_\nu$.

**Where the divergence comes from.** Step 4 above, and nowhere else: every other step is exact. This is worth saying because it tells you what the series can and cannot do. It cannot be summed; it can be truncated. The best accuracy available at a given $z$ is reached by stopping at the smallest term, and is of that term's order — the optimal truncation of :ref[]{to=asym.asymptotic-series}. The worked example below exhibits this.

**Half-integer order: the series terminates.** For $\nu = \pm\tfrac12, \pm\tfrac32, \dots$ one of the factors $\mu - (2k-1)^{2} = 4\nu^{2} - (2k-1)^{2}$ vanishes, so $P_\nu$ and $Q_\nu$ become polynomials in $1/z$ and the asymptotic forms become *exact* solutions :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, p. 694"}. At $\nu = \tfrac12$, $\mu = 1$, so $P_{1/2} = 1$ and $Q_{1/2} = 0$, and the expansion collapses to $J_{1/2}(z) = \sqrt{2/\pi z}\cos(z - \pi/2) = \sqrt{2/\pi z}\sin z$ — which is the exact closed form :cite[]{work=rhb-2006 loc="§18.5.1, the worked example, p. 605"}. This is the cross-check that fixes every convention in the statement, and it is also the source of the elementary closed forms of :ref[]{to=spec.spherical-bessel}.

**How large is "large".** The leading term of $J_n$ is accurate when the $Q_\nu\sin\chi$ term is negligible, that is when $8x \gg |4n^{2}-1|$ :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, Eq. (14.145), p. 694"}. For $n = 0$ this is satisfied already around $x = 5$; for $n$ of order $10$ the asymptotic region begins near $x = 50$. The condition is about $\nu^{2}/x$, not about $x$ alone, which is why high-order Bessel functions need a different treatment (an expansion in which $\nu$ and $x$ are comparable) rather than more terms of this one.

**Two sources, two regimes.** We follow Arfken's $P_\nu$, $Q_\nu$ form throughout. :cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="§16.2.1, pp. 504-505"} derives the same leading forms by a saddle-point calculation performed at fixed $\nu/x$, writing $\nu = x\cos\beta$, and then specialises to $x \gg \nu$; in that specialisation the first correction appears as a $\nu$-independent $1/8ix$, and his two-term results read $J_\nu(x)\approx\sqrt{2/\pi x}\bigl[\cos\chi + \tfrac{1}{8x}\sin\chi\bigr]$ and $Y_\nu(x)\approx\sqrt{2/\pi x}\bigl[\sin\chi - \tfrac{1}{8x}\cos\chi\bigr]$. These agree with Arfken term by term at $\nu = 0$, where $Q_0 = -1/8z$. They are not in conflict for general $\nu$ either: Hassani's expansion parameter is $1/(x\sin\beta) = 1/\sqrt{x^{2}-\nu^{2}}$ and his phase is $x\sin\beta - \nu\beta$, and expanding both for fixed $\nu$ and large $x$ restores the $\nu$-dependence that Arfken's $Q_\nu = (4\nu^{2}-1)/8z$ carries explicitly. The practical point: if you use Hassani's two-term formulas with the simplified phase $\chi$, they are reliable only for $\nu^{2} \ll x$; Arfken's are uniform in $\nu$ to the order stated.

**What the small-argument statement does not claim.** Each line of the small-argument statement is a leading term, not an expansion with an error bound; the symbol used in Arfken is $\to$ or "$+\cdots$", and we have kept it. For $Y_\nu$ at integer order the omitted terms include a $J_n(x)\ln(x/2)$ piece which is *not* negligible relative to the next power, only relative to the leading $x^{-n}$.
:::

:::method[Using the expansions]{provenance=original}
1. **Decide which end you are at.** Compare $x$ with $\nu$. If $x \lesssim 1$, use the small-argument statement. If $8x \gg |4\nu^{2}-1|$, use the large-argument one. If $x$ and $\nu$ are comparable, neither applies: this is the transition region, where the function changes character, and it needs uniform (Airy-type) asymptotics instead — see :ref[]{to=spec.airy-functions}.
2. **Check that your argument is in the sector.** For real positive arguments every formula in the statement applies. For complex arguments, read the sector off the statement before using the formula, especially for $I_\nu$.
3. **Compute the phase first.** Almost all errors in using these formulas are errors in $\chi = x - \nu\pi/2 - \pi/4$. Note that it depends on $\nu$, so $J_0$ and $J_1$ are a quarter-period out of step: this is why the zeros of successive Bessel functions interlace at large argument.
4. **Add terms until they stop shrinking, then stop.** Compute the terms $t_r = \prod_{k=1}^{r}\bigl(\mu-(2k-1)^{2}\bigr)/\bigl(r!\,(8z)^{r}\bigr)$ one at a time by the ratio $t_r/t_{r-1} = \bigl(\mu-(2r-1)^{2}\bigr)/(8zr)$. Stop at the smallest $|t_r|$; the error is then of that order. Adding further terms makes the answer worse.
5. **Estimate the error as the first omitted term.** This is the working rule for an asymptotic series and is what the example below confirms numerically.
6. **Check against a special value.** At $\nu = \pm\tfrac12$ the series terminates and the result must reproduce $\sqrt{2/\pi x}\sin x$ or $\sqrt{2/\pi x}\cos x$ exactly. Any sign or phase error in a hand-derived formula shows up there immediately.
:::

:::example[Two numerical checks: $J_0(10)$ and $K_0(5)$]{provenance=original}
**Part (a): the oscillatory case.** Take $\nu = 0$, $x = 10$, so $\mu = 0$ and $\chi = 10 - \pi/4 = 9.2146018$, with

$$
\cos\chi = -0.9779942 ,
\qquad
\sin\chi = 0.2086322 ,
\qquad
\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\cdot10}} = 0.2523133 .
$$

The leading term alone gives

$$
J_0(10) \approx (0.2523133)(-0.9779942) = -0.2467609 ,
$$

against the true value $J_0(10) = -0.2459358$: an error of $-8.3\times10^{-4}$.

Now include the next order. With $\mu = 0$,

$$
P_0(10) = 1 - \frac{(-1)(-9)}{2!\,(80)^{2}} = 1 - \frac{9}{12800} = 0.9992969 ,
\qquad
Q_0(10) = \frac{-1}{80} = -0.0125 ,
$$

so

$$
J_0(10) \approx 0.2523133\bigl[(0.9992969)(-0.9779942) - (-0.0125)(0.2086322)\bigr]
= 0.2523133\,(-0.9746987) = -0.2459294 .
$$

The error has dropped to $6.4\times10^{-6}$, a factor of $130$ for one extra order in $1/x$ — consistent with the accuracy criterion $8x \gg |4\nu^{2}-1|$, which here reads $80 \gg 1$.

**Part (b): the exponential case, and optimal truncation.** Take $\nu = 0$, $z = 5$, so $8z = 40$ and the prefactor is

$$
\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{10}}\,e^{-5} = (0.5604991)(0.0067379) = 0.0037766134 .
$$

The terms $t_r$ are generated by $t_0 = 1$ and $t_r = t_{r-1}\cdot\bigl(-(2r-1)^{2}\bigr)/(40r)$, giving

$$
t_1 = -0.0250000 , \quad t_2 = 0.0028125 , \quad t_3 = -0.0005859 , \quad t_4 = 0.0001794 , \quad t_5 = -0.0000727 .
$$

The partial sums of the bracket, and the resulting approximations to $K_0(5) = 0.00369110$, are

- $1$ term: bracket $1.0000000$, value $0.00377661$, error $+8.55\times10^{-5}$;
- $2$ terms: bracket $0.9750000$, value $0.00368220$, error $-8.90\times10^{-6}$;
- $3$ terms: bracket $0.9778125$, value $0.00369282$, error $+1.72\times10^{-6}$;
- $4$ terms: bracket $0.9772266$, value $0.00369061$, error $-4.91\times10^{-7}$;
- $5$ terms: bracket $0.9774060$, value $0.00369128$, error $+1.86\times10^{-7}$.

Each error is smaller than, and has the sign of, the first omitted term multiplied by the prefactor — step 5 of the method. For instance after $4$ terms the next term is $t_4 = 1.794\times10^{-4}$, giving $0.0037766\times1.794\times10^{-4} = 6.8\times10^{-7}$ as the predicted error bound, and the actual error is $4.9\times10^{-7}$.

The series does not keep improving. Continuing the recursion, $|t_r|$ decreases to a minimum at $r = 10$, where $t_{10} = 1.127\times10^{-5}$, and increases from $r = 11$ onwards ($|t_{11}| = 1.129\times10^{-5}$, $|t_{12}| = 1.244\times10^{-5}$, $|t_{13}| = 1.496\times10^{-5}$). Truncating at the smallest term gives $0.0036911191$ against the exact $0.0036910983$, an error of $2.1\times10^{-8}$; truncating at $r = 13$ instead gives an error of $3.1\times10^{-8}$, worse. So at $z = 5$ the series can deliver between five and six correct significant figures and no more, however many terms are kept. That ceiling is the defining feature of an asymptotic series. Where it sits can be estimated: Stirling's approximation applied to $|t_r| = \bigl((2r-1)!!\bigr)^{2}/\bigl(r!\,(8z)^{r}\bigr)$ gives $|t_r| \approx C\,(r/2ez)^{r}$, which is smallest at $r \approx 2z$ with value of order $e^{-2z}$. At $z = 5$ that predicts a minimum near $r = 10$ of relative size $e^{-10} = 4.5\times10^{-5}$, and the computation above found it at $r = 10$ with relative size $1.1\times10^{-5}$.

All values are rounded from a 20-digit computation; the quoted digits are correct as shown.
:::

:::application[Radiation conditions, phase shifts, and the zeros of $J_n$]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§14.6, Example 14.6.1, p. 695, the discussion of the zeros and of the use of asymptotics in Wronskian formulas, pp. 693-694; arfken-2013:§14.4, the role of the Hankel functions as travelling waves, p. 678"}
**Picking the solution that radiates.** This is the reason the asymptotic forms exist in a physics text at all. In a two-dimensional wave problem with a source at the origin and nothing coming in from infinity, the physical requirement is $U \sim e^{i(kr - \omega t)}$ at large $r$. Of the solutions of Bessel's equation, only $H^{(1)}_0(kr)$ has that behaviour, by the large-argument statement; so the solution is $H^{(1)}_0(kr)e^{-i\omega t}$, and its divergence as $r \to 0$ — logarithmic, by the small-argument statement — is exactly what a point source should look like :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, Example 14.6.1, p. 695"}. The same argument in three dimensions, applied to $h^{(1)}_l$, is what makes :ref[]{to=spec.spherical-bessel} the right basis for radiation problems, and it is the content of the Sommerfeld radiation condition used in :ref[]{to=green.helmholtz-green-functions}.

**Phase shifts in scattering.** The entire phase-shift formalism rests on the leading large-argument form. A free radial wave behaves as $\sin(kr - l\pi/2)/kr$ at large $r$; a scattered one as $\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)/kr$. The potential is encoded in the single number $\delta_l$ per channel precisely because the asymptotic form has one free phase and one free amplitude, and conservation of probability fixes the amplitude. The $-l\pi/2$ that $\delta_l$ is measured against comes from the $\nu\pi/2$ in $\chi$ with $\nu = l+\tfrac12$.

**Where the zeros are.** Setting the leading term to zero predicts that the large zeros of $J_n$ are near the zeros of $\cos(x - n\pi/2 - \pi/4)$, that is near $x = (m + \tfrac{n}{2} + \tfrac34)\pi$, and that consecutive zeros are spaced by $\pi$. Comparing with a table shows the agreement is already good for the first few zeros when $n$ is small :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, p. 693"}. The same observation explains why the zeros of $J_n$ and $Y_n$ interlace at large argument: their leading terms are a cosine and a sine of the same phase. These estimates are the usual starting values for a Newton iteration when the zeros are needed accurately, as they are for the Fourier-Bessel expansions of :ref[]{to=spec.bessel-orthogonality}.

**Fixing Wronskian constants.** A Wronskian of two solutions of Bessel's equation is known to be $\mathrm{const}/x$ by Abel's identity, and the constant can be read off from the asymptotic forms as easily as from the behaviour at the origin — more easily, when one of the functions is singular there :cite[]{work=arfken-2013 loc="§14.6, p. 694"}. This is a standard use of the expansions that has nothing to do with large arguments as such.

**Exponential selection.** For the modified functions the asymptotic statement does the same job as the regularity condition does at the origin: in a problem on an unbounded domain, $I_\nu(x) \sim e^{x}/\sqrt{2\pi x}$ disqualifies $I_\nu$ immediately and leaves $K_\nu$. Screened potentials, cylindrical Green's functions and boundary layers all select their solution this way; see :ref[]{to=spec.modified-bessel}.
:::
