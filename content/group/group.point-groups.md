:::definition[Point groups and the operations they contain]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§17.4, 'Other Discrete Groups', p. 835; rhb-2006:§28.1, pp. 1041-1042"}
Fix a point of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and call it the origin. A **point group** is a group of distance-preserving transformations of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that all keep that point fixed. Such a transformation is linear and preserves the Euclidean inner product, so it is given by an orthogonal matrix (:ref[]{to=linalg.unitary-orthogonal-matrices}); a point group is therefore a subgroup of $\mathrm{O}(n)$, and the point group of a figure $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is
$$
G(F) = \{\, Q \in \mathrm{O}(n) : Q F = F \,\} .
$$
Here we consider only **finite** point groups. Arfken, Weber and Harris introduce them as the groups that keep a central point fixed, and note that they are the symmetry groups that arise when one studies properties depending on the shape of a molecule.

In three dimensions the elements are named by what they do:

- $C_n$, a **proper rotation** by $2\pi/n$ about an axis through the origin; $\det = +1$. The axis is called an $n$-fold axis.
- $\sigma$, a **reflection** in a plane through the origin; $\det = -1$.
- $i$, the **inversion** $x \mapsto -x$; in three dimensions $\det = -1$.
- $S_n$, an **improper rotation**: a rotation by $2\pi/n$ followed by the reflection in the plane perpendicular to its axis; $\det = -1$. Thus $S_1 = \sigma$ and $S_2 = i$.

Every element of **finite order** in $\mathrm{O}(3)$ is of one of these kinds. Indeed, the real canonical form of an orthogonal matrix (:ref[]{to=linalg.unitary-orthogonal-matrices}) puts it, in a suitable orthonormal frame, in the shape $\operatorname{diag}(\pm1, R(\theta))$; finite order forces $\theta$ to be a rational multiple of $2\pi$, say $\theta = 2\pi m/n$, and the two signs give $C_n$-type and $S_n$-type elements respectively. The rotations of a point group $G$ form the subgroup $G \cap \mathrm{SO}(3)$, of index $1$ or $2$ in $G$.

Two further notations are in use and we record both, since the literature mixes them. In **Schoenflies** notation the cyclic group generated by one $C_n$ is written $C_n$, the group generated by $C_n$ together with a perpendicular two-fold axis is $D_n$, and subscripts $v$, $h$ mark the addition of vertical or horizontal mirror planes. In **Hermann-Mauguin** (international, crystallographic) notation a group is named instead by its axes and mirrors written as numbers and the letter $m$: :cite{work=rhb-2006 loc="§29.11.3, p. 1110"} writes the symmetry group of the equilateral triangle as $3m$ and gives $C_{3v}$ as its chemical synonym, and names the class of its rotations $3_z$ against the chemists' $C_3$. We use the Schoenflies names and give the crystallographic one when a source does.
:::

:::statement[Finite point groups of the plane]{provenance=original}
Let $G \subseteq \mathrm{O}(2)$ be finite and of order $N$. Then exactly one of the following holds.

1. $G$ consists of rotations only. Then $G = C_n$ is cyclic of order $n = N$, generated by the rotation $R(2\pi/n)$ through the smallest positive angle occurring in $G$.
2. $G$ contains a reflection. Then $N = 2n$ is even, the rotations of $G$ form a cyclic group $C_n$, and $G$ consists of those $n$ rotations together with $n$ reflections in $n$ lines through the origin spaced by $\pi/n$. This group is the symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon centred at the origin (the **dihedral group**), for $n \geq 3$.

Every finite subgroup of $\mathrm{O}(2)$ is therefore a $C_n$ or a dihedral group of order $2n$.

Notation: following :cite{work=dummit-foote-2004 loc="§1.2, p. 24"} we write the dihedral group of the regular $n$-gon as $D_{2n}$, the subscript giving its order $2n$. Physics and chemistry texts write the same group as $D_n$, the subscript giving the order of the principal axis. We use $D_{2n}$ in this unit and say so wherever a count could be misread.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
A finite group of plane isometries fixing the origin is almost forced. Its rotations form a group of angles closed under addition and subtraction, and a *finite* such set of angles is the set of multiples of its smallest positive member: this is the same division-with-remainder argument that shows a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic. If a reflection is present as well, then composing it with each rotation produces the remaining elements and nothing else, because the product of two reflections is a rotation. So there are exactly as many reflections as rotations.
:::

:::proof{provenance=original}
By the classification of $2 \times 2$ real orthogonal matrices (:ref[]{to=linalg.unitary-orthogonal-matrices}), every element of $\mathrm{O}(2)$ is either a rotation $R(\theta)$ with $\det = +1$ or a reflection $F(\theta)$ in the line at angle $\theta/2$, with $\det = -1$.

**The rotations form a cyclic group.** Let $H = G \cap \mathrm{SO}(2)$, a subgroup of $G$ since the determinant is multiplicative. Let
$$
A = \{\, \theta \in \mathbb{R} : R(\theta) \in H \,\} .
$$
Because $R(\theta)R(\varphi) = R(\theta + \varphi)$ and $R(\theta)^{-1} = R(-\theta)$, the set $A$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ containing $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$. If $H = \{I\}$ put $n = 1$. Otherwise $A$ contains an angle that is not a multiple of $2\pi$, and since $G$ is **finite** the set $A \cap (0, 2\pi)$ is finite and non-empty; let $\alpha$ be its least element. Given any $\theta \in A$, divide: $\theta = m\alpha + \rho$ with $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $0 \le \rho < \alpha$. Then $\rho = \theta - m\alpha \in A$, and $\rho < \alpha$ forces $\rho = 0$ by minimality of $\alpha$. Hence $A = \alpha\mathbb{Z}$. Since $2\pi \in A$ we get $2\pi = n\alpha$ for some positive integer $n$, that is $\alpha = 2\pi/n$, and
$$
H = \{\, R(2\pi k/n) : k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1 \,\} ,
$$
cyclic of order $n$ generated by $R(2\pi/n)$. Finiteness was used twice: to know that a least positive angle exists, and to know that $n$ is finite.

**Case 1.** If $G$ contains no reflection then $G = H = C_n$ and $N = n$.

**Case 2.** Suppose $G$ contains a reflection $F_0$. The map $h \mapsto F_0 h$ is a bijection from $H$ onto the set $F_0 H$ of elements of determinant $-1$ in $G$, with inverse $g \mapsto F_0 g$ (using $F_0^2 = I$, which holds because a reflection is an involution). Conversely, if $g \in G$ has $\det g = -1$ then $F_0 g$ has determinant $+1$, so $F_0 g \in H$ and $g \in F_0 H$. Hence $G = H \sqcup F_0H$ and $N = 2n$. Writing $F_0 = F(\varphi)$ and using $F(\varphi)R(\theta) = F(\varphi - \theta)$ — both sides send the unit vector at angle $\psi$ to the unit vector at angle $\varphi - \theta - \psi$ — the $n$ reflections are $F(\varphi - 2\pi k/n)$, $k = 0, \dots, n-1$. Their mirror lines are at angles $(\varphi - 2\pi k/n)/2$, consecutive ones differing by $\pi/n$.

**It is the symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon.** Let $P$ be the regular $n$-gon with one vertex at angle $\varphi/2$. The rotations in $H$ permute its vertices cyclically and the reflection $F(\varphi)$ fixes the line through that vertex, so $G \subseteq G(P)$. Conversely a symmetry of $P$ permutes the $n$ vertices and is determined by the image of one vertex together with the choice of orientation, so $|G(P)| \le 2n = |G|$, by the count in :cite{work=dummit-foote-2004 loc="§1.2, p. 24"}. Hence $G = G(P)$. $\square$
:::

:::statement[The crystallographic restriction]{provenance=original}
Let $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ be a lattice of full rank, that is
$$
L = \mathbb{Z}a_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}a_d
$$
for some basis $a_1, \dots, a_d$ of $\mathbb{R}^d$, and let $Q \in \mathrm{O}(d)$ satisfy $QL = L$. Then:

1. The matrix $M$ of $Q$ in the basis $a_1, \dots, a_d$ has integer entries and $\det M = \pm 1$; in particular $\operatorname{tr} Q = \operatorname{tr} M \in \mathbb{Z}$.
2. **$d = 2$.** If $Q$ is a rotation of order $n$, then $n \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6\}$.
3. **$d = 3$.** If $Q$ is a proper rotation ($\det Q = +1$) of order $n$, then $n \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6\}$. The same list holds for an improper rotation $S_n$ with $n$ even, and $S_n$ with $n$ odd has order $2n$ with $n \in \{1,3\}$.

No lattice in two or three dimensions admits a rotation axis of order $5$, or of any order $\ge 7$. The point groups compatible with a lattice in three dimensions — the **crystallographic point groups** — are therefore only finitely many, and :cite{work=arfken-2013 loc="§17.10, p. 869"} records that there are exactly $32$ of them, compatible with the $14$ Bravais lattices and combining with translations into $230$ space groups.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
A symmetry of a lattice sends lattice points to lattice points, so in a basis of the lattice its matrix has integer entries — even though in Cartesian coordinates it is full of cosines. The trace does not care which basis is used. So the Cartesian trace, which is $2\cos\theta$ in the plane and $1 + 2\cos\theta$ in space, must be an integer; and $\cos\theta$ is confined to $[-1,1]$, so only five values of $\theta$ survive. The whole restriction is the statement that $2\cos\theta$ is an integer.
:::

:::proof{provenance=original}
**Part 1.** Each $a_j$ lies in $L$, so $Qa_j \in QL = L$ and $Qa_j$ is an integer combination of the basis: $Qa_j = \sum_i M_{ij} a_i$ with $M_{ij} \in \mathbb{Z}$. This is exactly the statement that the matrix of $Q$ in the basis $(a_i)$ is the integer matrix $M$. The same argument applied to $Q^{-1}$, which also maps $L$ onto $L$ because $Q L = L$ gives $L = Q^{-1}L$, produces an integer matrix $M'$ with $MM' = I$; taking determinants, $\det M \cdot \det M' = 1$ with both factors integers, so $\det M = \pm 1$. Finally, if $B$ is the matrix whose columns are the $a_j$ in Cartesian coordinates, then $Q = BMB^{-1}$, and the trace is invariant under conjugation, so $\operatorname{tr} Q = \operatorname{tr} M$, an integer. **This is the step that uses the full rank of $L$:** the $a_j$ must be a basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$ for $B$ to be invertible.

**Part 2 ($d = 2$).** A rotation of the plane by $\theta$ has Cartesian matrix $R(\theta)$ with $\operatorname{tr} R(\theta) = 2\cos\theta$. By part 1 this is an integer, and $|2\cos\theta| \le 2$, so
$$
2\cos\theta \in \{2,\ 1,\ 0,\ -1,\ -2\}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad
\theta \equiv \pm\theta_0 \pmod{2\pi}, \quad
\theta_0 \in \left\{0,\ \tfrac{\pi}{3},\ \tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \tfrac{2\pi}{3},\ \pi\right\},
$$
the two lists corresponding term by term. Write $Q = R(\alpha)$ with $\alpha$ one of these angles. The order of $R(\alpha)$ is the least $n \ge 1$ with $n\alpha \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, which for $\alpha = 0,\ \pm\tfrac{\pi}{3},\ \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \pm\tfrac{2\pi}{3},\ \pi$ is $1, 6, 4, 3, 2$ respectively. So $n \in \{1,2,3,4,6\}$.

**Part 3 ($d = 3$).** A proper rotation of $\mathbb{R}^3$ by $\theta$ about an axis has, in an orthonormal frame adapted to the axis, the matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1, R(\theta))$ (:ref[]{to=linalg.unitary-orthogonal-matrices}), so $\operatorname{tr}Q = 1 + 2\cos\theta$. By part 1 this is an integer, hence $2\cos\theta \in \mathbb{Z}$ again, and the five angles of part 2 are reproduced together with their orders: $n \in \{1,2,3,4,6\}$.

For an improper element, $S_n$ is the rotation by $2\pi/n$ about an axis followed by the reflection in the perpendicular plane, so in the adapted frame its matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(-1, R(2\pi/n))$ and $\operatorname{tr} S_n = -1 + 2\cos(2\pi/n)$. Part 1 again gives $2\cos(2\pi/n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ and the same five values of $n$. The order of $S_n$ as a group element is $n$ when $n$ is even (since $S_n^n = \operatorname{diag}((-1)^n, I) = I$) and $2n$ when $n$ is odd; with $n \in \{1,2,3,4,6\}$ the odd cases are $n = 1$ (the reflection $\sigma$, of order $2$) and $n = 3$ (of order $6$). $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used, and what fails without them]{provenance=original}
**The lattice is essential; the point group alone is not restricted.** Nothing in the definition of a finite point group forbids a five-fold axis: the regular pentagon has symmetry group $D_{10}$ (order $10$) in the plane, and the icosahedron has a point group containing six five-fold axes. Arfken, Weber and Harris make the same point by saying that a point group may have a single $n$-fold axis for any $n$, and that the restriction to the tetrahedral, octahedral and icosahedral cases concerns only groups with *several* axes of order at least three :cite{work=arfken-2013 loc="p. 835"}. The crystallographic restriction uses the hypothesis $QL = L$ at the very first line of the proof and nowhere else; drop it and the argument has no content.

**Full rank is essential.** Take $L = \mathbb{Z}(1,0,0) \subset \mathbb{R}^3$, a rank-one lattice in three-space, and let $Q$ be the rotation by $2\pi/5$ about the $x$-axis. Then $QL = L$, yet $Q$ has order $5$. The proof breaks at the point where the $a_j$ are used as a basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$: with one generator there is no such basis, and the Cartesian trace $1 + 2\cos(2\pi/5)$ is not forced to be an integer.

**The restriction bounds angles, not groups.** Part 2 and part 3 constrain each individual rotation. That there are then only $32$ crystallographic point groups in three dimensions, $14$ Bravais lattices and $230$ space groups is a much longer classification, which we quote from :cite{work=arfken-2013 loc="§17.10, p. 869"} and do not prove.

**Quasicrystals are not a counterexample.** A material whose diffraction pattern shows a sharp ten-fold symmetry is not periodic: it has no lattice $L$, so the theorem does not apply to it. The theorem is what made such patterns surprising when they were first seen.

**Arfken's tiling argument proves less than the trace argument.** Example 17.10.1 of :cite{work=arfken-2013 loc="p. 869"} argues in the plane that an integer number of identical regular polygons must meet at a vertex, which succeeds for the triangle, square and hexagon and fails for the pentagon. That rules out tilings by one regular polygon; it does not by itself rule out a five-fold *rotation axis* of a lattice whose unit cell is some other shape. The trace argument above does.
:::

:::method[Finding the point group of a molecule or a unit cell]{provenance=original}
1. **Place the origin** at the centroid of the figure. Every symmetry fixes the centroid, because an isometry of a finite set of equal masses permutes them and so preserves their mean.
2. **Find the proper rotations.** For each candidate axis (through two atoms, through an atom and the centroid, or through the midpoints of two bonds), find the largest $n$ with the rotation by $2\pi/n$ a symmetry. Record the axis of largest $n$ as the principal axis.
3. **Test the improper elements:** the inversion $x \mapsto -x$; the reflection $\sigma_h$ in the plane perpendicular to the principal axis; the reflections $\sigma_v$ in planes containing it; and the improper rotations $S_{2n}$ about it.
4. **Count.** Let $r$ be the number of proper rotations found, including the identity. If no improper element is a symmetry, $|G| = r$; otherwise $|G| = 2r$, by the coset argument used in Case 2 of the planar proof, which needs only that the determinant is a homomorphism onto $\{\pm 1\}$.
5. **For a crystal**, discard any axis of order other than $1, 2, 3, 4, 6$: by the crystallographic restriction it cannot be a symmetry of the lattice, so an apparent five-fold or seven-fold axis means either that the figure is not the unit cell of a lattice or that an error has been made.
6. **Sort the elements into conjugacy classes** (:ref[]{to=group.conjugacy-classes}), since those are what a character table is indexed by. Two rotations by the same angle about axes related by a symmetry of the figure are conjugate.
:::

:::example[The planar hexagonal lattice, and why five-fold symmetry is impossible]{provenance=original}
**Part A: the matrix of a six-fold rotation is an integer matrix.** Take the planar hexagonal (triangular) lattice $L = \mathbb{Z}a_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z}a_2$ with
$$
a_1 = (1, 0), \qquad a_2 = \left(-\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right),
$$
so $\|a_1\| = \|a_2\| = 1$ and the angle between them is $120^\circ$. Let $Q = R(\pi/3)$ be the rotation by $60^\circ$. In Cartesian coordinates
$$
Q a_1 = \left(\cos 60^\circ,\ \sin 60^\circ\right) = \left(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right),
$$
$$
Q a_2 = \left(-\tfrac12\cos 60^\circ - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\sin 60^\circ,\ -\tfrac12\sin 60^\circ + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cos 60^\circ\right)
= \left(-\tfrac14 - \tfrac34,\ -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\right) = (-1, 0) .
$$
Both are lattice vectors, since $(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) = a_1 + a_2$ and $(-1,0) = -a_1$. So $QL = L$, and in the basis $(a_1, a_2)$
$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$
an integer matrix, as part 1 of the restriction requires, with $\det M = 1\cdot 0 - (-1)\cdot 1 = +1$ and $\operatorname{tr} M = 1$. The Cartesian trace agrees: $2\cos 60^\circ = 1$. Its characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1$, whose roots $e^{\pm i\pi/3}$ are primitive sixth roots of unity, and indeed $M^6 = I$ while no smaller positive power is the identity. So $Q$ has order exactly $6$.

**Part B: the point group of this lattice.** The six rotations $R(k\pi/3)$, $k = 0, \dots, 5$, all preserve $L$, being powers of the $Q$ of Part A. So does the reflection $\sigma$ in the $x$-axis: $\sigma a_1 = a_1$ and $\sigma a_2 = (-\tfrac12, -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) = -(a_1 + a_2)$, both in $L$. There is nothing else. Indeed the rotation subgroup is some $C_n$ by the planar classification, it contains $R(\pi/3)$ so $6$ divides $n$, and the crystallographic restriction caps $n$ at $6$; hence $n = 6$ exactly, and the presence of $\sigma$ doubles the count by Case 2 of the planar classification. The group is dihedral of order
$$
|G| = 2 \times 6 = 12 ,
$$
that is $D_{12}$ in the order-subscript convention of this unit ($D_6$ or $6mm$ in the crystallographers' notation). This is the largest point group a planar lattice can have.

**Part C: five-fold symmetry is excluded.** Suppose some planar lattice $L'$ of full rank had a symmetry $Q'$ of order $5$. By part 1 its trace in a lattice basis is an integer; by part 2 that trace is $2\cos(2\pi/5)$. But
$$
2\cos\frac{2\pi}{5} = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2} = 0.6180339887\ldots ,
$$
which is irrational, hence not an integer. The contradiction shows no such $L'$ exists. The same number settles the three-dimensional case, where the trace would be $1 + 0.618\ldots = 1.618\ldots$, again not an integer. For contrast, $2\cos(2\pi/7) = 1.2469\ldots$ and $2\cos(2\pi/8) = \sqrt2 = 1.4142\ldots$ are likewise non-integers, so seven- and eight-fold axes are excluded too, exactly as the five values $\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$ of the proof predict.
:::

:::application{provenance=original}
**Reading a diffraction pattern.** X-ray, electron and neutron diffraction from a crystal produce a pattern whose symmetry is that of the crystal's point group, acting on the reciprocal lattice. Because the crystallographic restriction allows only $1,2,3,4,6$-fold axes, a diffraction photograph of a periodic solid can show only those rotational symmetries, and the observed symmetry immediately narrows the structure to one of the $32$ crystallographic point groups and then, with the systematic absences of reflections, to one of the $230$ space groups :cite{work=arfken-2013 loc="§17.10, p. 869"}. This is the first step of every structure determination, and it is a group-theoretic step: no intensity needs to be modelled to take it.

**Quasicrystals.** In 1982 Shechtman observed, in a rapidly cooled Al-Mn alloy, a sharp electron-diffraction pattern with ten-fold rotational symmetry. The crystallographic restriction says that a lattice cannot do this, so the sample could not be periodic, and the result was resisted for years on exactly that ground. The resolution was that the solid is ordered but aperiodic, built on a Penrose-type tiling; the theorem had not failed, its hypothesis $QL = L$ had. The episode is the clearest case in solid-state physics of a classification theorem forcing a physical conclusion.

**Selection rules and degeneracies.** The normal modes of a molecule, and the electronic states of an atom in a crystal field, organise themselves into irreducible representations of the point group, and the dimension of the irreducible representation is the degeneracy of the level (:ref[]{to=group.representations}, :ref[]{to=group.product-representations}). Whether a transition between two levels is allowed is decided by whether the product representation contains the identity representation, which is a finite calculation in the character table of the point group. Because a crystallographic point group has at most $48$ elements, these calculations are short, and they predict which infrared and Raman lines appear without any dynamical model of the molecule.

**Material tensors.** A physical property of a crystal expressed as a tensor — electrical conductivity, elasticity, piezoelectric coupling — must be invariant under every element of the point group. Each symmetry operation is a linear constraint on the tensor components, so the point group determines how many independent constants a material can have. The inversion $i$ is the most consequential single element: a tensor of odd rank changes sign under $x \mapsto -x$, so a centrosymmetric crystal has no piezoelectric effect and no second-harmonic generation at all. Knowing only the point group therefore rules out whole classes of devices for a given material, or selects it for them.
:::
