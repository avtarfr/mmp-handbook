:::definition[The Clifford algebra of a quadratic form]{provenance=adapted src="hassani-2013:Eq. (27.4), p. 830, Eqs. (27.14)–(27.16) and Theorem 27.2.1, p. 834, and Eqs. (27.40)–(27.41) with footnote 3, p. 846"}
Let $V$ be a real vector space of dimension $N$ carrying a symmetric bilinear form $g$. The **Clifford algebra** $\mathcal{C}_V$ is the associative algebra with unit $1$, generated by the vectors of $V$, in which multiplication — written by juxtaposition — obeys

$$
uv + vu = 2\,g(u,v)\,1 \qquad\text{for all } u, v \in V .
$$

Hassani reaches this relation by combining the wedge product with an interior product, and then observes that it is "sometimes taken as the definition"; we take it as the definition. Setting $u = v$ gives the equivalent form $v^2 = g(v,v)\,1$.

In an orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_N$, in which $g(e_i,e_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$ and $g(e_i,e_i) = \pm 1$, the relation becomes

$$
e_ie_j = -e_je_i \quad (i \neq j), \qquad e_i^2 = +1 \ \ (1 \le i \le \mu), \qquad e_i^2 = -1 \ \ (\mu < i \le N) ,
$$

where $\mu$ is the number of basis vectors of positive square and $\nu = N - \mu$ the number of negative square. The algebra determined by these relations is Hassani's $\mathcal{C}^{\nu}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})$; he lists $\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)$, $\mathcal{C}\ell_{\mu,\nu}(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mathcal{C}\ell_{p,q}(\mathbb{R})$ with $p = \mu$, $q = \nu$ as equally current notations, and we write $\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)$ throughout. **The order of the two indices is not standard between authors; here the first counts the $+1$s.**

Two pieces of structure come free. An element is **even** if it is a linear combination of products of an even number of basis vectors and **odd** otherwise; the even elements form a subalgebra $\mathcal{C}^0_V$, the odd ones only a subspace, and $\mathcal{C}_V = \mathcal{C}^0_V \oplus \mathcal{C}^1_V$. A **representation** of $\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)$ by matrices is a choice of square matrices $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$ with

$$
\gamma_i\gamma_j + \gamma_j\gamma_i = 2 g_{ij} I ;
$$

the matrices of such a representation are what physics calls **gamma matrices**, and a column vector on which they act is a **spinor**.
:::

:::statement[Dimension, basis, and the small cases]{provenance=adapted src="hassani-2013:Theorem 27.2.1 and Eq. (27.12), p. 834, Examples 27.4.1 and 27.4.2, pp. 846–847, Table 27.2 and Theorem 27.4.6, p. 851, and §27.4.3, p. 852"}
Let $e_1,\dots,e_N$ be an orthonormal basis of $(V,g)$ as above.

1. **(Basis.)** The $2^N$ ordered products

   $$
   1,\quad e_i,\quad e_ie_j \ (i<j),\quad e_ie_je_k \ (i<j<k),\quad \dots,\quad e_1e_2\cdots e_N
   $$

   form a basis of $\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)$ as a real vector space. In particular $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu) = 2^N$, and the even subalgebra has dimension $2^{N-1}$.
2. **(The small algebras.)** With $M_k(\mathbb{A})$ the $k\times k$ matrices over $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{H}$ the :ref[quaternions]{to=group.quaternions},

   | Algebra | $N$ | Isomorphic to | $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}$ |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | $\mathcal{C}(1,0)$ | $1$ | $\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$ (split complex numbers) | $2$ |
   | $\mathcal{C}(0,1)$ | $1$ | $\mathbb{C}$ | $2$ |
   | $\mathcal{C}(2,0)$ | $2$ | $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ | $4$ |
   | $\mathcal{C}(0,2)$ | $2$ | $\mathbb{H}$ | $4$ |
   | $\mathcal{C}(3,0)$ | $3$ | $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ — the **Pauli algebra** | $8$ |
   | $\mathcal{C}(0,3)$ | $3$ | $\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ | $8$ |
   | $\mathcal{C}(3,1)$ | $4$ | $M_4(\mathbb{R})$ | $16$ |

   Hassani's Examples 27.4.1 and 27.4.2 give three of these — $\mathcal{C}(1,0)$, $\mathcal{C}(0,1)$ and $\mathcal{C}(0,2)$ — and his §27.4.3 gives the last; $\mathcal{C}(2,0)$ together with $\mathcal{C}(0,2)$ is the $n=2$ row of his Table 27.2, p. 851, and the two three-dimensional entries are the $n=3$ row of the same table. Note that $\mathcal{C}(3,0)$ and $\mathcal{C}(0,3)$ are *not* isomorphic: the quadratic form, not merely the dimension of $V$, fixes the algebra.
3. **(Dirac's relation.)** Requiring that the relativistic energy–momentum relation $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2c^2 + m^2c^4$ factor into two first-order expressions forces the coefficients to obey

   $$
   (\gamma^0)^2 = 1, \qquad (\gamma^i)^2 = -1 \ (i = 1,2,3), \qquad \gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 0 \ (\mu \neq \nu) ,
   $$

   which is Arfken, Weber and Harris's Eqs. (2.74)–(2.75); equivalently $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}I$ with $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. No numbers satisfy this; the smallest matrices that do are $4\times 4$, and $\psi$ must then be a four-component column.
4. **(Pin and Spin.)** Inside the group of invertible elements of $\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)$ sit the groups $\mathrm{Pin}(\mu,\nu)$ and its even part $\mathrm{Spin}(\mu,\nu) = \mathrm{Pin}(\mu,\nu)\cap\mathcal{C}^0$, which act on $V$ by twisted conjugation $\tau_a$. Hassani's Theorem 31.2.3 states that $a \mapsto \tau_a$ is a surjective homomorphism $\mathrm{Pin}(\mu,\nu)\to\mathrm{O}(\mu,\nu)$ with kernel $\{1,-1\}$, and his Theorem 31.2.6 that its restriction $\mathrm{Spin}(\mu,\nu)\to\mathrm{SO}(\mu,\nu)$ is also surjective with kernel $\{1,-1\}$. Each rotation therefore comes from exactly two elements of $\mathrm{Spin}$, differing by a sign.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
Everything follows from one move: *the relation lets you push any two basis vectors past each other at the cost of a sign, and lets you delete a repeated pair at the cost of a sign.* So any product of generators collapses to $\pm$ an ordered product with distinct indices, of which there are $2^N$ — one for each subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$. That is the basis theorem, and it also explains why the small algebras are the familiar ones: a two-dimensional $V$ with both squares $-1$ gives four basis elements $1, e_1, e_2, e_1e_2$ whose multiplication table is forced, and it is the quaternion table.

Dirac's relation comes from the same move read backwards. He wanted $\bigl(\beta E + \sum\alpha_ip_i\bigr)$ to square to $E^2 - \mathbf{p}^2$ (with the mass carried along); squaring produces cross terms $\alpha_i\alpha_j + \alpha_j\alpha_i$, and the only way for them to disappear is for the coefficients to anticommute. That is the Clifford relation, so the coefficients cannot be numbers; the smallest matrices that work give four-component spinors.

Spinors themselves are not an extra construction. Once the algebra is a matrix algebra, the columns of a matrix are already a space the algebra acts on from the left, and that space is a minimal left ideal: the smallest non-zero subspace closed under multiplication by the algebra. That is what a spinor space is.
:::

:::proof[Part 1: the $2^N$ ordered products span, and the quaternion and Pauli cases]{provenance=adapted src="hassani-2013:Theorem 27.2.1, p. 834, Example 27.4.2, p. 847, and §31.2.1, pp. 997–998"}
**Step 1 (reduction to ordered products).** Any element of the algebra is, by definition, a linear combination of finite products $e_{i_1}e_{i_2}\cdots e_{i_p}$. If two adjacent factors have different indices, $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$ exchanges them at the cost of a sign; repeating this bubble-sorts the word into non-decreasing index order. If two adjacent factors are then equal, $e_i^2 = \pm 1$ deletes both at the cost of a sign. Iterating, every word reduces to $\pm$ a product $e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_q}$ with $i_1 < \dots < i_q$. There is one such product for each subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$, so the $2^N$ listed elements span. That they are also linearly independent — so that the algebra is exactly $2^N$-dimensional and not smaller — is Hassani's Theorem 27.2.1, which we quote. For the two cases used below, independence is visible directly, because the spanning elements are exhibited as linearly independent matrices.

**Step 2 ($\mathcal{C}(0,2) \cong \mathbb{H}$).** Here $N = 2$ with $e_1^2 = e_2^2 = -1$. Write $a = e_1$, $b = e_2$, $c = e_1e_2$. Then

$$
c^2 = e_1e_2e_1e_2 = -e_1e_1e_2e_2 = -(-1)(-1) = -1 ,
$$

using the anticommutation once and then both squares; and

$$
ab = c, \qquad bc = e_2e_1e_2 = -e_1e_2e_2 = e_1 = a, \qquad ca = e_1e_2e_1 = -e_1e_1e_2 = e_2 = b .
$$

So $a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = -1$ with $ab = c$, $bc = a$, $ca = b$, which is exactly the multiplication table of the quaternion units $i, j, k$ in :ref[quaternions]{to=group.quaternions}. The four elements $1, a, b, c$ therefore span a copy of $\mathbb{H}$, and by Step 1 they span everything: $\mathcal{C}(0,2) \cong \mathbb{H}$. This is Hassani's Example 27.4.2.

**Step 3 ($\mathcal{C}(3,0) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$).** Here $N = 3$ with all three squares $+1$. The Pauli matrices

$$
\sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
$$

satisfy $\sigma_i^2 = I$ and $\sigma_i\sigma_j = -\sigma_j\sigma_i$ for $i \neq j$ — Arfken, Weber and Harris's Eq. (2.59) — that is, $\sigma_i\sigma_j + \sigma_j\sigma_i = 2\delta_{ij}I$. So $e_i \mapsto \sigma_i$ is a representation. It is onto: the eight products of Step 1 map to

$$
I,\quad \sigma_1,\ \sigma_2,\ \sigma_3,\quad \sigma_1\sigma_2 = i\sigma_3,\ \ \sigma_1\sigma_3 = -i\sigma_2,\ \ \sigma_2\sigma_3 = i\sigma_1,\quad \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = iI ,
$$

which, up to sign, is the list $\{I, \sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3, iI, i\sigma_1,i\sigma_2,i\sigma_3\}$. These eight matrices are linearly independent over $\mathbb{R}$. To see it, let $c_k$ be the coefficient of $\sigma_k$ and $d_k$ that of $i\sigma_k$ (with $\sigma_0 = I$), and read off the four entries of the combination: their real and imaginary parts are $c_0 \pm c_3$, $d_0 \pm d_3$, $c_1 \pm d_2$ and $d_1 \mp c_2$. Every coefficient occurs in two of these eight numbers, once with each sign, so the eight can vanish together only if all eight coefficients vanish. Hassani leaves the same verification to his reader. They are therefore a real basis of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, which has real dimension $8 = 2^3$. Hence $\mathcal{C}(3,0) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, with the eight-element real basis above.

**Step 4 (the even part of the Pauli algebra is $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ exponentiated).** From $\sigma_i\sigma_j + \sigma_j\sigma_i = 2\delta_{ij}I$ one gets, for $j \neq k$, $[\sigma_j,\sigma_k] = 2\sigma_j\sigma_k$, and the products computed in Step 3 give $[\sigma_j,\sigma_k] = 2i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$. Putting $s_j = -i\sigma_j/2$ turns this into

$$
[s_j, s_k] = \epsilon_{jkl}s_l ,
$$

Hassani's Eq. (31.11). The $s_j$ are traceless and anti-Hermitian, hence a basis of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, and under the identification of Step 3 they are the three bivectors up to a factor: $e_1e_2 = i\sigma_3 = -2s_3$, and likewise for the cyclic companions. The even subalgebra of $\mathcal{C}(3,0)$ is four-dimensional, spanned by $1$ and the three bivectors $e_1e_2$, $e_2e_3$, $e_3e_1$; each bivector squares to $-1$ (by the computation of Step 2, which used only $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$ and $e_i^2 = \pm1$ with the two signs cancelling), and any two distinct bivectors anticommute, for instance $(e_1e_2)(e_2e_3) = e_1e_3 = -(e_2e_3)(e_1e_2)$. Up to a choice of signs these are the quaternion relations, so $\mathcal{C}^0(3,0) \cong \mathbb{H}$, and its elements of unit norm form $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ (see :ref[quaternions]{to=group.quaternions} and :ref[the rotation group and SU(2)]{to=group.so3-su2}). $\square$
:::

:::proof[Part 2: Dirac's conditions]{provenance=adapted src="hassani-2013:§27.1.1, Eqs. (27.9)–(27.11), pp. 832–834; arfken-2013:§2.2, Eqs. (2.60)–(2.75), pp. 110–113"}
Work in units with $\hbar = c = 1$, as Hassani does, so the relation to be reproduced is $E^2 - \mathbf{p}^2 - m^2 = 0$.

**Step 1 (what is being demanded).** Dirac looked for a first-order equation $\bigl(\beta E + \sum_{i}\alpha_ip_i + m\bigr)\psi = 0$ whose multiplication by the conjugate factor $\bigl(\beta E + \sum_j\alpha_jp_j - m\bigr)$ returns $\bigl(E^2 - \sum_i p_i^2 - m^2\bigr)\psi = 0$. Here $E$ and $p_i$ are treated as commuting operators and $\beta,\alpha_i$ are unknown quantities, not assumed to be numbers.

**Step 2 (multiply out).** Expanding the product, four cross terms cancel in pairs — $\pm m\beta E$ and $\pm m\sum_i\alpha_ip_i$, the mass having opposite signs in the two factors — and the result is

$$
\beta^2E^2 + \sum_i (\beta\alpha_i + \alpha_i\beta)Ep_i + \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}(\alpha_i\alpha_j + \alpha_j\alpha_i)\,p_ip_j - m^2 ,
$$

the symmetrisation in the third term being legitimate because $p_ip_j = p_jp_i$.

**Step 3 (match coefficients).** Comparing with $E^2 - \sum_i p_i^2 - m^2$ term by term:

- $\beta^2 = 1$;
- $\beta\alpha_i + \alpha_i\beta = 0$ for each $i$, since no $Ep_i$ term is wanted;
- $\alpha_i\alpha_j + \alpha_j\alpha_i = -2\delta_{ij}$, since the coefficient of $p_i^2$ must be $-1$ and no $p_ip_j$ cross terms are wanted.

**Step 4 (this is a Clifford algebra).** Rename $\beta = \gamma^0$ and $\alpha_i = \gamma^i$. The three conditions read $(\gamma^0)^2 = 1$, $(\gamma^i)^2 = -1$, and $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 0$ for $\mu \neq \nu$ — exactly Arfken, Weber and Harris's Eqs. (2.74)–(2.75) — which is the single relation

$$
\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}I, \qquad \eta = \operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1) .
$$

Hassani's remark is that this *is* the defining relation of a Clifford algebra: it is his Eq. (27.4) with $g = \eta$. The algebra is $\mathcal{C}(1,3)$ in the convention of the definition above.

**Step 5 (why matrices, and how big).** Numbers commute, so no four numbers can satisfy $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu = -\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu$ with all of them non-zero; matrices must be used. Arfken, Weber and Harris reach the explicit $4\times4$ set by the direct-product construction of their Eqs. (2.68)–(2.72), starting from the identity $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf{p})^2 = \mathbf{p}^2I_2$, which is itself the Pauli case of Step 3 above; one such set is

$$
\gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix}I_2 & 0\\ 0 & -I_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma^i = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \sigma_i\\ -\sigma_i & 0\end{pmatrix} \quad (i = 1,2,3) ,
$$

and one checks the relation block by block: $(\gamma^0)^2 = I_4$; $(\gamma^i)^2 = \operatorname{diag}(-\sigma_i^2, -\sigma_i^2) = -I_4$; $\gamma^0\gamma^i + \gamma^i\gamma^0 = 0$ because the off-diagonal blocks acquire opposite signs; and for $i \neq j$, $\gamma^i\gamma^j + \gamma^j\gamma^i = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(-(\sigma_i\sigma_j+\sigma_j\sigma_i),\,-(\sigma_i\sigma_j+\sigma_j\sigma_i)\bigr) = 0$. Since $\psi$ must be a column the $\gamma^\mu$ can act on, it has four components, two of which were eventually identified with the electron's two spin states and two with the positron's. $\square$
:::

:::remark{provenance=original}
**Where the hypotheses are used, which conventions clash, and what the two books are counting.**

- *Only the symmetry of $g$ and the invertibility of $2$ are used in the basis theorem.* Step 1 needs $uv + vu = 2g(u,v)$ with $g$ symmetric, and an orthonormal basis, which exists for any symmetric real form by Sylvester's law of inertia. Degeneracy is harmless there: if $g(e_i,e_i) = 0$ for some $i$ the relations still reduce every word, and one gets an exterior algebra in those directions. Non-degeneracy *is* needed for the classification in the table and for the theory of $\mathrm{Pin}$ and $\mathrm{Spin}$, because twisted conjugation by $a$ is built from $a^{-1}$ and the reflection formula.
- *The signature, not the dimension, fixes the algebra.* $\mathcal{C}(3,0) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple, while $\mathcal{C}(0,3) \cong \mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ is not even indecomposable: it has two non-trivial two-sided ideals. Both are built on $V = \mathbb{R}^3$ and both have dimension $8$. Anyone quoting "the Clifford algebra of three-space" must say which sign convention is in force, and the same applies to the Dirac algebra: with $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ one gets $\mathcal{C}(1,3)$, while with the opposite overall sign one gets $\mathcal{C}(3,1) \cong M_4(\mathbb{R})$, the algebra Hassani works out in §27.4.3. These two are not isomorphic as real algebras, though their complexifications are.
- *Two different things are both called "the dimension" of the Dirac algebra, and both equal 16.* Arfken, Weber and Harris write that the Pauli matrices with $1_2$ form a Clifford algebra "of dimension 4", counting the four linearly independent $2\times 2$ matrices *over $\mathbb{C}$*; and that the Dirac matrices belong to a Clifford algebra of dimension $16$, with the complete basis $1_4$, $\gamma^5 = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$, the four $\gamma^\mu$, the four $\gamma^5\gamma^\mu$ and the six $\sigma^{\mu\nu} = i\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu$, which is a basis of $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ over $\mathbb{C}$. Hassani's count is of the *real* algebra: $2^N$, which is $8$ for $N=3$ and $16$ for $N=4$. For $N=3$ the two counts disagree ($4$ complex versus $8$ real) and both are right, because $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has complex dimension $4$ and real dimension $8$. For $N=4$ they agree numerically at $16$ while still counting different things: Arfken, Weber and Harris's sixteen matrices include explicit factors of $i$, so they are a *complex* basis of $M_4(\mathbb{C})$, whereas Hassani's sixteen ordered products are a *real* basis of $\mathcal{C}(1,3)$. The coincidence of the two numbers at $N=4$ is an accident of the counting and should not be read as the same statement.
- *The chain $\mathrm{Spin}(3,0) \cong \mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$ is not to be read literally, and Hassani says so.* His Theorem 31.2.6 states that $\Psi : \mathrm{Spin}(\mu,\nu)\to\mathrm{SO}(\mu,\nu)$ is surjective with kernel $\{1,-1\}$ — a two-to-one map, not an isomorphism. Two pages later, summarising the Pauli case, he writes the chain of isomorphism signs above, introducing it with "there seems to be the following chain", and says that its first link confirms the theorem. The tension is only apparent, and he resolves it himself: a footnote hung on that sentence (footnote 3, p. 999) says the first link is not exactly an isomorphism but a homomorphism that is locally, though not globally, one, and that the double-valued kernel lets one picture $\mathrm{Spin}(3,0)$ as two copies of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. So the book is consistent, and what it asserts is the theorem: $\mathrm{Spin}(3,0) \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$, with the map onto $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ two-to-one with kernel $\{\pm 1\}$. That is also what :ref[the rotation group and SU(2)]{to=group.so3-su2} establishes independently, and it is what this unit uses; the displayed chain is shorthand, and the footnote is the statement to quote.
- *What the kernel $\{\pm1\}$ costs.* Because $a$ and $-a$ give the same rotation, a spinor cannot be recovered from the rotation it is transported by: carrying a spinor once around a full $2\pi$ rotation returns $-\psi$, and only $4\pi$ returns $\psi$. The example below makes this explicit — the rotor for angle $\theta$ contains $\theta/2$.
- *Spinors are not tensors.* Arfken, Weber and Harris say it in §4.1 and Riley, Hobson and Bence repeat it in a footnote: a spinor has none of the transformation properties of a scalar, a vector or a tensor of any rank, and that is precisely because its transformation law is a representation of $\mathrm{Spin}$ rather than of $\mathrm{SO}$.
:::

:::method[Building a gamma-matrix representation in any even dimension]{provenance=original}
To represent $\mathcal{C}(2k,0)$ — equivalently, to produce $2k$ anticommuting matrices squaring to $+I$ — use the tensor-product ladder:

1. **Fix the size.** The matrices will be $2^k \times 2^k$, acting on $(\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes k}$: one Pauli factor for each pair of directions.
2. **Write the two families.** For $a = 1,\dots,k$ set

   $$
   \gamma_{2a-1} = \underbrace{\sigma_3\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_3}_{a-1}\otimes\,\sigma_1\,\otimes \underbrace{I\otimes\cdots\otimes I}_{k-a}, \qquad
   \gamma_{2a} = \underbrace{\sigma_3\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_3}_{a-1}\otimes\,\sigma_2\,\otimes \underbrace{I\otimes\cdots\otimes I}_{k-a} .
   $$

3. **Why it works.** In the same slot $a$, the two matrices differ by $\sigma_1$ versus $\sigma_2$, which anticommute. In different slots $a < b$, the matrix from slot $b$ carries a $\sigma_3$ in slot $a$ where the matrix from slot $a$ carries $\sigma_1$ or $\sigma_2$; those anticommute, and every other slot contributes a commuting pair. One anticommuting pair in the tensor product makes the whole product anticommute. Each $\gamma$ squares to $I$ because every factor does.
4. **Change the signature.** To make $\gamma_i^2 = -I$, multiply that $\gamma_i$ by $i$; this preserves all the anticommutation relations. So $\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)$ with $\mu+\nu = 2k$ is represented inside the same matrix algebra.
5. **Add the chirality element in odd dimensions.** The product $\gamma_{2k+1} = (-i)^{k}\gamma_1\gamma_2\cdots\gamma_{2k}$ anticommutes with all of the others — it is a product of an even number $2k$ of them, and moving one $\gamma_i$ through the other $2k-1$ factors costs $(-1)^{2k-1} = -1$ — and squares to $I$, giving a representation of $\mathcal{C}(2k+1,0)$ of the same size. Verified numerically for $k = 1,2,3$. For $k=2$ this is the analogue of the Dirac matrix $\gamma^5$, which Arfken, Weber and Harris define as $i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$ for their own signature.
6. **Check.** Verify $\gamma_i\gamma_j + \gamma_j\gamma_i = 2g_{ij}I$ for all $i \le j$; with $2k$ matrices that is $k(2k+1)$ identities, so $3$, $10$ and $21$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$. Done numerically for $k = 1,2,3$ — $2$, $4$ and $6$ matrices of sizes $2$, $4$ and $8$ — all relations hold to machine precision.
:::

:::example[A rotor and a Dirac trace]{provenance=original}
**(a) A rotor, with a number at the end.** Work in the Pauli algebra $\mathcal{C}(3,0)$, representing $e_i$ by $\sigma_i$ as in Step 3. Let $B = e_1e_2$, so $B^2 = -1$ by the computation in Step 2 (the sign there did not use the squares being $-1$: $e_1e_2e_1e_2 = -e_1^2e_2^2 = -1$ here as well). Since $B^2 = -1$, the exponential series splits exactly as for a complex exponential:

$$
R(\theta) = \exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\theta}{2}B\Bigr) = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} - \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,B , \qquad R(\theta)^{-1} = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} + \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,B .
$$

Write $c = \cos(\theta/2)$, $s = \sin(\theta/2)$. Using $Be_1 = e_1e_2e_1 = -e_2$, $e_1B = e_1e_1e_2 = e_2$ and $Be_1B = -e_2e_1e_2 = e_1$,

$$
R e_1 R^{-1} = (c - sB)e_1(c + sB) = c^2e_1 + cs\,e_2 + sc\,e_2 - s^2e_1 = (c^2-s^2)e_1 + 2cs\,e_2 = \cos\theta\,e_1 + \sin\theta\,e_2 ,
$$

by the double-angle formulas; the same computation gives $Re_2R^{-1} = -\sin\theta\,e_1 + \cos\theta\,e_2$ and $Re_3R^{-1} = e_3$, since $e_3$ commutes with $B$. So $R(\theta)$ implements the rotation by $\theta$ about the $e_3$ axis — and it does so through the *half*-angle, so $R(2\pi) = -1$ while the rotation it induces is the identity. That is the kernel $\{\pm1\}$ of statement 4, made concrete.

Now take $\theta = 60^{\circ} = \pi/3$ and $v = e_1 + 2e_2 + 3e_3$. Then

$$
RvR^{-1} = \bigl(\tfrac12 - \sqrt3\bigr)e_1 + \bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + 1\bigr)e_2 + 3e_3 = -1.2320508\,e_1 + 1.8660254\,e_2 + 3\,e_3 ,
$$

and the length is preserved:

$$
(-1.2320508)^2 + (1.8660254)^2 + 3^2 = 1.5179492 + 3.4820508 + 9 = 14 = 1^2+2^2+3^2 .
$$

Carrying the whole calculation out with the $2\times2$ matrices — forming $R = \cos 30^{\circ}\,I - \sin 30^{\circ}\,\sigma_1\sigma_2$, computing $R(\sigma_1 + 2\sigma_2 + 3\sigma_3)R^{-1}$, and extracting the coefficients as $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\,\cdot\,\sigma_i)$ — returns the same three numbers to machine precision.

**(b) A Dirac trace.** With the explicit $\gamma^\mu$ of Part 2, Step 5, the Clifford relation alone gives

$$
\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^1\gamma^2 = -\gamma^1\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^2 = -(-I)(-I) = -I, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^1\gamma^2\bigr) = -4 .
$$

This agrees with the standard four-gamma trace identity $\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma) = 4(\eta^{\mu\nu}\eta^{\rho\sigma} - \eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma} + \eta^{\mu\sigma}\eta^{\nu\rho})$, which at $(\mu,\nu,\rho,\sigma) = (1,2,1,2)$ gives $4(0 - (-1)(-1) + 0) = -4$. Evaluating all $256$ traces $\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma)$ numerically from the explicit matrices and comparing with the identity gives no mismatches, and $\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3) = 0$ as the identity requires.

:::

:::example[A spinor as a column]{provenance=adapted src="hassani-2013:§31.2.1, p. 999"}
**(c)** This one follows Hassani's construction of the Pauli spinors inside the algebra. In $\mathcal{C}(3,0) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, set $P = \tfrac12(1 + e_3)$. Then, since $e_3^2 = 1$,

$$
P^2 = \tfrac14(1 + 2e_3 + e_3^2) = \tfrac14(2 + 2e_3) = P ,
$$

so $P$ is an idempotent; in the Pauli representation it is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. The left ideal $\mathcal{C}(3,0)\,P$ consists of all matrices whose second column is zero, which is a copy of $\mathbb{C}^2$ and is minimal. For suppose a non-zero subspace of it is closed under left multiplication, and take an element of that subspace with a non-zero entry $z$ in row $r$. Left multiplication by the matrix unit $E_{sr}$ carries that entry into row $s$, and left multiplication by the scalar $z^{-1}1$ normalises it — a *complex* scalar is available because $i\,1 = e_1e_2e_3$ lies in the algebra, by Step 3, so a subspace invariant under the real algebra is automatically a complex subspace. The subspace therefore contains both $E_{11}$ and $E_{21}$, hence all of $\mathcal{C}(3,0)\,P$. Hassani reaches the same conclusion along a shorter route, citing his Theorem 3.3.1: the minimal left ideals of a total matrix algebra are the matrices with a single non-zero column. He identifies this left ideal as the space of Pauli spinors, the two-component complex columns on which the Pauli matrices act, and obtains a basis of it by left-multiplying $P$ by the basis elements of the algebra, recording four matrices — a basis of $S$ over $\mathbb{R}$, as his dimension count requires, since $S \cong \mathbb{C}^2$ has real dimension four. Over $\mathbb{C}$ two of them suffice: $P = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $e_1P = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, whose non-zero columns are $(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$ and $(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$, the spin-up and spin-down states.
:::

:::application{provenance=original}
**The electron.** The Dirac equation $\bigl(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m\bigr)\psi = 0$ is the first-order equation Part 2 was constructing, and its four-component solution is the relativistic electron. Two of the four components became the two spin states — spin, as Hassani puts it, came out of the equation rather than being put in — and the other two were recognised only later as the positron's. That the gamma matrices must be $4\times4$ and not $2\times2$, and that $\psi$ is therefore a four-component object, is not a modelling choice: it is forced by the Clifford relation, as Step 5 shows.

**Spin-$\tfrac12$ in non-relativistic quantum mechanics.** The Pauli algebra of example (a) is the whole apparatus of spin-$\tfrac12$: the states are the two-component spinors of example (c), the observables are the $\sigma_i$, and the rotation of a spin state by angle $\theta$ about an axis is the rotor $\exp(-\tfrac{\theta}{2}B)$. The half-angle is directly observable — in neutron interferometry a beam rotated through $2\pi$ in a magnetic field returns with its sign reversed and interferes destructively with the unrotated beam, which is the kernel $\{\pm1\}$ measured in a laboratory.

**Rotations in computation.** Because $\mathcal{C}(0,2) \cong \mathbb{H}$ and the even part of $\mathcal{C}(3,0)$ gives $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, the rotor formula $v \mapsto RvR^{-1}$ of example (a) is exactly the unit-quaternion representation of rotations used in computer graphics, robotics and spacecraft attitude control. It is preferred to Euler angles because composing rotations is a single product, interpolation between orientations is smooth, and there is no gimbal lock; the price is the two-to-one redundancy, which shows up as the need to choose a sign when converting a rotation matrix back to a quaternion.

**Trace technology.** In quantum field theory a cross-section is an average over spins of $|\mathcal{M}|^2$, and the spin sums turn the amplitude into traces of products of gamma matrices. Identities such as the one checked in example (b) reduce those traces to metric tensors, so that a calculation that looks like a $4\times4$ matrix product is carried out purely with the Clifford relation and never needs an explicit representation. That independence of representation is the practical payoff of defining the algebra by generators and relations rather than by matrices.
:::
