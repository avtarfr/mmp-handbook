:::definition[Matrix Lie group]{provenance=adapted src="lee-2013:Thm 20.12, p. 523, and Prop. 20.9, p. 521"}
Write $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ for the complex $n\times n$ matrices and
$$
GL(n,\mathbb{C}) = \{\, A \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) : \det A \neq 0 \,\}
$$
for the **general linear group**, a group under matrix multiplication (:ref[determinants]{to=linalg.determinants}). A **matrix Lie group** is a subgroup $G \subseteq GL(n,\mathbb{C})$ that is a *closed* subset of $GL(n,\mathbb{C})$: if $A_k \in G$ and $A_k \to A$ with $A$ invertible, then $A \in G$.

The word "closed" is a condition inside $GL(n,\mathbb{C})$, not inside $M_n(\mathbb{C})$. A sequence in $G$ is allowed to run off to a singular limit; what is forbidden is an invertible limit outside $G$.

This definition is a shortcut. In the general theory a Lie group is a group that is also a smooth manifold with smooth multiplication and inversion, and the point of :cite[Lee's]{work=lee-2013 loc="Thm 20.12, p. 523"} **closed subgroup theorem** is that a subgroup of a Lie group which is merely a closed subset is automatically an embedded Lie subgroup. Taking the conclusion as the definition costs us nothing in this chapter and spares us the manifold apparatus.
:::

:::definition[Lie algebra, and the Lie algebra of a matrix group]{provenance=adapted src="lee-2013:p. 190, Example 8.36(c), and Prop. 20.9, p. 521"}
A **Lie algebra** over $\mathbb{R}$ is a real vector space $\mathfrak{g}$ carrying a map $\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}$, written $(X,Y)\mapsto[X,Y]$ and called the **bracket**, such that for all $X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{g}$ and all $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$:

1. **bilinearity**: $[aX+bY,Z] = a[X,Z]+b[Y,Z]$ and $[Z,aX+bY] = a[Z,X]+b[Z,Y]$;
2. **antisymmetry**: $[X,Y] = -[Y,X]$;
3. **the Jacobi identity**: $[X,[Y,Z]] + [Y,[Z,X]] + [Z,[X,Y]] = 0$.

The bracket is not associative; the Jacobi identity is what stands in its place. A **Lie subalgebra** is a linear subspace closed under the bracket, and a **Lie algebra homomorphism** is a linear map $\phi$ with $\phi[X,Y] = [\phi X,\phi Y]$.

The basic example is $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ — or $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ — with the **commutator**
$$
[A,B] = AB - BA .
$$
Bilinearity and antisymmetry are immediate; the Jacobi identity is checked by expanding the six products, each of which occurs twice with opposite signs. Regarded this way the matrices are written $\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{C})$, in lower-case fraktur, and *as a real Lie algebra* $\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{C})$ has dimension $2n^2$.

For a matrix Lie group $G \subseteq GL(n,\mathbb{C})$, the **Lie algebra of $G$** is
$$
\mathfrak{g} \;=\; \{\, X \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) : e^{tX} \in G \ \text{ for every } t \in \mathbb{R} \,\} ,
$$
where $e^{X} = \sum_{k\ge0}X^k/k!$ is the :ref[matrix exponential]{to=linalg.matrix-exponential}. The elements of $\mathfrak{g}$ are the **generators** (in the mathematicians' normalisation; see the convention note below), and the curve $t\mapsto e^{tX}$ is the **one-parameter subgroup** generated by $X$. The requirement is for *all* real $t$, not merely for small $t$: that is what makes the set a vector space.
:::

:::note[Two sign conventions, and which one we use where]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:Eqs. (17.35)–(17.36) and (17.43), pp. 846, 848; hassani-2013:Def. 29.1.28, p. 927"}
Mathematics and physics write the same object with different factors of $i$, and mixing them silently is the commonest source of sign errors in this subject.

- **Mathematicians' convention** (:cite[Lee]{work=lee-2013 loc="Prop. 20.9, p. 521"}, :cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="Def. 29.1.28, p. 927"}): group elements are $e^{X}$ with $X\in\mathfrak{g}$, and the structure constants are defined by $[\xi_i,\xi_j] = \sum_k c_{ij}^{\,k}\,\xi_k$ with no $i$. For a unitary group the $X$ are anti-Hermitian.
- **Physicists' convention** (:cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris]{work=arfken-2013 loc="Eqs. (17.35)–(17.36), p. 846"}): group elements are $U(\varphi) = \exp(i\varphi S)$ with $S$ **Hermitian**, obtained from an infinitesimal element $U(\delta\varphi) = 1 + i\,\delta\varphi\,S$ and the limit $U(\varphi) = \lim_{N\to\infty}(1+i\varphi S/N)^N$. The structure constants then carry an $i$:
$$
[S_j,S_k] = i\sum_l f_{jkl}\,S_l .
$$

The two are related by $X = iS$, and the structure constants by $c = i f$ up to the index ordering. We use the mathematicians' form in this unit, because $\mathfrak{g}$ is then a *real* vector space closed under the commutator and the statements below need no qualification; from :ref[the rotation group and SU(2)]{to=group.so3-su2} onwards we switch to the physicists' form, because the Hermitian $S$ are the observables and their eigenvalues are the quantum numbers. The two conventions are flagged again at each switch.
:::

:::statement[The Lie algebra of a matrix Lie group]{provenance=adapted src="lee-2013:Prop. 20.2, pp. 517–518, Prop. 20.8, pp. 519–521, Prop. 20.9, p. 521, Cor. 20.11, p. 523; hassani-2013:Thm 29.1.21, p. 925"}
Let $G\subseteq GL(n,\mathbb{C})$ be a matrix Lie group and $\mathfrak{g}$ the set defined above. Then:

1. **$\mathfrak{g}$ is a real Lie algebra.** It is a real linear subspace of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$, closed under $[\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,]$, and closed as a subset of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$.
2. **The exponential map lands in $G$ and is a local chart.** $\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G$, $X\mapsto e^{X}$, is smooth, satisfies $e^{(s+t)X}=e^{sX}e^{tX}$, $(e^{X})^{-1}=e^{-X}$, and restricts to a diffeomorphism of a neighbourhood of $0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ onto a neighbourhood of $I$ in $G$. In particular $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{g}$ is the number of real parameters of $G$, and two matrix Lie groups with the same Lie algebra look alike near the identity.
3. **One-parameter subgroups.** Every continuous homomorphism $\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to G$ is of the form $\gamma(t) = e^{tX}$ for a unique $X\in\mathfrak{g}$, namely $X = \gamma'(0)$.
4. **Homomorphisms descend.** If $\Phi : G \to H$ is a homomorphism of matrix Lie groups that is smooth, there is a unique Lie algebra homomorphism $\phi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}$ with
$$
\Phi(e^{X}) = e^{\phi(X)} \qquad \text{for all } X \in \mathfrak{g},
$$
and $\phi(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\Phi(e^{tX})\big|_{t=0}$.
5. **Structure constants.** If $\xi_1,\dots,\xi_r$ is a basis of $\mathfrak{g}$ then, because $[\xi_i,\xi_j]\in\mathfrak{g}$, there are real numbers $c_{ij}^{\,k}$ with
$$
[\xi_i,\xi_j] = \sum_{k=1}^{r} c_{ij}^{\,k}\,\xi_k ,
$$
and these satisfy
$$
c_{ij}^{\,k} = -\,c_{ji}^{\,k},
\qquad
\sum_{\kappa}\Bigl( c_{\rho\sigma}^{\,\kappa}c_{\kappa\mu}^{\,\nu} + c_{\sigma\mu}^{\,\kappa}c_{\kappa\rho}^{\,\nu} + c_{\mu\rho}^{\,\kappa}c_{\kappa\sigma}^{\,\nu} \Bigr) = 0 ,
$$
which are antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity written in components. :cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="p. 927"} calls the constancy of the $c_{ij}^{\,k}$ over the group **Lie's second theorem** and the two displayed identities **Lie's third theorem**.
6. **The classical algebras.** With $A^{\dagger}$ the conjugate transpose and $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ the transpose,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C}) &= \{X : \mathrm{tr}\,X = 0\}, &
\mathfrak{u}(n) &= \{X : X^{\dagger} = -X\}, \\
\mathfrak{su}(n) &= \{X : X^{\dagger} = -X,\ \mathrm{tr}\,X = 0\}, &
\mathfrak{so}(n) &= \{X \text{ real} : X^{\mathsf{T}} = -X\},
\end{aligned}
$$
of real dimensions $2(n^2-1)$, $n^2$, $n^2-1$ and $n(n-1)/2$ respectively.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
Three short arguments do all the work.

That $\mathfrak{g}$ is closed under *scalars* is free, since $e^{t(sX)} = e^{(ts)X}$. That it is closed under *addition* is not free, because $e^{X}e^{Y}\neq e^{X+Y}$ in general; the fix is the Lie product formula, which recovers $e^{t(X+Y)}$ as a limit of products of elements of $G$, and then closedness of $G$ finishes the job. That it is closed under the *bracket* comes from a different trick: conjugation by a group element preserves $\mathfrak{g}$, so $t\mapsto e^{tX}Ye^{-tX}$ is a curve inside the subspace $\mathfrak{g}$, and the derivative of a curve in a closed subspace stays in the subspace — that derivative is exactly $[X,Y]$.

The other statements are facts about the matrix exponential transported to $G$. The series $\sum t^kX^k/k!$ converges for every $X$, its derivative at $t=0$ is $X$, and $\exp$ therefore has derivative the identity at the origin, so the inverse function theorem makes it invertible near $0$. That single sentence is why a curved object — the group — is governed near its identity by a flat one, its Lie algebra.
:::

:::proof[$\mathfrak{g}$ is a real Lie algebra]{provenance=adapted src="lee-2013:Cor. 20.11, p. 523, and Thm 20.12, p. 523"}
Throughout, $G$ is closed in $GL(n,\mathbb{C})$ and $\mathfrak{g} = \{X : e^{tX}\in G \text{ for all } t\in\mathbb{R}\}$.

**Step 1: $\mathfrak{g}$ is closed under real scalars.** If $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ and $s\in\mathbb{R}$, then for every $t$ we have $e^{t(sX)} = e^{(ts)X}\in G$, so $sX\in\mathfrak{g}$. Note that this uses only real $s$: $\mathfrak{g}$ need not be a complex subspace, and for $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ it is not, since $iX$ is Hermitian when $X$ is anti-Hermitian.

**Step 2: $\mathfrak{g}$ is a closed subset of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$.** Let $X_k\in\mathfrak{g}$ with $X_k\to X$. For fixed $t$ the exponential is continuous (the series converges uniformly on bounded sets, by :ref[the matrix exponential]{to=linalg.matrix-exponential}), so $e^{tX_k}\to e^{tX}$, and $e^{tX}$ is invertible with inverse $e^{-tX}$. **Here the closedness of $G$ is used**: the limit of the elements $e^{tX_k}\in G$ is an invertible matrix, hence lies in $G$. So $X\in\mathfrak{g}$.

**Step 3: $\mathfrak{g}$ is closed under addition.** Let $X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}$ and fix $t\in\mathbb{R}$. We use the **Lie product formula**
$$
e^{t(X+Y)} \;=\; \lim_{m\to\infty}\Bigl( e^{\frac{t}{m}X}\, e^{\frac{t}{m}Y}\Bigr)^{m} ,
$$
which is :cite[Lee's Corollary 20.11]{work=lee-2013 loc="p. 523"}; for matrices it also follows from the expansion $e^{sX}e^{sY} = I + s(X+Y) + O(s^2)$ and taking $s = t/m$ in $\bigl(I + s(X+Y) + O(s^2)\bigr)^{m}$. Each factor $e^{(t/m)X}e^{(t/m)Y}$ lies in $G$ because $X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}$, hence so does each $m$-th power, $G$ being a group. The limit is invertible, so by closedness of $G$ it lies in $G$. As $t$ was arbitrary, $X+Y\in\mathfrak{g}$. With step 1, $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real subspace.

**Step 4: conjugation preserves $\mathfrak{g}$.** Let $A\in G$ and $Y\in\mathfrak{g}$. For every $t$,
$$
e^{\,t\,AYA^{-1}} = A\,e^{tY}A^{-1} ,
$$
because conjugation is a ring homomorphism and therefore acts term by term on the series. The right-hand side is a product of three elements of $G$, so $AYA^{-1}\in\mathfrak{g}$.

**Step 5: $\mathfrak{g}$ is closed under the commutator.** Let $X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}$ and put
$$
\gamma(t) = e^{tX}\,Y\,e^{-tX} .
$$
By step 4 with $A = e^{tX}\in G$, the curve $\gamma$ takes its values in $\mathfrak{g}$. It is differentiable, and by the product rule together with $\frac{d}{dt}e^{tX} = Xe^{tX} = e^{tX}X$,
$$
\gamma'(0) = XY - YX = [X,Y] .
$$
Now $\gamma'(0)$ is the limit of the difference quotients $\bigl(\gamma(t)-\gamma(0)\bigr)/t$, each of which lies in $\mathfrak{g}$ because $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real subspace (steps 1 and 3). **Here step 2 is used**: a limit of elements of $\mathfrak{g}$ lies in $\mathfrak{g}$. Hence $[X,Y]\in\mathfrak{g}$.

Bilinearity, antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity hold for the commutator on all of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ and therefore on $\mathfrak{g}$. So $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real Lie algebra. $\square$
:::

:::proof[The exponential map, one-parameter subgroups, and homomorphisms]{provenance=adapted src="lee-2013:Prop. 20.2, pp. 517–518, Prop. 20.8, pp. 519–521; hassani-2013:Thm 29.1.21, p. 925, and Example 29.1.23, pp. 925–926"}
**Part 2 (the exponential map).** The identities $e^{(s+t)X} = e^{sX}e^{tX}$ and $(e^{X})^{-1} = e^{-X}$ are the statements proved for the :ref[matrix exponential]{to=linalg.matrix-exponential}; :cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="Example 29.1.23, pp. 925–926"} derives the first by multiplying the two series and recognising the binomial sum $\sum_{n}t^{m-n}s^{n}/((m-n)!\,n!) = (t+s)^m/m!$. For the local statement, differentiate the series term by term at the origin: for $X\in\mathfrak{g}$,
$$
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} e^{tX} = X ,
$$
so the differential of $\exp$ at $0\in\mathfrak{g}$ is the identity map of $\mathfrak{g}$. Since $\exp$ is smooth, the inverse function theorem gives a neighbourhood of $0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ mapped diffeomorphically onto a neighbourhood of $I$ in $G$. This is :cite[Hassani's Theorem 29.1.21]{work=hassani-2013 loc="p. 925"}, and the consequence he records in Box 29.1.22 — that two groups with the same Lie algebra are locally alike — is immediate from it. The statement is *local*, and §"Where the hypotheses are used" below shows that nothing global follows.

**Part 3 (one-parameter subgroups).** If $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ then $\gamma(t) = e^{tX}$ is a homomorphism $\mathbb{R}\to G$ by part 2, and $\gamma'(0)=X$. Conversely let $\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to G$ be a continuous homomorphism. By part 2 choose a ball $U_0 = \{\|X\|<\varepsilon\}$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ on which $\exp$ is injective with image a neighbourhood of $I$ in $G$, and put $U = \tfrac12U_0$, $V = \exp(U)$, so that $U$ is convex, $2U\subseteq U_0$, and $\exp$ is injective on $2U$. By continuity of $\gamma$ there is $\tau>0$ with $\gamma(t)\in V$ for all $|t|\le\tau$; write $\gamma(\tau) = e^{Z}$ with $Z\in U$ and set $X = Z/\tau$.

We claim $\gamma(\tau/2^{k}) = e^{Z/2^{k}}$ for every $k\ge0$, by induction. It holds at $k=0$. If it holds at $k$, write $\gamma(\tau/2^{k+1}) = e^{W}$ with $W\in U$ (possible since $\tau/2^{k+1}\le\tau$). Squaring and using the homomorphism property, $e^{2W} = \gamma(\tau/2^{k}) = e^{Z/2^{k}}$. Both $2W$ and $Z/2^{k}$ lie in $2U$, on which $\exp$ is injective, so $2W = Z/2^{k}$, that is $W = Z/2^{k+1}$. **This is where the injectivity of $\exp$ near $0$ is used**, and it is why the neighbourhood had to be halved first.

Since $\gamma$ is a homomorphism, $\gamma(m\tau/2^k) = \gamma(\tau/2^k)^m = e^{mZ/2^k} = e^{(m\tau/2^k)X}$ for all integers $m$ and all $k\ge0$. The numbers $m\tau/2^k$ are dense in $\mathbb{R}$, and both $t\mapsto\gamma(t)$ and $t\mapsto e^{tX}$ are continuous, so they agree everywhere. Uniqueness of $X$ follows by differentiating at $t=0$. :cite[Lee]{work=lee-2013 loc="Thm 20.1, p. 516, and Prop. 20.2, pp. 517–518"} obtains the same conclusion by identifying one-parameter subgroups with the integral curves of left-invariant vector fields through the identity.

**Part 4 (homomorphisms).** Let $\Phi:G\to H$ be a smooth homomorphism and $X\in\mathfrak{g}$. Then $t\mapsto\Phi(e^{tX})$ is a continuous homomorphism $\mathbb{R}\to H$, so by part 3 it equals $e^{t\phi(X)}$ for a unique $\phi(X)\in\mathfrak{h}$, and differentiating at $t=0$ identifies $\phi(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\Phi(e^{tX})|_{t=0}$. Setting $t=1$ gives $\Phi(e^{X}) = e^{\phi(X)}$, which is :cite[Lee's]{work=lee-2013 loc="Prop. 20.8(g), p. 520"} commuting square. Linearity of $\phi$ follows from $\phi(sX) = s\phi(X)$ (replace $t$ by $st$) and from applying $\Phi$ to the Lie product formula of step 3 above. Finally $\phi$ preserves brackets: apply $\Phi$ to the identity $e^{tX}Ye^{-tX}\in\mathfrak{g}$ of step 4 in the previous proof — more precisely to $\Phi\bigl(e^{tX}e^{sY}e^{-tX}\bigr) = e^{t\phi(X)}e^{s\phi(Y)}e^{-t\phi(X)}$ — and differentiate once in $s$ at $s=0$ and once in $t$ at $t=0$. The left-hand side gives $\phi([X,Y])$ and the right-hand side $[\phi(X),\phi(Y)]$.

**Part 6 (the classical algebras).** Each is an application of the definition of $\mathfrak{g}$ together with one identity.

- $\mathfrak{sl}$: by the determinant-trace identity $\det e^{tX} = e^{t\,\mathrm{tr}X}$ of :ref[the matrix exponential]{to=linalg.matrix-exponential}, $\det e^{tX} = 1$ for all $t$ if and only if $\mathrm{tr}X = 0$. :cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="Box 29.1.17, p. 924"} states the conclusion as "the Lie algebra of the special linear group is the traceless operators".
- $\mathfrak{u}$: $(e^{tX})^{\dagger}e^{tX} = e^{tX^{\dagger}}e^{tX}$. Differentiating at $t=0$ gives $X^{\dagger}+X$, so unitarity for all $t$ forces $X^{\dagger} = -X$; conversely if $X^{\dagger}=-X$ then $(e^{tX})^{\dagger} = e^{-tX} = (e^{tX})^{-1}$. This is :cite[Hassani's Box 29.1.19]{work=hassani-2013 loc="p. 924"}.
- $\mathfrak{su} = \mathfrak{sl}\cap\mathfrak{u}$, giving anti-Hermitian traceless matrices; the diagonal of an anti-Hermitian matrix is purely imaginary with $n$ free entries and the strict upper triangle has $n(n-1)/2$ complex entries, so $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{u}(n) = n + n(n-1) = n^2$ and the trace condition removes one more, $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{su}(n) = n^2-1$ (:cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="Box 29.1.20, p. 925"}).
- $\mathfrak{so}$: the same computation with the transpose in place of the conjugate transpose, over $\mathbb{R}$; a real skew-symmetric matrix has zero diagonal and $n(n-1)/2$ free entries. :cite[Lee]{work=lee-2013 loc="Example 20.4, p. 518"} records the forward direction as "the exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix is orthogonal". Note that $\mathrm{tr}X=0$ is automatic here, so $\mathfrak{so}(n) = \mathfrak{o}(n)$ and $SO(n)$, $O(n)$ have the same Lie algebra — the first sign that the Lie algebra sees only the identity component. $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used]{provenance=original}
- **Closedness of $G$ is used twice**, in step 2 and step 3 of the first proof, and it cannot be dropped. Inside the torus $T = \{\mathrm{diag}(e^{i\theta},e^{i\varphi})\}\subset GL(2,\mathbb{C})$ take
$$
H_\alpha = \bigl\{\, \mathrm{diag}\!\left(e^{it}, e^{i\alpha t}\right) : t\in\mathbb{R} \,\bigr\}, \qquad \alpha \text{ irrational.}
$$
$H_\alpha$ is a subgroup and the image of a one-parameter subgroup, but it is dense in $T$ and so is not closed. Its closure $T$ is two-dimensional while $H_\alpha$ is a one-dimensional curve, so no neighbourhood of the identity in $H_\alpha$ is homeomorphic to a neighbourhood in the subspace topology of a Euclidean ball: $H_\alpha$ is a Lie group in its own right but not an *embedded* subgroup, and the closed subgroup theorem does not apply to it.
- **"For all $t$", not "for small $t$".** If the condition $e^{tX}\in G$ were imposed only for $|t|<\varepsilon$ the set would still be defined, but step 1 would fail to give a subspace stable under large scalars, and the identification with the tangent space at $I$ would break. The condition for all $t$ is :cite[Lee's]{work=lee-2013 loc="Prop. 20.9, p. 521"} characterisation and it is exactly what the halving argument in part 3 reconstructs.
- **$\exp$ is local, and only local.** Part 2 says nothing about the image of $\exp$ in the large. Two failures are standard. First, $\exp$ need not be onto even for a connected group: in $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$, a traceless real $X$ has eigenvalues $\pm\lambda$ with $\lambda$ real, $\pm i\mu$ with $\mu$ real, or $0$ twice, so $\mathrm{tr}\,e^{X}$ equals $2\cosh\lambda\ge 2$, $2\cos\mu\ge-2$, or $2$; hence no element with trace $<-2$, for instance $\mathrm{diag}(-2,-\tfrac12)$, is an exponential. Second, $\exp$ need not be injective: $e^{2\pi k X} = I$ for every integer $k$ when $X = \mathrm{diag}(i,-i)$.
- **$e^{X}e^{Y} = e^{X+Y}$ requires $[X,Y]=0$.** It is used nowhere above, and the Lie product formula of step 3 exists precisely to avoid needing it. The exact condition under which $e^{sX}e^{tY} = e^{sX+tY}$ holds for all small $s,t$ is commutativity, as :ref[the matrix exponential]{to=linalg.matrix-exponential} records.
- **Smoothness of $\Phi$ in part 4** can in fact be weakened to continuity, but the proof above uses differentiability of $t\mapsto\Phi(e^{tX})$ at $0$ and we do not claim the stronger statement.
- **Real versus complex.** $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real Lie algebra even when $G$ consists of complex matrices. Forgetting this is the usual reason for a factor-of-two error in a dimension count: $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ has real dimension $3$, not $3$ complex dimensions, and $SU(2)$ is a three-parameter group.
:::

:::method[Finding the Lie algebra and the structure constants of a matrix group]{provenance=original}
Let $G$ be specified by a set of equations on matrices, say $F(A) = 0$.

1. **Linearise the defining equations at the identity.** Put $A = e^{tX}= I + tX + O(t^2)$, substitute into $F(A)=0$, and keep the terms of first order in $t$. The resulting linear conditions on $X$ are the candidate for $\mathfrak{g}$. (For $A^{\dagger}A = I$ this returns $X^{\dagger}+X = 0$; for $\det A = 1$ it returns $\mathrm{tr}X = 0$.)
2. **Verify both directions.** Check that each $X$ satisfying the linearised conditions really has $e^{tX}\in G$ for all $t$. Step 1 alone gives only a necessary condition.
3. **Count.** $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{g}$ is the number of real parameters of $G$; :cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris]{work=arfken-2013 loc="p. 845"} call this the **order** of the group.
4. **Choose a basis $\xi_1,\dots,\xi_r$** adapted to the problem — usually one that makes as many brackets as possible vanish, or one with a physical meaning.
5. **Compute the brackets** $[\xi_i,\xi_j]$ as matrix commutators and expand them in the basis; the coefficients are the structure constants. Check antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity as an arithmetic test of the algebra.
6. **If you need Hermitian generators**, set $S_j = -i\xi_j$ (equivalently $\xi_j = iS_j$). Then $e^{\varphi\xi_j} = e^{i\varphi S_j}$ and the brackets acquire a factor $i$, $[S_j,S_k] = i f_{jkl}S_l$, as in :cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris, Eq. (17.43)]{work=arfken-2013 loc="p. 848"}. If $G$ is unitary the $S_j$ are Hermitian, and if $\det = 1$ they are also traceless, by their Eqs. (17.40) and (17.41).
7. **Record the rank**, the largest number of mutually commuting independent generators. It is the number of labels — quantum numbers — needed to tell apart the basis vectors of an irreducible representation, and also, by :cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris]{work=arfken-2013 loc="p. 849"}, the number of independent Casimir operators (see :ref[Casimir operators]{to=group.casimir-operators}).
:::

:::example[The Heisenberg group: a group where every step is exact]{provenance=original}
Let
$$
G \;=\; \left\{\, A(x,y,z) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x & z \\ 0 & 1 & y \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} : x,y,z\in\mathbb{R} \,\right\} \subset GL(3,\mathbb{R}).
$$

**Step 1: it is a matrix Lie group.** Multiplying,
$$
A(x,y,z)\,A(x',y',z') = A\bigl(x+x',\; y+y',\; z+z'+xy'\bigr),
$$
so $G$ is closed under products, $A(0,0,0)=I$, and $A(x,y,z)^{-1} = A(-x,-y,xy-z)$. It is a closed subset of $GL(3,\mathbb{R})$ because it is cut out by the equations $A_{11}=A_{22}=A_{33}=1$, $A_{21}=A_{31}=A_{32}=0$, all of which survive limits. Note that the product is **not** commutative: the term $xy'$ is not symmetric in the two arguments.

**Step 2: linearise.** Writing $A = I + tX + O(t^2)$, the defining equations say that $X$ has zeros on and below the diagonal. So the candidate algebra is the strictly upper triangular matrices
$$
\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{R}\xi_1 \oplus \mathbb{R}\xi_2 \oplus \mathbb{R}\xi_3,
\qquad
\xi_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\xi_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\xi_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 3: verify, by computing $\exp$ exactly.** For $M = a\xi_1+b\xi_2+c\xi_3$ one finds $M^2 = ab\,\xi_3$ and $M^3 = 0$, so the exponential series stops after three terms:
$$
e^{M} \;=\; I + M + \tfrac12 M^2 \;=\; A\!\left(a,\; b,\; c + \tfrac{ab}{2}\right).
$$
Every $e^{tM}$ therefore lies in $G$, so $\mathfrak{g}$ is exactly the Lie algebra, $\dim\mathfrak{g}=3$, and $G$ is a three-parameter group. Here $\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G$ is a bijection, not merely a local one: given $(x,y,z)$, solve $a=x$, $b=y$, $c = z - xy/2$. **A number:** at $(a,b,c) = (2,3,1)$,
$$
e^{M} = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&4\\0&1&3\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad\text{since } c + \tfrac{ab}{2} = 1 + 3 = 4 .
$$

**Step 4: the structure constants.** Direct multiplication gives
$$
[\xi_1,\xi_2] = \xi_3, \qquad [\xi_1,\xi_3] = 0, \qquad [\xi_2,\xi_3] = 0 ,
$$
so the only non-zero structure constants are $c_{12}^{\;3} = 1 = -c_{21}^{\;3}$. Antisymmetry is visible, and the Jacobi identity holds because every double bracket contains $\xi_3$, which is central.

**Step 5: a consistency check on the group commutator.** The group commutator of two one-parameter subgroups should, to leading order, reproduce the Lie bracket; here it does so *exactly*. Using the multiplication law of step 1 with $e^{s\xi_1} = A(s,0,0)$ and $e^{s\xi_2} = A(0,s,0)$,
$$
e^{s\xi_1}e^{s\xi_2}e^{-s\xi_1}e^{-s\xi_2} = A(0,0,s^2) = e^{\,s^2[\xi_1,\xi_2]} .
$$
At $s = \tfrac12$ the right-hand corner entry is $s^2 = 0.25$, and no other entry is disturbed. This is :cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris's]{work=arfken-2013 loc="Eq. (17.42), p. 848"} second-order expansion $U_k^{-1}U_j^{-1}U_kU_j = 1 + \epsilon_j\epsilon_k[S_j,S_k]+\cdots$ with the higher-order terms absent, because the algebra is nilpotent.

**Step 6: contrast with a group where $\exp$ is not onto.** In $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ — also three-dimensional, also connected — the remark above shows that $\mathrm{diag}(-2,-\tfrac12)$, of determinant $1$ and trace $-2.5$, is not $e^{X}$ for any $X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$. The Heisenberg group is the pleasant case, not the typical one.
:::

:::application{provenance=original}
**Symmetry and conservation laws.** A continuous symmetry of a physical system is an action of a Lie group, and its generators are the conserved quantities: the three generators of translations are the momentum components, those of rotations are the angular momenta (:ref[angular momentum algebra]{to=group.angular-momentum}), and the generator of time translation is the Hamiltonian. The statement "the symmetry group is $G$" becomes the computable statement "these $r$ operators satisfy these structure constants", which is what one actually uses.

**Quantum mechanics and spin.** The passage $U = e^{i\varphi S}$ with Hermitian $S$ is the link between a one-parameter family of unitary evolutions and a self-adjoint observable; in infinite dimensions it is :ref[Stone's theorem]{to=funcan.stone-theorem}. The whole theory of angular momentum is the representation theory of one three-dimensional Lie algebra, as :ref[the rotation group and SU(2)]{to=group.so3-su2} makes explicit, and the Heisenberg group of the example above is the group generated by position and momentum, whose Lie algebra relation $[\xi_1,\xi_2]=\xi_3$ is the canonical commutation relation with $\xi_3$ central — that centrality is why $\hbar$ is a number and not an operator.

**Particle physics.** The standard model is specified by naming a Lie group, $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$, and the representations the fields carry. The structure constants of $\mathfrak{su}(3)$ fix the self-interactions of the gluons; those of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ fix the weak interactions. :cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris]{work=arfken-2013 loc="pp. 846, 853"} tell this history from isospin onwards.

**Rigid-body motion, robotics and computer vision.** The configuration of a rigid body is an element of the six-dimensional group $SE(3)$ of rigid motions; velocities are elements of its Lie algebra, and the exponential map is the standard way to parameterise a pose by a "twist". Because the group is curved, numerical integration of rigid-body dynamics is done with schemes that take their steps with $\exp$, so that the computed configuration stays exactly in the group instead of drifting off it.

**Differential equations.** Lie's own motivation was that a differential equation invariant under a one-parameter group can be reduced in order, and the generator tells one which change of variable does it; this is the systematic version of the ad hoc substitutions in :ref[first-order equations]{to=ode.first-order-methods}. The same idea classifies the special functions: as :cite[Hassani]{work=hassani-2013 loc="pp. 970–971"} puts it, many of the equations of mathematical physics are statements that a Casimir operator of some Lie algebra acts as a multiple of the identity.
:::
