:::definition[Group]{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§1.1, the definitions of a binary operation and of a group, pp. 16-17, and the power notation, p. 20; rhb-2006:§28.1.1, the four axioms (i)-(iv), p. 1043; hassani-2013:§23.1, the order $|G|$ and the powers $a^k$, p. 703"}
A **binary operation** on a set $G$ is a map $G \times G \to G$. We write it as juxtaposition, $(x,y) \mapsto xy$, and call $xy$ the **product** of $x$ and $y$; that the product always lands back in $G$ is the **closure** requirement, and it is built into the word "map". When a second operation is in play we write $x \star y$ instead.

A **group** is a set $G$ with a binary operation satisfying three axioms.

- **(G1) Associativity.** $(xy)z = x(yz)$ for all $x, y, z \in G$.
- **(G2) Identity.** There is an $e \in G$ with $ex = xe = x$ for every $x \in G$.
- **(G3) Inverses.** For each $x \in G$ there is an $x^{-1} \in G$ with $x x^{-1} = x^{-1} x = e$.

Axiom (G2) requires $G$ to have at least one element, so a group is never empty. The group is **abelian**, or commutative, if in addition $xy = yx$ for all $x,y$; most groups of physical interest are not. A **finite group** is one with finitely many elements, and the number of elements of a finite group is its **order**, written $|G|$. An infinite group is said to have infinite order.

For $n \in \mathbb{N}$ we write $x^n$ for the product of $n$ copies of $x$, $x^{-n}$ for the product of $n$ copies of $x^{-1}$, and $x^0 = e$; then $x^{i}x^{j} = x^{i+j}$ for all $i, j \in \mathbb{Z}$. When the operation is addition the same objects are written $e = 0$, $x^{-1} = -x$ and $x^n = nx$.

Two conventions are worth fixing at the start. First, (G2) only *postulates* an identity; its uniqueness is a theorem, proved below. Some texts, :cite[Riley, Hobson and Bence]{work=rhb-2006 loc="§28.1.1, axiom (iii), p. 1043"} among them, write "unique" into the axiom itself, which costs nothing because the two readings agree. Second, when the elements are operations acting on something, $XY$ means "do $Y$ first, then $X$", matching the order in which composed functions and matrices act.
:::

:::note[Where groups come from]{provenance=adapted src="rhb-2006:§28.1, the symmetry operations of the hydrogen and ammonia molecules, pp. 1041-1042; arfken-2013:§17.1, Examples 17.1.1-17.1.3, pp. 817-819; hassani-2013:§23.1, Example 23.1.2, pp. 703-704, and §23.2, Example 23.2.2 with Boxes 23.1.7, 23.2.3 and 23.2.4, pp. 705-706"}
The axioms are an abstraction of two quite different situations.

**Numbers.** $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ are abelian groups under addition; $\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$, $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ are abelian groups under multiplication. $\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$ is not a group under multiplication: $2$ has no integer reciprocal. Any vector space is an abelian group under vector addition, its scalar multiplication simply forgotten.

**Symmetries.** Fix an object and collect every transformation that leaves it looking the same. Composition of two such transformations is another, undoing one is another, and doing nothing is the identity, so the collection is a group. :cite[Riley, Hobson and Bence]{work=rhb-2006 loc="§28.1, pp. 1041-1042"} start from the hydrogen and ammonia molecules in exactly this way. For an equilateral triangle the group has six elements — the identity, two rotations by $\pm 120^{\circ}$, and three reflections — and it is not abelian, since a rotation followed by a reflection generally differs from the same two performed in the other order (:cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris]{work=arfken-2013 loc="Example 17.1.1 and Table 17.1, pp. 817-818"}). For a circular disk the symmetries form the continuous abelian group of rotations $R(\varphi)$, with $R(\varphi)R(\theta) = R(\varphi + \theta)$.

**Matrices.** The invertible $n \times n$ matrices over a field $F$ form the **general linear group** $GL(n,F)$ under matrix multiplication; more abstractly, $GL(V)$ is the group of invertible linear maps of a vector space $V$ to itself (:ref[Linear maps and matrices]{to=linalg.linear-maps-matrices}). Inside it sit the groups that physics uses constantly: $SL(n,F)$ (determinant $1$), $O(n)$ and $SO(n)$ (:ref[Unitary and orthogonal matrices]{to=linalg.unitary-orthogonal-matrices}), $U(n)$ and $SU(n)$, the Lorentz group $O(3,1)$, and the symplectic group $Sp(2n,\mathbb{R})$ of Hamiltonian mechanics. These are collected in :ref[The classical matrix groups]{to=group.matrix-group-catalogue}.

A group may also be specified by nothing but its multiplication table, with no numbers or geometry attached at all; :cite[Arfken, Weber and Harris]{work=arfken-2013 loc="Example 17.1.3 and Table 17.2, p. 819"} give the four-element *Vierergruppe* that way.
:::

:::statement[Uniqueness, inverses and cancellation]{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§1.1, Proposition 1 parts (1)-(4), p. 18, and Proposition 2 with the two remarks following it, p. 20"}
Let $G$ be a group with identity $e$, and let $a, b, u, v \in G$.

1. The identity is unique: if $f \in G$ satisfies $fx = xf = x$ for all $x$, then $f = e$.
2. Inverses are unique: if $ab = ba = e$ and $ac = ca = e$ then $b = c$. We may therefore speak of *the* inverse $a^{-1}$.
3. $(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$ and $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$, in that order.
4. The equations $ax = b$ and $ya = b$ have exactly one solution each, namely $x = a^{-1}b$ and $y = ba^{-1}$. In particular both **cancellation laws** hold: $au = av$ implies $u = v$, and $ub = vb$ implies $u = v$.
5. One side suffices: if $ab = e$ for some $b$, then $b = a^{-1}$ (and likewise if $ba = e$); and if $ab = a$ for a single $a$, then $b = e$.

Consequently a product $a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n$ needs no brackets, and in a finite group each row and each column of the multiplication table is a rearrangement of the elements of $G$.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
Everything here comes from multiplying by an inverse and from the fact that an inverse exists on both sides. Uniqueness of the identity is the one statement that does not need inverses at all: two identities $e$ and $f$ can be multiplied together, and each then claims the product for itself. Uniqueness of inverses is the same trick with a third element in the middle: evaluate $cab$ two ways, bracketing it once to the left and once to the right. The cancellation laws, and with them the rearrangement of the multiplication table, are the single observation that multiplying by $a^{-1}$ undoes multiplying by $a$.
:::

:::proof{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§1.1, the proofs of Proposition 1 (1)-(4), pp. 18-19, and of Proposition 2, p. 20"}
**1.** Consider the product $ef$. Reading $e$ as an identity gives $ef = f$; reading $f$ as an identity gives $ef = e$. Hence $e = f$.

**2.** Suppose $ab = ba = e$ and $ac = ca = e$. Bracket the product $c(ab)$ in the two ways permitted by (G1):
$$
c(ab) = ce = c, \qquad (ca)b = eb = b .
$$
Associativity says the two left-hand sides are equal, so $b = c$. (Only $ab = e$ and $ca = e$ were used, which is more than we need and will be sharpened in part 5.)

**3.** The defining equations $a a^{-1} = a^{-1} a = e$ say, read with the roles of $a$ and $a^{-1}$ exchanged, that $a$ is an inverse of $a^{-1}$. By part 2 the inverse of $a^{-1}$ is unique, so it is $a$. For the second identity, compute directly, using (G1) to move the brackets:
$$
(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = a\bigl(b b^{-1}\bigr)a^{-1} = a e a^{-1} = a a^{-1} = e ,
$$
and symmetrically $(b^{-1}a^{-1})(ab) = e$. So $b^{-1}a^{-1}$ is *an* inverse of $ab$, hence by part 2 it is $(ab)^{-1}$. The reversal of order is not optional: in a non-abelian group $a^{-1}b^{-1}$ generally fails.

**4.** $x = a^{-1}b$ solves $ax = b$, since $a(a^{-1}b) = (aa^{-1})b = b$. If $x'$ is any solution, multiply $ax' = b$ on the left by $a^{-1}$ and use (G1): $x' = (a^{-1}a)x' = a^{-1}b$. So the solution is unique, and the same argument on the other side handles $ya = b$. For left cancellation, $au = av$ gives $u = a^{-1}(au) = a^{-1}(av) = v$; right cancellation follows by multiplying on the right by $b^{-1}$.

**5.** If $ab = e$ then, multiplying on the left by $a^{-1}$, $b = a^{-1}(ab) = a^{-1}e = a^{-1}$. If $ba = e$, multiply on the right by $a^{-1}$ instead. If $ab = a$, then $ab = ae$, and left cancellation gives $b = e$.

**Bracketing and the multiplication table.** That a product of $n$ factors is independent of its bracketing follows by induction on $n$: the cases $n \le 3$ are (G1), and any bracketing of $a_1 \cdots a_n$ splits at some point into a product $(a_1\cdots a_k)(a_{k+1}\cdots a_n)$ of two shorter bracketed products, which by the inductive hypothesis have unambiguous values; repeated use of (G1) then moves every split to the same place. Finally, fix $a$ and consider the row of the table indexed by $a$, that is, the list $x \mapsto ax$. By part 4 this map is injective (cancellation) and surjective (solve $ax = b$), so the row contains every element of $G$ exactly once; columns are the same with the other factor fixed. $\square$
:::

:::definition[Subgroup]{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§2.1, the definition of a subgroup and the paragraph identifying its identity and inverses with those of $G$, p. 46; hassani-2013:§23.2, Definition 23.2.1 and the remark that $e \in S$ follows, pp. 705-706; rhb-2006:§28.6, the definition and the trivial and proper subgroups, p. 1061"}
A subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a **subgroup**, written $H \le G$, if $H$ is non-empty and is closed under the two operations of $G$: $xy \in H$ and $x^{-1} \in H$ whenever $x, y \in H$.

Such an $H$ is a group in its own right under the restricted operation. Associativity is inherited for free, since an equation among elements of $H$ is an equation in $G$; the identity of $H$ is the identity of $G$, because $e_H e_H = e_H = e_H e$ in $G$ and cancellation gives $e_H = e$; and for the same reason the inverse of $x$ in $H$ is its inverse in $G$. In particular $e \in H$ for every subgroup, so no two subgroups are disjoint.

Every group has the two **trivial subgroups** $\{e\}$ and $G$; any other subgroup is **proper**. The relation $\le$ is transitive: a subgroup of a subgroup of $G$ is a subgroup of $G$. An intersection of subgroups is again a subgroup, since each defining condition is inherited from every member of the family; a union of subgroups usually is not.
:::

:::statement[Subgroup criterion]{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§2.1, Proposition 1 and the sentence on finite subsets that follows it, pp. 47-48"}
Let $H$ be a subset of a group $G$. The following two conditions are equivalent.

1. $H$ is a subgroup of $G$.
2. **(S1)** $H$ has at least one element, and **(S2)** the combination $xy^{-1}$ belongs to $H$ whenever both $x$ and $y$ do.

Should $H$ happen to have only finitely many elements, a third equivalent condition joins the list:

3. $H$ has at least one element, and $xy$ belongs to $H$ whenever both $x$ and $y$ do.

So for a finite candidate the single word "inverses" may be struck from the definition, while for an infinite one it may not.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
One condition does the work of two because $xy^{-1}$ can be specialised. Feed it the same element twice and it produces the identity; feed it the identity and an element and it produces that element's inverse; once inverses are available, feeding it $x$ and $y^{-1}$ produces the product $xy$. For the finite case there are no inverses to manufacture out of nothing, so one uses finiteness instead: the powers of an element of a finite set must eventually repeat, and a repetition $x^{a} = x^{b}$ turns some positive power of $x$ into the identity, which puts the inverse of $x$ among its own positive powers.
:::

:::proof{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§2.1, the proof of Proposition 1 and the finite-subset argument, pp. 47-48"}
**A subgroup satisfies (S1) and (S2).** It is non-empty by definition, and if $x, y \in H$ then $y^{-1} \in H$ by closure under inverses and $xy^{-1} \in H$ by closure under products.

**(S1) and (S2) give a subgroup.** Three applications of (S2), in order.

- By (S1) pick some $x \in H$. Taking both arguments equal to $x$, $e = x x^{-1} \in H$.
- Now take the first argument to be $e$ and the second to be any $y \in H$: $y^{-1} = e y^{-1} \in H$. So $H$ is closed under inverses.
- Finally let $x, y \in H$. By the previous step $y^{-1} \in H$, so (S2) applied to the pair $x, y^{-1}$ gives $x(y^{-1})^{-1} = xy \in H$, using $(y^{-1})^{-1} = y$. So $H$ is closed under products.

Hence $H \le G$.

**The finite case.** Assume $H$ is a finite non-empty subset closed under products, and let $x \in H$. All the powers $x, x^2, x^3, \dots$ lie in $H$ by closure, and $H$ is finite, so they cannot all be distinct: there are exponents $a < b$ with $x^{a} = x^{b}$. Cancelling $x^{a}$ gives $x^{\,b-a} = e$ with $n := b - a \ge 1$. Therefore $e = x^{n} \in H$, and
$$
x\,x^{\,n-1} = x^{n} = e ,
$$
so by part 5 of the uniqueness statement $x^{-1} = x^{\,n-1}$. If $n = 1$ this reads $x^{-1} = x^{0} = e = x$, which is correct. In either case $x^{-1}$ is a power of $x$ with a non-negative exponent, hence lies in $H$. So $H$ is closed under inverses as well, and is a subgroup. $\square$
:::

:::definition[Order of an element; cyclic groups]{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§1.1, the definition of the order $|x|$, p. 20, and §2.3, the definition of a cyclic group and of $\\langle x \\rangle$, p. 54; hassani-2013:§23.2, Definition 23.2.5 and the cyclic subgroup $\\langle a \\rangle$, p. 707"}
Let $x$ be an element of a group $G$. The **order** of $x$, written $|x|$, is the least $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $x^{n} = e$; if no positive power of $x$ equals $e$, then $x$ has **infinite order**. The identity is the only element of order $1$.

The set of all integer powers of $x$,
$$
\langle x \rangle = \{\, x^{k} : k \in \mathbb{Z} \,\},
$$
is a subgroup of $G$: it is non-empty, and $x^{j}(x^{k})^{-1} = x^{\,j-k}$ lies in it, so (S2) holds. It is the **cyclic subgroup generated by $x$**, and it is abelian because powers of one element commute. A group $G$ is **cyclic** if $G = \langle x \rangle$ for some $x \in G$, and any such $x$ is a **generator**. A cyclic group may have several generators: $\langle x \rangle = \langle x^{-1}\rangle$ always, since $-k$ runs over $\mathbb{Z}$ as $k$ does.

The same symbol $|\cdot|$ is used for the order of an element and for the order of a group. The statement below shows the clash is harmless.
:::

:::statement[The cyclic subgroup generated by one element]{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§2.3, Proposition 2, Proposition 3 and Proposition 5(2), pp. 55-57"}
Let $G$ be a group and $x \in G$.

1. $|\langle x \rangle| = |x|$, with the convention that one side is infinite exactly when the other is. More precisely, if $|x| = n < \infty$ then $e, x, x^{2}, \dots, x^{\,n-1}$ are distinct and exhaust $\langle x \rangle$, while if $|x| = \infty$ then all the powers $x^{k}$, $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, are distinct.
2. If $x^{m} = e$ for some $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $|x|$ divides $m$. Conversely $x^{m} = e$ whenever $|x|$ divides $m$.
3. If $|x| = n < \infty$ and $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $|x^{a}| = n/\gcd(n,a)$. In particular $x^{a}$ generates $\langle x \rangle$ exactly when $\gcd(n,a) = 1$.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
Both of the first two parts are the division algorithm in disguise. Any exponent $k$ can be written $k = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$; since $x^{n} = e$, the quotient part collapses and $x^{k} = x^{r}$. That simultaneously shows the list $e, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$ is everything, and that $x^{k} = e$ forces $x^{r} = e$ with $r$ below the minimum $n$, so $r = 0$ and $n \mid k$. Part 3 then follows by asking for the smallest $m > 0$ with $n \mid am$, which is a statement about least common multiples.
:::

:::proof{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§2.3, the proofs of Propositions 2, 3 and 5(2), pp. 55-57"}
**Part 1, finite case.** Let $|x| = n < \infty$. The listed elements are distinct: if $x^{a} = x^{b}$ with $0 \le a < b \le n-1$, then cancelling $x^{a}$ gives $x^{\,b-a} = e$ with $1 \le b-a \le n-1$, contradicting the *minimality* of $n$ in the definition of order. They exhaust $\langle x \rangle$: given $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, divide, $k = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$, and use the exponent law,
$$
x^{k} = \bigl(x^{n}\bigr)^{q} x^{r} = e^{q} x^{r} = x^{r} .
$$
So $|\langle x \rangle| = n = |x|$.

**Part 1, infinite case.** Let $|x| = \infty$. If $x^{a} = x^{b}$ with $a < b$ then $x^{\,b-a} = e$ with $b - a \ge 1$, so $x$ would have finite order. Hence all powers are distinct and $\langle x \rangle$ is infinite.

**Part 2.** Suppose $x^{m} = e$. If $m = 0$ there is nothing to prove, and $x^{-m} = (x^{m})^{-1} = e$, so we may assume $m > 0$. Then $x$ has finite order; write $n = |x|$ and divide, $m = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$. As in part 1, $x^{r} = x^{m} = e$. Since $n$ is the *least* positive exponent with that property and $r < n$, the only possibility is $r = 0$, that is, $n \mid m$. The converse is immediate: if $m = nq$ then $x^{m} = (x^{n})^{q} = e$.

**Part 3.** Put $n = |x|$, $d = \gcd(n,a)$, and write $n = db$, $a = dc$ with $\gcd(b,c) = 1$ and $b > 0$. First, $(x^{a})^{b} = x^{ab} = x^{\,dcb} = (x^{\,db})^{c} = (x^{n})^{c} = e$, so $x^{a}$ has finite order, and by part 2 that order divides $b$. Conversely let $m = |x^{a}|$, so $x^{am} = e$; by part 2 again $n \mid am$, that is, $db \mid dcm$, hence $b \mid cm$. Since $\gcd(b,c) = 1$, $b$ divides $m$. The two divisibilities give $m = b = n/d$, which is the claim. The last sentence follows because $\langle x^{a}\rangle \subseteq \langle x\rangle$ and, by part 1, the two have sizes $n/d$ and $n$, so they coincide exactly when $d = 1$. $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used]{provenance=adapted src="dummit-foote-2004:§1.1, the non-associativity of the vector cross product, p. 16, and §2.1, Example 5(b) on $\\mathbb{Z}^{+}$, p. 47; hassani-2013:§23.2, Example 23.2.2 item 3, p. 706"}
**Associativity (G1) is a real restriction.** The cross product is a perfectly good binary operation on $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, but $\mathbf{e}_1 \times (\mathbf{e}_1 \times \mathbf{e}_2) = -\mathbf{e}_2$ while $(\mathbf{e}_1 \times \mathbf{e}_1) \times \mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{0}$, so it is not associative and $(\mathbb{R}^{3}, \times)$ is not a group. Without (G1) the proof of uniqueness of inverses fails at its first line, and products of three or more elements stop having well-defined values.

**Inverses are not implied by closure.** The positive integers are closed under addition, contain no identity for it, and contain no additive inverses; $\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ is therefore not a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$. This is also the hypothesis that the finite half of the subgroup criterion replaces: the shortcut "closed under products" is valid **only for finite $H$**, and $\mathbb{Z}^{+} \subset \mathbb{Z}$ is precisely the infinite counterexample. The proof shows where finiteness enters — in the pigeonhole step that forces two powers of $x$ to coincide.

**The operation must be the one inherited from $G$.** $\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$ is a group under multiplication and a subset of the additive group $\mathbb{R}$, but it is not a subgroup of it: the operations are different. "$H \le G$" always means $H$ carries the restriction of $G$'s operation.

**Non-emptiness.** The empty set satisfies (S2) vacuously, so (S1) cannot be dropped. In practice one verifies (S1) by exhibiting $e \in H$.

**Minimality in the definition of order.** Part 2 above fails without it. The element $x = \mathrm{i}$ of $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ satisfies $x^{4} = 1$ and $x^{8} = 1$; it is only because $4$ is the *least* such exponent that one may conclude $4 \mid m$ from $x^{m} = 1$.

**What is special about cyclic groups.** Every cyclic group is abelian, so a non-abelian group is never cyclic — the six-element symmetry group of the equilateral triangle, for instance. The converse fails: $\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic, but the group computed in the example below is abelian of order $8$ and not cyclic. Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ are the exception in which cyclicity is automatic: each is $m\mathbb{Z} = \langle m \rangle$ for a unique $m \ge 0$.
:::

:::example[A group of order eight that is not cyclic]{provenance=original}
Let
$$
G = (\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z})^{\times} = \{\, \bar a : 1 \le a \le 14,\ \gcd(a,15) = 1 \,\},
$$
the residue classes modulo $15$ that have a multiplicative inverse, under multiplication of classes. This is a group: multiplication of classes is associative and has identity $\bar 1$; it is closed, because $\gcd(a,15) = \gcd(b,15) = 1$ gives $\gcd(ab,15) = 1$; and each class is invertible by construction. Discarding the multiples of $3$ and of $5$ leaves
$$
G = \{\bar 1, \bar 2, \bar 4, \bar 7, \bar 8, \bar{11}, \bar{13}, \bar{14}\}, \qquad |G| = 8 .
$$
We drop the bars from here on.

**Orders of the elements.** Work out the powers of $2$ modulo $15$:
$$
2^{1} = 2, \quad 2^{2} = 4, \quad 2^{3} = 8, \quad 2^{4} = 16 = 1 ,
$$
so $|2| = 4$ and $\langle 2 \rangle = \{1,2,4,8\}$, a subgroup of order $4$ — which matches part 1 of the statement above. For $7$:
$$
7^{2} = 49 = 45 + 4 = 4, \quad 7^{3} = 7 \cdot 4 = 28 = 13, \quad 7^{4} = 7 \cdot 13 = 91 = 90 + 1 = 1 ,
$$
so $|7| = 4$. The remaining elements are quicker. $4^{2} = 16 = 1$, so $|4| = 2$; $11^{2} = 121 = 120 + 1 = 1$, so $|11| = 2$; $14^{2} = 196 = 195 + 1 = 1$, so $|14| = 2$ (indeed $14 = -1$ modulo $15$). For $8 = 2^{3}$ part 3 applies with $n = 4$ and $a = 3$: $|8| = 4/\gcd(4,3) = 4$. For $13 = 7^{3}$ the same gives $|13| = 4$. Collecting:

| element | $1$ | $2$ | $4$ | $7$ | $8$ | $11$ | $13$ | $14$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| order | $1$ | $4$ | $2$ | $4$ | $4$ | $2$ | $4$ | $2$ |

**Conclusion.** The largest order occurring is $4$. By part 1, a generator of a cyclic group of order $8$ would have to be an element of order $8$, and there is none. Hence $G$ is abelian of order $8$ but **not cyclic**, and no single element generates it. Two elements do: every element of $G$ is $2^{i} 11^{j}$ with $i \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ and $j \in \{0,1\}$, since $11 \notin \langle 2\rangle$ and $4 \cdot 2 = 8$ counts the products correctly.

**Two checks.** First, the orders listed are all divisors of $|G| = 8$, as :ref[Lagrange's theorem]{to=group.cosets-lagrange} requires. Second, the four elements of order dividing $2$, namely $\{1, 4, 11, 14\}$, are closed under multiplication — $4 \cdot 11 = 44 = 45 - 1 = 14$, $4 \cdot 14 = 56 = 45 + 11 = 11$, $11 \cdot 14 = 154 = 150 + 4 = 4$ — and so form a subgroup of order $4$ by the finite subgroup criterion. The final number to carry away: $G$ has exactly $4$ elements of order $4$, namely $2$, $7$, $8$ and $13$.
:::

:::application{provenance=original}
**Symmetry as a bookkeeping device.** A Hamiltonian, a crystal, a molecule or a field theory comes with a group of transformations that leave it unchanged, and almost everything one can say about the system without solving it follows from that group. Which transformations are symmetries is a closure question: the set of them is automatically a group, which is why the axioms are the right abstraction and why one may speak of "the" symmetry group of a configuration. The finite ones are the point groups of molecules and crystals (:ref[Point groups and crystal symmetry]{to=group.point-groups}); the continuous ones are the matrix groups $SO(3)$, $SU(2)$, $SU(3)$ and the Lorentz group (:ref[Matrix Lie groups and Lie algebras]{to=group.lie-groups-algebras}).

**Orders control what can happen.** The order of an element is the number of times an operation must be repeated to get back to the start, so it is directly observable: a rotation axis of a crystal has order $2$, $3$, $4$ or $6$ and nothing else, and that single arithmetic fact is the crystallographic restriction. In the quantum-mechanical setting the order of a symmetry operation limits the phases it can multiply a state by: if $S^{n} = \mathbb{1}$ and $S$ acts on an eigenstate by a number $\lambda$, then $\lambda^{n} = 1$, so $\lambda$ is an $n$th root of unity. Parity ($n = 2$, $\lambda = \pm 1$) is the familiar case.

**Cyclic groups and periodicity.** A cyclic group of order $n$ is the symmetry group of anything with $n$-fold periodicity: the rotations of a regular $n$-gon, the Bloch phases $e^{2\pi \mathrm{i} k/n}$ of a ring of $n$ lattice sites, the discrete Fourier transform. The classification of these groups is as complete as it could be — cyclic groups of equal order are always isomorphic to one another, so for each $n$ there is only one (:cite[Dummit and Foote]{work=dummit-foote-2004 loc="§2.3, Theorem 4, p. 56"}), and the infinite one is $\mathbb{Z}$. That is why "the" $\mathbb{Z}_n$ symmetry of a model is a well-posed phrase.

**Subgroups and symmetry breaking.** When a system cools through a phase transition or has a field applied, the symmetry group shrinks to a subgroup: $SO(3)$ to a point group in a crystal, a point group to one of its subgroups under strain. The subgroup criterion is then the practical test for which sets of surviving operations are consistent, and the degeneracy of the energy levels splits accordingly (:ref[Representations of groups]{to=group.representations}).

**Arithmetic.** $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$, the group of the example, is the computational backbone of public-key cryptography and of the fast algorithms for modular exponentiation; the order of an element there is the period of the corresponding repeating decimal or pseudorandom sequence.
:::
