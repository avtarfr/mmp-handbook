:::definition[Fixed point, contraction, iteration]{provenance=adapted src="kreyszig-1978:§5.1, pp. 299–300, and Def. 5.1-1, p. 300; stakgold-holst-2011:§4.4, pp. 245–246"}
Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space and $T : X \to X$ a map, not assumed linear.

- A **fixed point** of $T$ is a point $x$ with $Tx = x$.
- $T$ is **Lipschitz** on $X$ with constant $\rho \ge 0$ if $d(Tx,Ty) \le \rho\, d(x,y)$ for all $x,y \in X$.
- $T$ is a **contraction** on $X$ if it is Lipschitz with some constant $\alpha$ satisfying $\alpha < 1$. The smallest such $\alpha$ is the **contraction constant**.
- The **iteration** generated by $T$ from a starting point $x_0 \in X$ is the sequence
  $$
  x_{n+1} = Tx_n , \qquad n = 0,1,2,\dots,
  $$
  so that $x_n = T^n x_0$.

A contraction is continuous, indeed uniformly so: given $\varepsilon>0$, $d(x,y)<\varepsilon$ already forces $d(Tx,Ty) \le \alpha\varepsilon < \varepsilon$.

Solving an equation and finding a fixed point are the same problem in different clothing. Any equation $F(x)=0$ can be rewritten $x = x - \lambda F(x) =: Tx$ for a constant $\lambda \ne 0$, and more generally as $x = Tx$ in many ways; the art is choosing a rewriting for which $T$ is a contraction.
:::

:::note[Three books, three uses of the word]{provenance=adapted src="reed-simon-1980:§V.5, p. 151; stakgold-holst-2011:§4.4, pp. 245–246; kreyszig-1978:§5.1, Def. 5.1-1, p. 300; evans-2010:§9.2.1, p. 502"}
The terminology is not uniform in the library, and the difference is exactly the strictness of the inequality.

- :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§V.5, p. 151"} call $T$ a **contraction** when $\rho(Tx,Ty) \le \rho(x,y)$ — no shrinking required — and a **strict contraction** when there is a $K<1$. Their Theorem V.18 is about strict contractions.
- :cite[Evans]{work=evans-2010 loc="§9.2.1, p. 502"} likewise says **strict contraction** for the condition with $\gamma<1$.
- :cite[Kreyszig]{work=kreyszig-1978 loc="§5.1, Def. 5.1-1, p. 300"} and :cite[Stakgold and Holst]{work=stakgold-holst-2011 loc="§4.4, p. 246"} build $\alpha<1$ into the word **contraction** itself; Stakgold and Holst reserve **weak contraction** for the strict-inequality-without-a-constant condition $\lVert Tu - Tv\rVert < \lVert u-v\rVert$, $u \ne v$.

This unit follows Kreyszig and Stakgold-Holst: *contraction* means $\alpha<1$ throughout. Readers consulting Reed and Simon or Evans should read their "strict contraction" wherever this unit says "contraction". The distinction is not pedantry — the theorem below is false for a weak contraction, as the remark shows.
:::

:::statement[Banach's fixed point theorem, with error bounds]{provenance=adapted src="kreyszig-1978:§5.1, Thm 5.1-2, p. 300, Cor. 5.1-3, p. 302, and Thm 5.1-4, p. 303; stakgold-holst-2011:§4.4, pp. 246–248; reed-simon-1980:§V.5, Thm V.18, p. 151; evans-2010:§9.2.1, Thm 1, p. 502"}
**1. The theorem.** Let $(X,d)$ be a *nonempty complete* metric space and let $T : X \to X$ be a contraction with constant $\alpha<1$. Then $T$ has exactly one fixed point $x^\ast \in X$. For every starting point $x_0 \in X$ the iteration $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$ converges to $x^\ast$.

**2. Error bounds.** With $x_0$ arbitrary and $x_n = T^nx_0$, for every $m \ge 1$:

$$
\underbrace{d(x_m,x^\ast) \;\le\; \frac{\alpha^{m}}{1-\alpha}\, d(x_0,x_1)}_{\textbf{prior estimate}} ,
\qquad
\underbrace{d(x_m,x^\ast) \;\le\; \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\, d(x_{m-1},x_m)}_{\textbf{posterior estimate}} .
$$

The prior estimate uses only the first step and so predicts, before any computing, how many iterations a given accuracy needs. The posterior estimate uses the step just taken and is never worse than the prior one.

**3. Contraction on a ball.** Let $(X,d)$ be complete, $x_0 \in X$, $r>0$, and let $Y = \{x \in X : d(x,x_0) \le r\}$. Suppose $T : X \to X$ satisfies $d(Tx,Ty)\le\alpha\, d(x,y)$ for all $x,y \in Y$, with $\alpha<1$, and suppose

$$
d(x_0,Tx_0) \;<\; (1-\alpha)\,r .
$$

Then every iterate $x_n = T^nx_0$ lies in $Y$, the iteration converges to a point $x^\ast \in Y$, and $x^\ast$ is the unique fixed point of $T$ in $Y$. (Nothing is claimed about fixed points outside $Y$.)

**4. On a closed subset of a Banach space.** Let $X$ be a Banach space, $S \subseteq X$ a *closed* set, and $T : S \to S$ a contraction. Then $T$ has exactly one fixed point in $S$, reachable by iteration from any $u_0 \in S$. This is the form in which the theorem is usually applied, and it is part 1 applied to the complete metric space $S$ (see :ref[metric spaces and completeness]{to=funcan.metric-spaces}, part 5).

**5. Powers.** If $T$ maps a closed subset $S$ of a Banach space into itself and $T^k$ is a contraction for some integer $k\ge1$, then $T$ has exactly one fixed point in $S$ — even though $T$ itself need not be a contraction, and need not even be continuous.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
Each application of $T$ shrinks distances by at least the factor $\alpha$, so the successive steps of the iteration shrink geometrically: $d(x_m,x_{m+1}) \le \alpha^m d(x_0,x_1)$. The total distance travelled from step $m$ onward is therefore bounded by the tail of a geometric series, $\alpha^m/(1-\alpha)$ times the first step. A sequence whose remaining journey is summable is Cauchy, and completeness converts that into an actual limit. Continuity of $T$ then moves $T$ inside the limit, which is what makes the limit a fixed point.

Uniqueness is independent of all this and needs no completeness: if two points were both fixed, the map would have to shrink the distance between them while leaving it unchanged, so that distance is zero.

The error bounds are the geometric-series estimate itself, read in two ways. Letting the far index run to infinity in the Cauchy estimate gives the prior bound; restarting the count at $x_{m-1}$ rather than at $x_0$ gives the posterior bound.
:::

:::proof[Parts 1 and 2]{provenance=adapted src="kreyszig-1978:§5.1, Thm 5.1-2, pp. 300–302, and Cor. 5.1-3, p. 302; stakgold-holst-2011:§4.4, pp. 246–247"}
**Step 1: uniqueness, which uses only $\alpha<1$.** Suppose $Tx = x$ and $Ty = y$. Then $d(x,y) = d(Tx,Ty) \le \alpha\, d(x,y)$, so $(1-\alpha)d(x,y) \le 0$. Since $\alpha<1$ the factor is positive, so $d(x,y)=0$ and $x=y$ by axiom (M2). Neither completeness nor nonemptiness was used, so a contraction on *any* metric space has **at most** one fixed point.

**Step 2: consecutive steps shrink geometrically.** Fix $x_0 \in X$ (possible because $X \ne \varnothing$) and set $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$. The contraction property applied $n$ times gives
$$
d(x_n,x_{n+1}) = d(Tx_{n-1},Tx_n) \le \alpha\, d(x_{n-1},x_n) \le \cdots \le \alpha^{n} d(x_0,x_1) .
$$

**Step 3: the iteration is Cauchy.** For $n>m$, the generalized triangle inequality and step 2 give
$$
d(x_m,x_n) \le \sum_{j=m}^{n-1} d(x_j,x_{j+1})
\le \bigl(\alpha^m + \alpha^{m+1}+\cdots+\alpha^{n-1}\bigr)\,d(x_0,x_1)
= \alpha^m\,\frac{1-\alpha^{\,n-m}}{1-\alpha}\,d(x_0,x_1) .
$$
Because $0\le\alpha<1$ we have $1-\alpha^{\,n-m} < 1$, so

$$
d(x_m,x_n) \;\le\; \frac{\alpha^m}{1-\alpha}\,d(x_0,x_1) \qquad (n>m) . \tag{$\ast$}
$$

The right-hand side does not involve $n$ and tends to $0$ as $m\to\infty$, **and this is exactly where $\alpha<1$ is used a second time**: for $\alpha=1$ the sum in step 3 is $(n-m)d(x_0,x_1)$, which is unbounded. So $(x_m)$ is Cauchy.

**Step 4: a limit exists.** $X$ is complete, so $x_m \to x^\ast$ for some $x^\ast \in X$. **This is the only use of completeness.**

**Step 5: the limit is fixed.** $T$ is continuous (it is Lipschitz), so by sequential continuity (:ref[metric spaces]{to=funcan.metric-spaces}, part 6),
$$
Tx^\ast = T\bigl(\lim_m x_m\bigr) = \lim_m Tx_m = \lim_m x_{m+1} = x^\ast .
$$
With step 1, $x^\ast$ is the unique fixed point, and since $x_0$ was arbitrary every iteration converges to this same point.

**Step 6: the prior estimate.** Fix $m$ and let $n\to\infty$ in $(\ast)$. The constant sequence $x_m,x_m,\dots$ converges to $x_m$ and $x_n \to x^\ast$ by step 4, so $d(x_m,x_n) \to d(x_m,x^\ast)$ by the joint continuity of the metric (part 1 of :ref[metric spaces]{to=funcan.metric-spaces}, third claim), while the right side of $(\ast)$ does not involve $n$:
$$
d(x_m,x^\ast) \le \frac{\alpha^m}{1-\alpha}\,d(x_0,x_1) .
$$

**Step 7: the posterior estimate.** Apply the prior estimate with $x_{m-1}$ in place of $x_0$. The iteration started at $x_{m-1}$ is $x_{m-1},x_m,x_{m+1},\dots$ and has the same limit $x^\ast$, so taking the prior estimate at its own first step, that is with exponent $1$,
$$
d(x_m,x^\ast) \le \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\, d(x_{m-1},x_m) .
$$
Since $d(x_{m-1},x_m) \le \alpha^{m-1}d(x_0,x_1)$ by step 2, the posterior bound is at most the prior bound; it is usually smaller, because step 2 is an inequality. $\square$
:::

:::proof[Parts 3, 4 and 5]{provenance=adapted src="kreyszig-1978:§5.1, Thm 5.1-4, p. 303; stakgold-holst-2011:§4.4, pp. 246–248"}
**Part 3.** The one thing to check is that nothing leaves $Y$, since only there is $T$ known to contract. The hypothesis is a statement about the single point $x_0$, and the first move is to turn it into a statement about all of $Y$.

*$T$ maps $Y$ into $Y$.* Let $y \in Y$, so $d(x_0,y)\le r$. Insert $Tx_0$ and use the contraction bound, which is available because $x_0$ and $y$ both lie in $Y$:
$$
d(x_0,Ty) \;\le\; d(x_0,Tx_0) + d(Tx_0,Ty) \;\le\; d(x_0,Tx_0) + \alpha\, d(x_0,y) \;<\; (1-\alpha)r + \alpha r \;=\; r .
$$
So $Ty \in Y$, and $T|_Y$ is a contraction of $Y$ into itself. Since $x_0 \in Y$, induction gives $x_n = T^nx_0 \in Y$ for every $n$; the orbit never escapes, and steps 2 and 3 of the previous proof apply to it verbatim.

*The conclusion.* $Y$ is closed in $X$, being where the continuous function $x \mapsto d(x,x_0)$ stays at or below $r$, and $X$ is complete, so $Y$ is a complete metric space by part 5 of :ref[metric spaces]{to=funcan.metric-spaces}. It is nonempty, containing $x_0$. Part 1 applied to $T|_Y : Y \to Y$ therefore gives a unique fixed point $x^\ast \in Y$, reached by the iteration from $x_0$. Nothing has been said about $X \setminus Y$, where $T$ need not contract.

*What the hypothesis buys quantitatively.* Taking $m=0$ in the unsimplified estimate of step 3, before $1-\alpha^{\,n}$ was discarded, and using $x_1 = Tx_0$,
$$
d(x_0,x_n) \;\le\; \frac{1-\alpha^{n}}{1-\alpha}\, d(x_0,x_1) \;\le\; \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\,d(x_0,Tx_0) \;<\; \frac{(1-\alpha)r}{1-\alpha} = r ,
$$
so the whole orbit, and with it $x^\ast$, stays strictly inside the ball: the bound $d(x_0,Tx_0)<(1-\alpha)r$ is exactly what leaves room for the geometric series.

**Part 4.** A closed subset $S$ of a Banach space is a complete metric space in the induced metric $d(u,v) = \lVert u-v\rVert$ (:ref[metric spaces]{to=funcan.metric-spaces}, part 5). Apply part 1 to $(S,d)$. **Closedness is what is doing the work**: it is the hypothesis that makes the limit of the iterates, which certainly exists in $X$, belong to $S$.

**Part 5.** Let $a$ be the unique fixed point of the contraction $T^k$. Applying $T$ to $T^k a = a$ gives $T^{k}(Ta) = T(T^k a) = Ta$, so $Ta$ is also a fixed point of $T^k$; uniqueness forces $Ta = a$, so $a$ is fixed by $T$. Conversely any fixed point of $T$ is fixed by $T^k$, hence equals $a$. Note that continuity of $T$ was never needed — only of $T^k$, which is automatic. $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used, and what fails without them]{provenance=adapted src="kreyszig-1978:§5.1, Thm 5.1-2, pp. 300–302, and Problem 4, p. 304; stakgold-holst-2011:§4.4, the remark on p. 247 and Corollary 4.4.2, p. 248"}
Each hypothesis is used at an identifiable point of the proof, and dropping it kills the theorem.

- **Completeness** enters only at step 4. Without it the iteration still has all the good properties — it is Cauchy, with the same geometric bounds — but may have nowhere to converge to. :cite[Stakgold and Holst's]{work=stakgold-holst-2011 loc="§4.4, p. 247"} example is $S = (0,1)$ with $Tu = u/2$: a contraction with $\alpha=\tfrac12$, whose iterates head for $0$, which is not in $S$. The failure is of existence only; uniqueness (step 1) survives.
- **$T$ must map the space into itself.** Otherwise the iteration is not defined after the first step. In part 3 this is the content of the hypothesis $d(x_0,Tx_0)<(1-\alpha)r$, which is a statement about one point and nevertheless keeps the whole orbit inside the ball.
- **$\alpha<1$ strictly, not $\alpha\le1$.** It is used twice, in step 1 and in step 3, and the second use is the one that breaks. The standard counterexample, :cite[Kreyszig's Problem 4]{work=kreyszig-1978 loc="§5.1, Problem 4, p. 304"} and also :cite[Stakgold and Holst's]{work=stakgold-holst-2011 loc="§4.4, p. 247"}, is
  $$
  X = [1,\infty), \qquad Tx = x + \frac1x .
  $$
  $X$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}$ and hence complete. By the mean value theorem $\lvert Tx-Ty\rvert = \lvert 1 - c^{-2}\rvert\,\lvert x-y\rvert$ for some $c$ between $x$ and $y$, and $0 \le 1-c^{-2} < 1$ for $c \ge 1$, so $T$ strictly decreases every distance: $\lvert Tx-Ty\rvert < \lvert x-y\rvert$ whenever $x \ne y$. But $Tx=x$ would need $1/x = 0$, so there is no fixed point at all. There is no single $\alpha<1$ here, because $1-c^{-2}\to1$ as $c\to\infty$: *uniform* shrinking is the hypothesis, not shrinking.
- **The identity map and a rotation** show the two ways $\alpha=1$ can fail: the identity on any $S$ has every point fixed, and :cite[a rotation of a closed annulus]{work=stakgold-holst-2011 loc="§4.4, p. 247"} through a nonzero angle less than $2\pi$ has none, while both are isometries.
- **Compactness repairs the weak case.** A weak contraction on a *compact* set does have a fixed point, since $x\mapsto d(x,Tx)$ is then continuous on a compact set and attains its minimum, which must be $0$. Compactness is a strong hypothesis and is what the $[1,\infty)$ example lacks.
- **Continuity of $T$ is not a separate assumption** in parts 1–4; it follows from the Lipschitz bound. It *is* a real gain in part 5: a wildly discontinuous $T$ with a contractive power still has a unique fixed point, which is why one often checks $T^k$ rather than $T$.
- **The error bounds need no extra hypotheses**, but they do need the contraction constant $\alpha$, not merely the existence of one. A bound computed with an overestimate $\alpha' > \alpha$ is still valid (both sides increase), so in practice one uses any $\alpha$ one can certify.
:::

:::method[Applying the theorem]{provenance=original}
1. **Rewrite the problem as $u = Tu$.** For $F(u)=0$ the family $T_\lambda u = u - \lambda F(u)$ gives one rewriting per $\lambda$; for a differential equation, integrate it to get an integral equation, because integration maps continuous functions to continuous functions whereas differentiation does not.
2. **Choose the space.** Pick a complete metric space, or a closed subset $S$ of a Banach space, and *check that $T$ maps it into itself*. For an unbounded nonlinearity this usually means shrinking $S$ to a ball and verifying the hypothesis of part 3.
3. **Estimate the contraction constant.** On an interval of $\mathbb{R}$, $\alpha = \sup\lvert T'\rvert$ by the mean value theorem; for an integral operator with kernel $k$, $\alpha \le \sup_s \int \lvert k(s,t)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}t$ in the sup norm; for a matrix iteration $x \mapsto Cx + b$, $\alpha = \lVert C\rVert$ in whichever induced norm is being used.
4. **If $\alpha \ge 1$, do not give up.** Three standard repairs: shrink the domain (a small interval, a small time horizon, a small parameter — this is the pattern :cite[Evans]{work=evans-2010 loc="§9.2.1, pp. 502–503"} identifies in nonlinear partial differential equations); change to an equivalent metric that is weighted so as to make $\alpha<1$; or apply part 5 to a power $T^k$.
5. **Iterate and stop on the posterior bound.** Compute $\alpha\,d(x_{m-1},x_m)/(1-\alpha)$ after each step and stop when it is below tolerance. Use the prior bound beforehand to budget the number of steps: $m \ge \log\bigl[\varepsilon(1-\alpha)/d(x_0,x_1)\bigr]/\log\alpha$ guarantees accuracy $\varepsilon$.
6. **Remember what is proved.** Existence, uniqueness *in the set chosen*, and a rate. A fixed point outside that set is not excluded, and the convergence rate is only linear, with error multiplied by about $\alpha$ each step — quadratically convergent methods such as Newton's do better precisely because their contraction constant tends to $0$ near the root.
:::

:::example[Kepler's equation, with both error bounds]{provenance=original}
A body on an elliptical orbit of eccentricity $e$ has eccentric anomaly $E$ related to the mean anomaly $M$ (which advances uniformly in time) by **Kepler's equation**

$$
E - e\sin E = M .
$$

There is no elementary solution for $E$. Rewrite it as a fixed-point problem,

$$
E = T(E) := M + e\sin E ,
$$

and take $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the usual metric, which is complete, and $T : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 1: the contraction constant.** $T'(E) = e\cos E$, so $\lvert T'\rvert \le e$ everywhere, and by the mean value theorem $\lvert T(E)-T(F)\rvert \le e\,\lvert E-F\rvert$. For any bound orbit $e<1$, so $T$ is a contraction with $\alpha = e$, and Kepler's equation has exactly one solution for every $M$. (For $e \to 1$ the bound degrades, which is the numerical difficulty with near-parabolic orbits.)

**Step 2: numbers.** Take $e = 0.3$ and $M = 0.5$ radians, so $\alpha = 0.3$ and $\alpha/(1-\alpha) = 3/7 = 0.4285714$. Start at $x_0 = M = 0.5$:

| $m$ | $x_m$ |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $0.500000000000$ |
| $1$ | $0.643827661581$ |
| $2$ | $0.680078363070$ |
| $3$ | $0.688656186522$ |
| $4$ | $0.690650059101$ |
| $5$ | $0.691111520955$ |
| $6$ | $0.691218213482$ |

The first step is $d(x_0,x_1) = 0.143827661581$.

**Step 3: the prior bound at $m=6$.**
$$
d(x_6,E^\ast) \le \frac{\alpha^6}{1-\alpha}\,d(x_0,x_1) = \frac{0.000729}{0.7}\times 0.143827661581 = 1.49786\times10^{-4} .
$$

**Step 4: the posterior bound at $m=6$.** $d(x_5,x_6) = 1.066925\times10^{-4}$, so
$$
d(x_6,E^\ast) \le \tfrac37 \times 1.066925\times10^{-4} = 4.57254\times10^{-5} ,
$$
about three times sharper than the prior bound, as advertised.

**Step 5: the true error.** Solving $E - 0.3\sin E = 0.5$ to high precision gives
$$
E^\ast = 0.691250289594 ,
$$
so the actual error at $m=6$ is $3.20761\times10^{-5}$. Both bounds hold, the posterior one by a factor $1.43$ and the prior one by a factor $4.67$.

**Step 6: budgeting in advance.** To reach $\varepsilon = 10^{-10}$ the prior bound asks for
$$
m \ \ge\ \frac{\ln\bigl[\varepsilon(1-\alpha)/d(x_0,x_1)\bigr]}{\ln\alpha} = \frac{\ln(4.8669\times10^{-10})}{\ln 0.3} = 17.81 ,
$$
so $m=18$ suffices; the bound there is $7.96\times10^{-11}$, while $m=17$ gives $2.653\times10^{-10}$ and is not enough. This is the whole practical value of the prior estimate: the iteration count is known before a single sine is evaluated.
:::

:::application{provenance=original}
**Existence for differential equations.** :cite[Kreyszig]{work=kreyszig-1978 loc="§5.3, Thm 5.3-1, pp. 315–317"} proves Picard's theorem by this route. For $x'=f(t,x)$, $x(t_0)=x_0$ with $f$ continuous and bounded by $c$ on a rectangle $\lvert t-t_0\rvert\le a$, $\lvert x-x_0\rvert\le b$, and Lipschitz in $x$ with constant $k$, the map
$$
(Tx)(t) = x_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} f\bigl(\tau,x(\tau)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
$$
sends the closed subset $\{x \in C(J) : \lvert x(t)-x_0\rvert \le c\beta\}$ of $C(J)$, $J=[t_0-\beta,t_0+\beta]$, into itself and contracts it with $\alpha = k\beta$, provided
$$
\beta < \min\{a,\ b/c,\ 1/k\} .
$$
The fixed point is the solution. :ref[Existence and uniqueness of solutions]{to=ode.existence-uniqueness} proves the same theorem by the telescoping-series route of Coddington and gets the longer interval $\alpha_{\text{C}} = \min\{a, b/M\}$ with no $1/k$ term; Kreyszig flags this himself in a footnote, noting that the classical interval is better and that a weighted metric would recover it. The two statements are both correct and are not the same statement, so a reader needing the largest guaranteed interval should use the ODE unit's version.

**Integral equations.** For a Fredholm equation $u(s) = g(s) + \lambda\int_a^b k(s,t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$, the right-hand side defines $T$ on $C[a,b]$ with $\alpha = \lvert\lambda\rvert\,\sup_s\int_a^b\lvert k(s,t)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}t$. Where $\alpha<1$ the iterates are precisely the partial sums of the :ref[Neumann series]{to=green.neumann-series}, so the theorem is the convergence proof for that series and the prior bound is its truncation error. For :ref[Volterra equations]{to=green.volterra-equations} the factor $t-a$ makes some power $T^k$ a contraction even when $T$ is not, which is part 5 in action.

**Nonlinear partial differential equations.** :cite[Evans]{work=evans-2010 loc="§9.2.1, pp. 502–503"} solves a reaction-diffusion system $\mathbf{u}_t - \Delta\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})$ by contraction in $C([0,T];L^2)$, with the Lipschitz constant of $\mathbf{f}$ times $T$ playing the role of $\alpha$; one solves on a short time interval and restarts. The restart loses nothing here: the length $T_1$ of the usable interval depends only on the Lipschitz constant of $\mathbf{f}$, so finitely many repetitions reach an arbitrary prescribed $T$, and :cite[Evans' Theorem 2]{work=evans-2010 loc="§9.2.1, Thm 2, pp. 503–505"} concludes with a unique weak solution on all of $[0,T]$. What the contraction step by itself delivers is short-time existence; it becomes global existence only because the step size does not shrink. Evans notes that a small parameter of this kind is the signature of every such proof, and that the globally Lipschitz hypothesis which keeps $T_1$ fixed is unrealistic — for a polynomial nonlinearity the restart time does shrink, and whether the solution lives forever or blows up is then a genuine question.

**Numerical linear algebra.** The splitting $Ax=b \Rightarrow x = Cx + d$ underlying the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel iterations is a contraction exactly when $\lVert C\rVert<1$ in some induced norm, and the theorem then gives both convergence and the stopping rule; see :ref[iterative methods for linear systems]{to=num.linear-systems-numerics} and :ref[relaxation methods]{to=num.relaxation-methods}. The same bound governs fixed-point iteration for scalar equations and the local convergence of :ref[Newton's method]{to=num.root-finding}, where $T(x)=x-f(x)/f'(x)$ has $T'(x^\ast)=0$ at a simple root, so $\alpha$ can be made as small as one likes by starting close enough.

**Orbital mechanics and ephemerides.** The worked example is the standard way Kepler's equation is solved in practice for moderate eccentricity; the contraction constant *is* the eccentricity, which explains both why the iteration is fast for planets ($e \approx 0.0167$ for Earth, giving an error reduction of about $60$ per step) and why comets need a different method.

**Self-similar sets.** A finite family of contractions on a complete metric space induces a contraction on the space of compact subsets with the Hausdorff metric; its unique fixed point is the attractor, which is how the Cantor set, the Koch curve and the other objects of :ref[fractals]{to=dyn.fractals} are defined and drawn.
