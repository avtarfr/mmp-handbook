:::note[What the theorem does]{provenance=original}
A Hermitian matrix can be diagonalised: there is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and in that basis the matrix acts by multiplying each coordinate by a real number (:ref[]{to=linalg.hermitian-spectral-theorem}). A compact self-adjoint operator behaves the same way, with a sequence of eigenvalues tending to zero (:ref[]{to=funcan.compact-self-adjoint}). But most of the operators that matter in physics have no eigenvectors at all. Multiplication by the coordinate on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ has none, and neither does $-i\,d/dx$; their spectra are the whole real line, filled with "eigenvalues" whose "eigenfunctions" are not square-integrable.

The spectral theorem says that nothing essential is lost. Every self-adjoint operator, bounded or not, still *is* a multiplication operator - not on a sequence space indexed by eigenvalues, but on an $L^2$ space over a measure space that the operator itself determines. Sums over an eigenbasis become integrals against a measure. Once that is known, one can form $h(A)$ for any bounded Borel function $h$, and in particular the spectral projections $P_\Omega$ that answer "how much of $\psi$ lives in the part of the spectrum lying in $\Omega$?" - the question quantum mechanics asks of every measurement.
:::

:::definition[Multiplication operators, spectral representations, spectral measures]{provenance=adapted src="reed-simon-1980:§VII.2, the definitions of cyclic vector and of spectral measure, pp. 226-228, the essential range, p. 229, and §VIII.3, Proposition 1 and the p.v.m. axioms, pp. 259, 263"}
Throughout, $\mathcal{H}$ is a complex separable Hilbert space, and a non-zero one, $\mathcal{H}\ne\{0\}$ - the exclusion is needed only in Step 1, which uses the non-emptiness of the spectrum - and the inner product $(\varphi,\psi)$ is linear in the first slot, as elsewhere in this handbook (:ref[]{to=linalg.inner-product-spaces}). An operator $A$ is **self-adjoint** when $A = A^{*}$ as operators *with their domains*, so that $\mathcal{D}(A) = \mathcal{D}(A^{*})$; symmetry alone is not enough (:ref[]{to=funcan.symmetric-self-adjoint}).

**Multiplication operator.** Let $(M,\mu)$ be a measure space and let $f : M \to \mathbb{C}$ be measurable and finite $\mu$-almost everywhere. The **multiplication operator** $T_f$ on $L^2(M,d\mu)$ is
$$
(T_f \varphi)(m) \;=\; f(m)\,\varphi(m) , \qquad
\mathcal{D}(T_f) \;=\; \bigl\{ \varphi \in L^2(M,d\mu) \;:\; f\varphi \in L^2(M,d\mu) \bigr\} .
$$
The domain is forced on us: if $f$ is unbounded, $f\varphi$ leaves $L^2$ for some $\varphi$, and there is nothing to be done about it. Complex-valued $f$ is allowed here because the proof below passes through $T_g$ with $g$ bounded and complex (Step 10) and through $T_{h\circ f}$ with $h$ a bounded complex Borel function (Step 13). On a finite measure space $T_f$ is self-adjoint exactly when $f$ is real almost everywhere - one direction is Step 8, the other the third part of Step 11 - and part A of the theorem asserts a real $f$.

**Essential range.** A number $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ lies in the **essential range** of $f$ if
$$
\mu\bigl( \{ m : \lvert f(m) - \lambda\rvert < \varepsilon \} \bigr) \;>\; 0 \qquad \text{for every } \varepsilon > 0 .
$$
This is the set of values $f$ takes "on a set of positive measure, up to an arbitrarily small error"; changing $f$ on a null set does not change it. :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VII.2, p. 229"} define the essential range only for a real-valued $f$ and a real $\lambda$, with the condition written as $\mu(\{m : \lambda - \varepsilon < f(m) < \lambda + \varepsilon\}) > 0$; for real $f$ and $\lambda$ that is the same condition, and the form with a modulus, which makes sense for complex $f$ and complex $\lambda$, is ours. The widening is used in one place only: it is what lets part B compare $\sigma(A)$, a subset of $\mathbb{C}$ a priori, with the essential range and so conclude $\sigma(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. The $f$ it is applied to is the real one of part A.

**Spectral representation.** A **spectral representation** of a self-adjoint operator $A$ is a triple $\bigl((M,\mu),\,U,\,f\bigr)$ with $U : \mathcal{H} \to L^2(M,d\mu)$ unitary and $U A U^{-1} = T_f$, domains included.

**Cyclic vector.** A vector $\psi \in \mathcal{H}$ is **cyclic** for a bounded self-adjoint $A$ if the finite linear combinations of $\psi, A\psi, A^2\psi, \dots$ are dense in $\mathcal{H}$. For unbounded $A$, :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, p. 262"} use instead the condition that $\{ g(A)\psi : g \in C_\infty(\mathbb{R})\}$ be dense, $C_\infty$ being the continuous functions vanishing at infinity.

**Projection-valued measure.** A family $\{P_\Omega\}$ of operators indexed by the Borel sets $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is a **projection-valued measure** (p.v.m.) if

1. every $P_\Omega$ is an orthogonal projection;
2. $P_{\varnothing} = 0$ and $P_{\mathbb{R}} = I$;
3. if $\Omega = \bigcup_{n\ge 1}\Omega_n$ with the $\Omega_n$ pairwise disjoint, then $P_\Omega = \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N} P_{\Omega_n}$ in the strong sense, that is, applied to each fixed vector;
4. $P_{\Omega_1}P_{\Omega_2} = P_{\Omega_1 \cap \Omega_2}$.

**Spectral measure of a vector.** Given a p.v.m. and $\psi \in \mathcal{H}$, put
$$
\mu_\psi(\Omega) \;=\; (P_\Omega \psi, \psi) \;=\; \lVert P_\Omega \psi\rVert^2 ,
$$
the second equality because $P_\Omega$ is an orthogonal projection, so $P_\Omega = P_\Omega^{*} = P_\Omega^2$. By axioms 1-3, $\mu_\psi$ is a finite positive Borel measure on $\mathbb{R}$ of total mass $\lVert\psi\rVert^2$; it is the **spectral measure** of $\psi$. Because $\mu_\psi(\Omega)$ is a squared norm it is real, so it does not matter which slot of the inner product is taken linear - a point worth recording, since the sources differ (see the note below).
:::

:::statement[The spectral theorem]{provenance=adapted src="reed-simon-1980:§VIII.3, Proposition 1, p. 259, Thm VIII.4, pp. 260-261, Thm VIII.5, p. 262, Thm VIII.6 and Proposition 3, pp. 263 and 264; reed-simon-1980:§VII.2, Thm VII.3 and the essential-range proposition, pp. 227-229"}
Let $A$ be a self-adjoint operator on a complex separable Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$, with domain $\mathcal{D}(A)$.

**A. Multiplication-operator form.** There are a measure space $(M,\mu)$ with $\mu$ a **finite** measure, a unitary $U : \mathcal{H} \to L^2(M,d\mu)$, and a real-valued measurable $f$ on $M$, finite $\mu$-a.e., such that

1. $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$ if and only if $f\cdot(U\psi) \in L^2(M,d\mu)$, and
2. $\bigl(UAU^{-1}\varphi\bigr)(m) = f(m)\varphi(m)$ for every $\varphi \in U[\mathcal{D}(A)]$.

Conversely every $T_f$ of the definition above, on a finite measure space and with $f$ real and a.e. finite, is self-adjoint; so "self-adjoint operator" and "multiplication by a real function" name the same class of objects up to unitary equivalence. The measure space and $f$ may in addition be chosen so that $f \in L^p(M,d\mu)$ for every $p$ with $1 \le p < \infty$. That last clause is no part of Theorem VIII.4, which says nothing about the integrability of $f$; it is the separate Proposition 3 of :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, Proposition 3, p. 264"}, and we record it with the rest because their Proposition 2 (p. 260), which identifies the cores of $T_f$, assumes it. Step 11 gives their proof.

**B. The spectrum.** $\sigma(A)$ is the essential range of $f$. In particular $\sigma(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, as it must be for a self-adjoint operator (:ref[]{to=funcan.spectrum}).

**C. Borel functional calculus.** There is exactly one map $h \mapsto h(A)$ from the bounded Borel functions on $\mathbb{R}$ into the bounded operators on $\mathcal{H}$ with these properties:

1. it is an algebraic $*$-homomorphism: $(h_1h_2)(A) = h_1(A)h_2(A)$, $(\alpha h)(A) = \alpha\,h(A)$, $1(A) = I$ and $\bar h(A) = h(A)^{*}$;
2. it is norm-continuous, $\lVert h(A)\rVert \le \lVert h\rVert_\infty$;
3. if $h_n$ are bounded Borel functions with $h_n(x) \to x$ for every $x$ and $\lvert h_n(x)\rvert \le \lvert x\rvert$ for all $x$ and $n$, then $h_n(A)\psi \to A\psi$ for every $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$;
4. if $h_n \to h$ pointwise and $\sup_n \lVert h_n\rVert_\infty < \infty$, then $h_n(A) \to h(A)$ strongly.

It automatically satisfies two more: if $A\psi = \lambda\psi$ then $h(A)\psi = h(\lambda)\psi$; and $h \ge 0$ implies $h(A) \ge 0$. Concretely $h(A) = U^{-1} T_{h \circ f} U$.

**D. Projection-valued-measure form.** Setting $P_\Omega = \chi_\Omega(A)$ for Borel $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ gives a projection-valued measure, and
$$
A \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}} \lambda \, dP_\lambda ,
$$
meaning that $\mathcal{D}(A) = \bigl\{\psi : \int \lambda^2 \, d\mu_\psi(\lambda) < \infty\bigr\}$ and $(A\psi,\varphi) = \int \lambda \, d(P_\lambda\psi,\varphi)$ for $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$ and $\varphi \in \mathcal{H}$. :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="Thm VIII.6, p. 263"} state this for $\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$, which is what polarisation gives directly, since polarising $(A\cdot,\cdot)$ needs $\psi + i^{k}\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$. The extension to every $\varphi \in \mathcal{H}$ is ours; it rests on a Cauchy-Schwarz estimate made inside the multiplication representation, which produces a positive finite measure dominating the complex measure $\Omega \mapsto (P_\Omega\psi,\varphi)$ and integrating $\lvert\lambda\rvert$ to at most $\lVert A\psi\rVert\,\lVert\varphi\rVert$, so that the integral converges absolutely for every $\varphi\in\mathcal{H}$; it is proved in Step 14. This correspondence is one-to-one: every projection-valued measure on $\mathbb{R}$ is the one belonging to exactly one self-adjoint operator. More generally, for a real Borel $g$ (not necessarily bounded), $g(A) = \int g(\lambda)\,dP_\lambda$ is self-adjoint on $\mathcal{D}_g = \bigl\{\psi : \int \lvert g(\lambda)\rvert^2 d\mu_\psi(\lambda) < \infty \bigr\}$. The construction below builds the calculus for *bounded* Borel functions and then treats the single unbounded function $g(\lambda)=\lambda$ in Step 14; the unbounded calculus in this generality is quoted from :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="Thm VIII.5 and the discussion of unbounded functions of a self-adjoint operator, pp. 262-263"} rather than proved here, and it is listed with the other quoted results in Step 10. For bounded $h$ it agrees with part C:
$$
\bigl( h(A)\psi, \psi \bigr) \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}} h(\lambda)\, d\mu_\psi(\lambda) .
$$

**E. Direct-sum form.** *(Proved below for bounded $A$; for unbounded $A$ it is quoted from :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, the cyclic-vector paragraph following Thm VIII.5, p. 262, which realises the measure space of Thm VIII.4, pp. 260-261, as a union of copies of $\mathbb{R}$"}, who assert it there without proof.)* One may take $(M,\mu)$ to be a disjoint union of at most countably many copies of $\mathbb{R}$, so that $U$ becomes a unitary from $\mathcal{H}$ onto $\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N} L^2(\mathbb{R}, d\mu_n)$, with $N \in \{1,2,\dots\} \cup \{\infty\}$, carrying $A$ to multiplication by the variable $\lambda$ in each summand. The measures $\mu_n$ are the spectral measures of a family of cyclic vectors, and $N = 1$ is possible exactly when $A$ has a cyclic vector. One direction is Steps 5-7; the other is read off any representation with $N = 1$, since in $L^2(\mathbb{R},d\mu_1)$ with $A$ multiplication by $\lambda$ the constant function $1$ is cyclic - it lies in $L^2$ because $\mu_1$ is finite, and $\{g\cdot 1 : g\in C_\infty(\mathbb{R})\}$ contains $C_c(\mathbb{R})$, which is dense in $L^2(\mathbb{R},d\mu_1)$; when $\mu_1$ has compact support the polynomials in $\lambda$ serve as well. "Cyclic" is meant in the sense of the definition above that fits the case at hand: for bounded $A$ the polynomial sense, which is what Steps 5-7 use, and for unbounded $A$ the $C_\infty$ sense that Reed and Simon use on p. 262, the polynomial sense being unavailable there because $A^{n}\psi$ need not be defined for every $\psi$ once $n \ge 1$, so the span of $\{A^{n}\psi\}$ is not available for an arbitrary $\psi$. It is not that $C^{\infty}$ vectors are scarce — $\bigcap_{n}\mathcal{D}(A^{n})$ is in fact dense for every self-adjoint $A$: in the representation of part A the vector $\psi_k = P_{[-k,k]}\psi$ has $U\psi_k = \chi_{[-k,k]}(f)\,U\psi$, so $\lvert f^{m}\,U\psi_k\rvert \le k^{m}\lvert U\psi\rvert \in L^2$ for every $m$, and conditions 1 and 2 of part A, applied $n$ times over, then put $\psi_k$ in $\mathcal{D}(A^{n})$ for every $n$, while $\psi_k \to \psi$ by axiom 3 — but that the cyclicity has to be asked of a class of vectors on which the whole calculus acts, which is why the definition uses $\{g(A)\psi : g \in C_\infty(\mathbb{R})\}$ instead.
:::

:::note[The spectral-family form, and the two sources]{provenance=adapted src="kreyszig-1978:§9.7, Definition 9.7-1 with (7)-(9), pp. 494-495; kreyszig-1978:§9.9, Thm 9.9-1 with (1) and (1*), pp. 505-506; kreyszig-1978:§10.6, the Cayley transform (1) and Thm 10.6-3 with (8), pp. 556-561"}
There is a second standard way to package part D, used by :cite[Kreyszig]{work=kreyszig-1978 loc="§9.7 and §10.6, pp. 494-495, 558-561"} and by much of the older literature. A **spectral family** (or **decomposition of unity**) is a one-parameter family $(E_\lambda)_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}$ of projections with
$$
E_\lambda E_\nu = E_{\min(\lambda,\nu)} , \qquad
E_\lambda \to 0 \ \ (\lambda\to-\infty) , \qquad
E_\lambda \to I \ \ (\lambda\to+\infty) , \qquad
E_{\nu} \to E_\lambda \ \ (\nu \to \lambda{+}0) ,
$$
the last three limits taken strongly. A spectral family and a projection-valued measure are the same data: $E_\lambda = P_{(-\infty,\lambda]}$ and, conversely, $P_{(a,b]} = E_b - E_a$. Kreyszig's Theorem 10.6-3 then states, for a densely defined self-adjoint $T$,
$$
(Tx,x) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \lambda \, dv(\lambda) , \qquad v(\lambda) = (F_\lambda x, x) , \qquad x \in \mathcal{D}(T),
$$
an ordinary Riemann-Stieltjes integral, where $F_\lambda = E_{2\arctan\lambda}$ and $(E_\theta)$ is the spectral family in the spectral representation of $-U$, with $U = (T-iI)(T+iI)^{-1}$ the Cayley transform of $T$. The minus sign is Kreyszig's and is load-bearing. His Lemma 10.6-2 (p. 557) shows that $1$ is not an eigenvalue of $U$, hence that $-1$ is not an eigenvalue of $-U$; that is what lets him prove $(E_\theta)$ continuous at $\theta = \pm\pi$, and the continuity at the endpoints is what makes the substitution $\theta = 2\arctan\lambda$, which sweeps $(-\pi,\pi)$ as $\lambda$ sweeps $\mathbb{R}$, lose nothing.

Two differences between the sources should be recorded, because they look like disagreements and are not.

- **Which slot is linear.** :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§II.1, p. 37"} take the inner product conjugate-linear in the first slot and write the functional calculus as $(\psi, h(A)\psi) = \int h \, d\mu_\psi$. Kreyszig, and this handbook, take it linear in the first slot, giving $(h(A)\psi,\psi)$. These two *expressions* denote the same number, because the conjugation sits in the first slot for them and in the second for us. What is not true, and would be an easy slip, is that the two orders agree inside one convention: in ours $(\psi, h(A)\psi) = \overline{(h(A)\psi,\psi)}$, and for complex-valued $h$ those differ. So the order has to be written as each book writes it; we write $(h(A)\psi,\psi) = \int h\,d\mu_\psi$ throughout. For real $h$ both numbers are real and equal, which is why $\mu_\psi$ itself is free of the convention.
- **Which route to the proof.** Reed and Simon reduce the unbounded case to the bounded one through the *resolvent* $(A+i)^{-1}$; Kreyszig reduces it through the *Cayley transform* $(A-i)(A+i)^{-1}$, which is unitary rather than merely bounded and normal. The two are visibly relatives of one another, and they deliver the same object. We follow Reed and Simon, because their route produces the multiplication-operator form directly, and the multiplication-operator form is the one that makes the functional calculus a triviality.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
**First make sense of $h(A)$ for polynomials, then for everything else.** For a bounded self-adjoint $A$ there is one identity that does all the work: for a polynomial $P$,
$$
\lVert P(A)\rVert \;=\; \sup_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} \lvert P(\lambda)\rvert .
$$
The left-hand side is an operator norm, the right-hand side a supremum of numbers; the identity says that the map $P \mapsto P(A)$ is an isometry for the supremum norm on $\sigma(A)$. Polynomials are dense in $C(\sigma(A))$ by Stone-Weierstrass, so the map extends to all continuous functions by continuity, and we have a continuous functional calculus.

**Numbers out of operators: the spectral measures.** For fixed $\psi$, the assignment $h \mapsto (h(A)\psi,\psi)$ is a positive linear functional on $C(\sigma(A))$. The Riesz-Markov theorem turns a positive linear functional on continuous functions into integration against a measure, and that measure $\mu_\psi$ is the whole content of $A$ as seen from $\psi$.

**From measures back to a multiplication operator.** If a single $\psi$ is cyclic, the map $h(A)\psi \mapsto h$ sends $\mathcal{H}$ onto $L^2(\sigma(A), d\mu_\psi)$ and is isometric by the previous paragraph; under it $A$ becomes multiplication by $\lambda$. A general $A$ splits $\mathcal{H}$ into countably many subspaces on each of which some vector is cyclic, and the pieces are reassembled as a direct sum.

**The unbounded case costs one trick.** If $A$ is self-adjoint and unbounded, $A \pm i$ are bijections of $\mathcal{D}(A)$ onto $\mathcal{H}$ with bounded inverses, and $R = (A+i)^{-1}$ is a bounded *normal* operator with trivial kernel. Diagonalise $R$ - which takes the bounded spectral theorem one step past the self-adjoint case, to bounded *normal* operators, a step we quote from Reed and Simon rather than prove: $R$ becomes multiplication by some bounded $g \ne 0$ a.e. Then $A$ must be multiplication by $f = g^{-1} - i$, which is finite a.e. but need not be bounded - exactly as it should be. The domain condition $f\cdot(U\psi) \in L^2$ is nothing but a restatement of $\psi \in \mathrm{Ran}\,R$.
:::

:::proof[Part A for bounded operators]{provenance=adapted src="reed-simon-1980:§VII.1, Thm VII.1 with Lemmas 1 and 2, pp. 222-224; reed-simon-1980:§VII.2, Lemma 1 with its proof, Lemma 2 and Thm VII.3 with its corollary, pp. 226-228; reed-simon-1980:§VI.3, Thm VI.6, p. 192; folland-1999:Thm 4.51, p. 141, Thm 7.2, p. 212, and Thm 7.8 with Prop. 7.9, p. 217"}
Let $A$ be bounded and self-adjoint, so $\sigma(A)$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}$ (:ref[]{to=funcan.spectrum}).

**Step 1: the spectrum of a polynomial in $A$.** For any bounded operator $B$ and polynomial $P$ one has $\sigma(P(B)) = \{P(\lambda) : \lambda \in \sigma(B)\}$. For "$\supseteq$": $P(x) - P(\lambda)$ vanishes at $x=\lambda$, so $P(x)-P(\lambda) = (x-\lambda)Q(x)$ for a polynomial $Q$, whence $P(B)-P(\lambda) = (B-\lambda)Q(B)$ with the two factors commuting; if this product had an inverse, $B-\lambda$ would have one too, so $\lambda \in \sigma(B)$ forces $P(\lambda)\in\sigma(P(B))$. For "$\subseteq$": a constant $P \equiv c$ gives $\sigma(cI) = \{c\}$ and $\{P(\lambda)\} = \{c\}$, since $\sigma(B) \ne \varnothing$ - this is the one place the standing hypothesis $\mathcal{H}\ne\{0\}$ is used, the spectrum of a bounded operator being non-empty only on a non-zero space (:ref[]{to=funcan.spectrum}) - so assume $P$ non-constant and factor $P(x) - \nu = c\prod_{j}(x-\lambda_j)$ with $c \ne 0$; if no $\lambda_j$ were in $\sigma(B)$ the product of the inverses would invert $P(B)-\nu$, so some $\lambda_j \in \sigma(B)$ and $\nu = P(\lambda_j)$.

**Step 2: the isometry.** Let $P$ be a polynomial. Then $P(A)^{*}P(A) = (\bar P P)(A)$, where $\bar P$ is the polynomial with conjugated coefficients, and $\bar P P$ has *real* coefficients - the coefficient of $x^{k}$ in it is $\sum_{i+j=k}\bar a_i a_j$, which is its own conjugate - so $(\bar PP)(A)^{*} = \bigl(\overline{\bar PP}\bigr)(A) = (\bar PP)(A)$ and the operator $(\bar PP)(A)$ is self-adjoint. (That $\bar PP$ takes real *values* on $\sigma(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ would not do at this stage: only the polynomial calculus exists so far, and $\sigma(A)$ may be a finite set, so the values on it do not determine the operator.) Two named facts now do the work: the $C^{*}$ identity $\lVert T\rVert^2 = \lVert T^{*}T\rVert$, valid for every bounded operator on a Hilbert space (:ref[]{to=funcan.adjoint-operators}), and the equality of norm and spectral radius for a self-adjoint operator (:cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="Thm VI.6, p. 192"}). They give, in that order,
$$
\lVert P(A)\rVert^2 = \bigl\lVert P(A)^{*}P(A)\bigr\rVert = \bigl\lVert (\bar P P)(A)\bigr\rVert = \sup\bigl\{ \lvert\nu\rvert : \nu \in \sigma\bigl((\bar PP)(A)\bigr)\bigr\} = \sup_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)} \lvert P(\lambda)\rvert^2 ,
$$
the last equality by Step 1. This is the identity the idea promised, and it is where self-adjointness of $A$ enters: for a general bounded operator the norm can exceed the spectral radius, as it does for the nilpotent $2\times 2$ matrix with a single $1$ above the diagonal, whose spectral radius is $0$ and whose norm is $1$. (Self-adjointness is sufficient, not necessary: a unitary has norm and spectral radius both $1$.)

**Step 3: continuous functions.** The polynomials with complex coefficients form a subalgebra $\mathcal{A}$ of $C(\sigma(A))$. It contains the constants; it separates the points of $\sigma(A)$, already through $P(x)=x$; and it contains $\bar P$ along with $P$, the conjugate of a polynomial restricted to $\sigma(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ being the polynomial with conjugated coefficients. The uniform closure of $\mathcal{A}$ inherits all three. So the complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem (:cite[Folland]{work=folland-1999 loc="Thm 4.51, p. 141"}), which we use here and do not reprove, leaves it two possibilities: all of $C(\sigma(A))$, or the functions vanishing at one point of $\sigma(A)$. The constants exclude the second. So the polynomials are dense. (The complex form is quoted because it applies directly to the complex algebra $\mathcal{A}$; the real form, Folland's Thm 4.45, p. 139, would also serve, at the cost of running it on real polynomials in $C(\sigma(A),\mathbb{R})$ and then splitting a complex $h$ into real and imaginary parts. His remark on p. 141 that the conclusion fails for complex algebras not closed under conjugation does not bite here, since $\mathcal{A}$ was just shown to be conjugation-closed.) By Step 2 the map $P \mapsto P(A)$ is isometric for $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$ on $\sigma(A)$; a densely defined isometry into a complete space extends uniquely to the closure. This gives a map $h \mapsto h(A)$ on $C(\sigma(A))$, isometric and multiplicative, with $x \mapsto x$ sent to $A$.

**Step 4: the spectral measures.** Fix $\psi \in \mathcal{H}$. The functional $h \mapsto (h(A)\psi,\psi)$ is linear on $C(\sigma(A))$ and positive: if $h \ge 0$ then $h = g^2$ with $g$ real and continuous, so $(h(A)\psi,\psi) = (g(A)^{*}g(A)\psi,\psi) = \lVert g(A)\psi\rVert^2 \ge 0$, using that $g(A)$ is self-adjoint because $g$ is real. By the Riesz-Markov theorem - the Riesz representation theorem for positive linear functionals on continuous functions, :cite[Folland]{work=folland-1999 loc="Thm 7.2, p. 212"} - there is a unique positive Radon measure $\mu_\psi$ on the compact set $\sigma(A)$ with
$$
\bigl( h(A)\psi,\psi \bigr) \;=\; \int_{\sigma(A)} h\, d\mu_\psi \qquad \text{for all } h \in C(\sigma(A)) .
$$
It is finite, of total mass $\mu_\psi(\sigma(A)) = (1(A)\psi,\psi) = \lVert\psi\rVert^2$. Two words about what "unique" covers. Folland's theorem is stated for $C_c(X)$, which is $C(\sigma(A))$ here because $\sigma(A)$ is compact; and it gives uniqueness only among Radon measures. That is uniqueness among *all* finite Borel measures in this case, because $\sigma(A)$ is a compact metric space, hence second countable and locally compact Hausdorff, and on such a space every Borel measure finite on compact sets is Radon (:cite[Folland]{work=folland-1999 loc="Thm 7.8, p. 217"}).

**Step 5: the cyclic case.** Suppose some $\psi$ is cyclic. Define $V$ on the dense set $\{h(A)\psi : h \in C(\sigma(A))\}$ by $V\bigl(h(A)\psi\bigr) = h$. It is isometric, because by Step 4
$$
\bigl\lVert h(A)\psi \bigr\rVert^2 = \bigl( (\bar h h)(A)\psi,\psi\bigr) = \int \lvert h\rvert^2 d\mu_\psi = \lVert h\rVert^2_{L^2(d\mu_\psi)} ,
$$
and the same computation shows $V$ is well defined. What well-definedness asks is that one vector of the dense set receive one function, that is, that $h(A)\psi = h'(A)\psi$ force $h = h'$ in $L^2(d\mu_\psi)$; and applying the display to $h - h'$ turns the first equality into $\lVert h-h'\rVert_{L^2(d\mu_\psi)} = 0$, which is the second. (Read the other way, the same display gives the converse implication, $h = h'$ in $L^2(d\mu_\psi) \Rightarrow h(A)\psi = h'(A)\psi$; that is the injectivity of $V$, not its well-definedness.) An isometry defined on a dense subspace extends to an isometry of $\mathcal{H}$, and its range contains $C(\sigma(A))$, which is dense in $L^2(\sigma(A), d\mu_\psi)$ because $\mu_\psi$ is a Radon measure on the compact space $\sigma(A)$ (:cite[Folland]{work=folland-1999 loc="Prop. 7.9, p. 217"}, quoted and not reproved); so the extension is unitary onto $L^2(\sigma(A),d\mu_\psi)$. Finally, for continuous $h$,
$$
\bigl(VAV^{-1}h\bigr)(\lambda) = \bigl(V A h(A)\psi\bigr)(\lambda) = \bigl( V (x h)(A)\psi \bigr)(\lambda) = \lambda\, h(\lambda) ,
$$
and by continuity this extends from $C(\sigma(A))$ to all of $L^2$. So $A$ is multiplication by $\lambda$.

**Step 6: removing the cyclicity assumption.** Separability gives a decomposition $\mathcal{H} = \bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_n$, $N \le \infty$, into closed subspaces each invariant under $A$ and each carrying a vector $\varphi_n$ cyclic for the restriction of $A$ to it: take a maximal family of mutually orthogonal cyclic subspaces, which exists by :ref[Zorn's lemma]{to=found.zorn-lemma}, and suppose the closed span $\mathcal{W}$ of the family were not all of $\mathcal{H}$. Then $\mathcal{W}^{\perp} \ne \{0\}$, and $\mathcal{W}^{\perp}$ is $A$-invariant: $\mathcal{W}$ is $A$-invariant, being spanned by $A$-invariant subspaces, so for $\varphi\in\mathcal{W}^{\perp}$ and $w\in\mathcal{W}$ we get $(A\varphi,w) = (\varphi,Aw) = 0$, using that $A$ is bounded and self-adjoint. Any unit vector of $\mathcal{W}^{\perp}$ therefore generates, inside $\mathcal{W}^{\perp}$, one more cyclic subspace orthogonal to every member of the family, contradicting maximality. That the family is at most countable - which is what the index $N \le \infty$ above asserts, and what the summability in Step 7 and the countable direct sum of part E need - is where separability is used: the members are non-zero and mutually orthogonal, so picking a unit vector in each produces an orthonormal set with one element per member, and in a separable Hilbert space every orthonormal set is at most countable (:ref[]{to=funcan.orthonormal-bases}). Label the members $\mathcal{H}_1,\mathcal{H}_2,\dots$ accordingly. Applying Step 5 in each summand and writing $\mu_n = \mu_{\varphi_n}$ gives a unitary $U : \mathcal{H} \to \bigoplus_{n} L^2(\mathbb{R},d\mu_n)$ carrying $A$ to multiplication by $\lambda$ in each slot. This is part E for bounded $A$; for unbounded $A$ part E is quoted rather than proved, as its statement says, and Step 10 lists it among the imports.

**Step 7: one finite measure space.** A direct sum of $L^2$ spaces is itself an $L^2$ space, over the disjoint union of the underlying measure spaces. So label $N$ disjoint copies of $\mathbb{R}$ by $n$, give the $n$th the measure $\mu_n$, and call the resulting space $(M,\mu)$: the unitary of Step 6 becomes a unitary onto $L^2(M,d\mu)$, and the operator that acted as multiplication by $\lambda$ in every slot becomes $T_f$ for the single function $f$ whose value at the point $\lambda$ of the $n$th copy is $\lambda$ itself. Only the finiteness of $\mu$ still has to be arranged, and the cyclic vectors are free to be rescaled: a non-zero multiple $c\varphi_n$ generates the same subspace and has $\mu_{c\varphi_n} = \lvert c\rvert^2\mu_{\varphi_n}$, since the functional of Step 4 scales by $\lvert c\rvert^2$. Normalise each $\varphi_n$ to length $2^{-n}$. Then $\mu_n(\mathbb{R}) = \lVert\varphi_n\rVert^2 = 4^{-n}$, whence $\mu(M) = \sum_{n} 4^{-n} \le \tfrac13$, a finite total mass; any summable choice of lengths would do as well. This is part A for bounded $A$. $\square$

VII, Problems 3-5, p. 246"}
**Step 8: multiplication operators are self-adjoint.** Let $\mu$ be finite and $f$ real and a.e. finite. $T_f$ is densely defined, since the sets $\{\lvert f\rvert \le n\}$ exhaust $M$ up to a null set and every $\varphi\in L^2$ is the $L^2$-limit of its restrictions to them; and $T_f$ is symmetric because $f$ is real. Let $\chi_n$ be the indicator of $\{\lvert f\rvert \le n\}$ and let $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(T_f^{*})$. For every $\varphi$ with $\lVert\varphi\rVert \le 1$ we have $\chi_n\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(T_f)$, so
$$
\bigl\lvert (\chi_n f \psi, \varphi) \bigr\rvert = \bigl\lvert (\psi, T_f \chi_n\varphi) \bigr\rvert = \bigl\lvert (T_f^{*}\psi, \chi_n \varphi)\bigr\rvert \le \lVert T_f^{*}\psi\rVert .
$$
Taking the supremum over such $\varphi$ gives $\lVert \chi_n f\psi\rVert \le \lVert T_f^{*}\psi\rVert$ for every $n$, and $\lvert \chi_n f \psi\rvert$ increases pointwise a.e. to $\lvert f\psi\rvert$; by the monotone convergence theorem (:ref[]{to=measure.monotone-convergence-fatou}) $f\psi \in L^2$, that is $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(T_f)$. Hence $\mathcal{D}(T_f^{*}) \subseteq \mathcal{D}(T_f)$, which with symmetry gives $T_f = T_f^{*}$.

**Step 9: the resolvent at $\pm i$.** Let $A$ be self-adjoint. By the basic criterion for self-adjointness (:ref[]{to=funcan.symmetric-self-adjoint}), $A \pm i$ map $\mathcal{D}(A)$ *onto* $\mathcal{H}$, and they are injective because $\lVert (A\pm i)\psi\rVert^2 = \lVert A\psi\rVert^2 + \lVert\psi\rVert^2 \ge \lVert\psi\rVert^2$ for $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$, the cross terms cancelling since $A$ is symmetric. So $R = (A+i)^{-1}$ is defined on all of $\mathcal{H}$; it is closed because $A+i$ is, and therefore bounded by the closed graph theorem (:ref[]{to=funcan.uniform-boundedness-open-mapping}). The identity
$$
\bigl( (A-i)^{-1}\psi, (A+i)\varphi \bigr) = \bigl( (A-i)(A-i)^{-1}\psi, \varphi \bigr) = (\psi,\varphi) \qquad \bigl(\psi\in\mathcal{H},\ \varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)\bigr)
$$
identifies $R^{*} = (A-i)^{-1}$, since $\mathrm{Ran}(A+i) = \mathcal{H}$. The first equality moves $A+i$ out of the second slot as its adjoint $(A+i)^{*} = A-i$, which is legitimate because $(A-i)^{-1}\psi$ lies in $\mathcal{D}(A)$; the second is $(A-i)(A-i)^{-1} = I$ on $\mathcal{H}$. (:cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="p. 261"} print the two factors in the opposite order at this point; the order above is the one the manipulation produces, and only this order is a composition of maps that can be applied.) The two resolvents commute. :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, p. 261"} take this from their Theorem VIII.2; it is also immediate from the algebra. On $\mathcal{D}(A^{2}) = \{\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A) : A\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)\}$ the two products $(A-i)(A+i)$ and $(A+i)(A-i)$ are both $A^{2}+1$, and each is a bijection of $\mathcal{D}(A^{2})$ onto $\mathcal{H}$, the two factors being bijections between different pairs of spaces: $A+i$ carries $\mathcal{D}(A^{2})$ onto $\mathcal{D}(A)$ - if $\chi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$ and $\psi = (A+i)^{-1}\chi$, then $A\psi = \chi - i\psi$ lies in $\mathcal{D}(A)$, so $\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A^{2})$ - and $A-i$ carries $\mathcal{D}(A)$ onto $\mathcal{H}$, and the same with the two factors exchanged; inverting the equality $(A-i)(A+i) = (A+i)(A-i)$ therefore gives
$$
(A+i)^{-1}(A-i)^{-1} \;=\; (A^{2}+1)^{-1} \;=\; (A-i)^{-1}(A+i)^{-1}
$$
on all of $\mathcal{H}$. Hence $R^{*}R = RR^{*}$: $R$ is a bounded **normal** operator. Finally $\ker R = \{0\}$, since $R$ is a bijection onto $\mathcal{D}(A)$.

**Step 10: diagonalising $R$.** Steps 1-7 give the multiplication-operator form for a bounded *self-adjoint* operator, and $R$ is only normal. What is needed is one step further out, and we quote it rather than prove it:

**The bounded normal case.** *Let $N$ be a bounded normal operator on a separable Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. Then there are a finite measure space $(M,\mu)$, a unitary $U : \mathcal{H}\to L^2(M,d\mu)$ and a bounded, in general complex-valued, Borel function $g$ on $M$ with $UNU^{-1} = T_g$.*

This is Problem 5(b) of Chapter VII of :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="Ch. VII, Problems 3-5, p. 246"}, who set it as an exercise rather than proving it in the text; it is not proved here either. It is the only step of the spectral-theorem construction itself that is taken from an exercise, but it is in no way the only thing the unit takes on quotation. The substantive imports - results quoted outright - are: the equality of norm and spectral radius for a self-adjoint operator in Step 2; the complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem in Steps 3 and 13a; the Riesz-Markov theorem and Folland's Thm 7.8 on Radon measures in Step 4, and his Prop. 7.9 on the density of $C(\sigma(A))$ in $L^2(\sigma(A),d\mu_\psi)$ in Step 5; the basic criterion for self-adjointness, which is a prerequisite unit, in Step 9; the bounded normal case here; the surjectivity half of Reed and Simon's Thm VIII.6 in Step 14; the direct-sum form of part E for unbounded $A$, which Reed and Simon assert on p. 262 without proof; and the functional calculus for *unbounded* real Borel functions named at the end of part D - that $g(A) = \int g\,dP_\lambda$ is self-adjoint on $\mathcal{D}_g$ - which is Reed and Simon's Thm VIII.5 with the discussion of unbounded functions following it, pp. 262-263, and of which the construction below proves the single case $g(\lambda) = \lambda$ in Step 14 and nothing more. Their Problems 3-5 lay out the route they intend, which is not a two-line variant of Steps 1-7: write $N = B + iC$ with $B = \tfrac12(N+N^{*})$ and $C = \tfrac1{2i}(N-N^{*})$, both self-adjoint and, because $N$ is normal, commuting; show that the spectral projections of $B$ commute with those of $C$; set $\Phi(\chi_{\Omega_1\times\Omega_2}) = P_{\Omega_1}(B)\,P_{\Omega_2}(C)$ on the rectangles of $\mathbb{R}^2$ and extend by linearity; pass to continuous functions on a product of intervals by the bounded-linear-transformation extension theorem, and then to Borel functions; and read the multiplication form off the two-variable calculus so obtained. Alongside these the proof draws without comment on the standard apparatus of the prerequisite units: Zorn's lemma in Step 6 (:ref[]{to=found.zorn-lemma}), the closed graph theorem in Step 9 (:ref[]{to=funcan.uniform-boundedness-open-mapping}), the monotone and dominated convergence theorems in Steps 8, 13 and 14 (:ref[]{to=measure.monotone-convergence-fatou}, :ref[]{to=measure.dominated-convergence}), the extension of a densely defined isometry in Steps 3 and 5, the operator norm and its submultiplicativity throughout (:ref[]{to=funcan.bounded-operators}), the $C^{*}$-identity $\lVert B^{*}B\rVert = \lVert B\rVert^2$ in Step 2 (:ref[]{to=funcan.adjoint-operators}), the non-emptiness of the spectrum of a bounded operator in Step 1 and the compactness of $\sigma(A)$ and its reality for a bounded self-adjoint $A$, used in the opening line of the bounded proof and again in Steps 3 and 4 (:ref[]{to=funcan.spectrum}), and the substitution rule for an image measure in Step 14, which is not quoted but proved on the spot there, in a line, from the definition of an image measure and monotone convergence.

Applied to $N = R$, which is normal by Step 9, this produces a finite measure space $(M,\mu)$, a unitary $U : \mathcal{H}\to L^2(M,d\mu)$ and a bounded measurable complex $g$ with $URU^{-1} = T_g$.

**Step 11: back to $A$.** Put $f := g^{-1} - i$, which is defined and finite a.e. because $\ker R = \{0\}$ makes $\{g = 0\}$ a $\mu$-null set. By Step 9, $\varphi \mapsto \psi = R\varphi$ is a bijection of $\mathcal{H}$ onto $\mathcal{D}(A)$, and it carries two identities with it: $U\psi = g\,U\varphi$, because $URU^{-1} = T_g$, and $A\psi = \varphi - i\psi$, because $(A+i)\psi = \varphi$. Four things remain.

*The action, condition 2.* Apply $U$ to $A\psi = \varphi - i\psi$ and substitute $U\varphi = g^{-1}U\psi$:
$$
(UA\psi)(m) = (U\varphi)(m) - i (U\psi)(m) = \bigl( g(m)^{-1} - i \bigr)(U\psi)(m) = f(m)\,(U\psi)(m) .
$$

*The domain, condition 1.* For $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$ the display just obtained reads $f\,U\psi = U(A\psi)$, which is in $L^2$. Conversely let $\psi \in \mathcal{H}$ with $f\,U\psi \in L^2$. Then $g^{-1}U\psi = (f+i)U\psi \in L^2$, so it equals $U\varphi$ for exactly one $\varphi \in \mathcal{H}$; multiplying by $g$ gives $U(R\varphi) = g\,U\varphi = U\psi$, so $\psi = R\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$.

*$f$ is real.* By the two conditions $T_f = UAU^{-1}$ is self-adjoint. Suppose $\mathrm{Im}\,f > 0$ on a set of positive measure. That set is the countable union of the sets $S_{k,n} = \{\mathrm{Im}\,f \ge 1/k\} \cap \{\lvert f\rvert \le n\}$, so some $S = S_{k,n}$ has $\mu(S) > 0$; on it $f$ is bounded, so $\chi_S \in \mathcal{D}(T_f)$, and
$$
\mathrm{Im}\,(T_f \chi_S, \chi_S) = \int_S \mathrm{Im}\,f \, d\mu \;\ge\; \mu(S)/k \;>\; 0 ,
$$
which no self-adjoint operator permits. Applying this to $-f$ rules out $\mathrm{Im}\,f < 0$.

*The integrability clause.* This is Proposition 3 of :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, Proposition 3, p. 264"}, stated by them separately from Theorem VIII.4, and what follows is their reweighting proof. Keep $f$ and replace $\mu$ by $d\tilde\mu = e^{-f^2}d\mu$ and $U$ by $VU$, where $(V\varphi)(m) = e^{f(m)^2/2}\varphi(m)$. Then $\int \lvert V\varphi\rvert^2 d\tilde\mu = \int\lvert\varphi\rvert^2 d\mu$, so $V$ is unitary from $L^2(d\mu)$ onto $L^2(d\tilde\mu)$; it commutes with multiplication by $f$, so conditions 1 and 2 survive verbatim, and $\tilde\mu(M) \le \mu(M) < \infty$. Finally $c_p := \sup_{t\ge0} t^p e^{-t^2}$ is finite for every $p \in [1,\infty)$, so $\int \lvert f\rvert^p d\tilde\mu = \int \lvert f\rvert^p e^{-f^2} d\mu \le c_p\,\mu(M) < \infty$, that is, $f \in L^p(M,d\tilde\mu)$ for every such $p$.

**Step 12: the spectrum.** By part A it suffices to compute $\sigma(T_f)$. If $\lambda$ is not in the essential range of $f$, then $\lvert f-\lambda\rvert \ge \varepsilon$ a.e. for some $\varepsilon>0$, so $(f-\lambda)^{-1}$ is bounded by $1/\varepsilon$ and multiplication by it maps $L^2$ into $\mathcal{D}(T_f)$, since $f\,(f-\lambda)^{-1} = 1 + \lambda(f-\lambda)^{-1}$ is bounded by $1+\lvert\lambda\rvert/\varepsilon$; it is therefore a two-sided inverse of $T_f - \lambda$, and $\lambda \in \rho(T_f)$. If $\lambda$ *is* in the essential range, put $S_n = \{\lvert f - \lambda\rvert < 1/n\}$, which has positive measure, and $\varphi_n = \chi_{S_n}/\lVert\chi_{S_n}\rVert$. Then $\lVert\varphi_n\rVert = 1$ and $\lVert (T_f-\lambda)\varphi_n\rVert \le 1/n$, so $T_f-\lambda$ has no bounded inverse and $\lambda\in\sigma(T_f)$. (This is Weyl's criterion in a concrete case.)

**Step 13: the functional calculus.** Define $h(A) = U^{-1}T_{h\circ f}U$ for bounded Borel $h$. Properties 1 and 2 of part C are immediate from the corresponding statements for multiplication operators, $\lVert T_{h\circ f}\rVert \le \lVert h\rVert_\infty$ because $h\circ f$ is bounded by $\lVert h\rVert_\infty$. For property 4, if $h_n \to h$ pointwise with $\sup_n\lVert h_n\rVert_\infty = C < \infty$, then for fixed $\varphi \in L^2$ the functions $\lvert (h_n\circ f) \varphi - (h\circ f)\varphi\rvert^2$ tend to $0$ a.e. and are dominated by $4C^2\lvert\varphi\rvert^2 \in L^1$, so the dominated convergence theorem (:ref[]{to=measure.dominated-convergence}) gives $T_{h_n\circ f}\varphi \to T_{h\circ f}\varphi$. Property 3 is the same argument with the domination $\lvert h_n(f)\varphi\rvert \le \lvert f \varphi\rvert$, which is in $L^2$ precisely when $\psi = U^{-1}\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$ - property 3 is the only one of the four that touches the domain condition of part A, and the condition itself is called on again in Step 14, where it describes $\mathcal{D}(A)$ through the spectral measures.

The two further properties listed in part C, which are (e) and (f) of :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, Thm VIII.5, p. 262"}, come off the same formula in a line each. *Eigenvectors.* Let $A\psi = \lambda\psi$ and put $\varphi = U\psi$. By condition 2 of part A, $f\varphi = \lambda\varphi$ a.e., so $\varphi$ vanishes a.e. on $\{f \ne \lambda\}$; hence $(h\circ f)\varphi = h(\lambda)\varphi$ a.e., which is $h(A)\psi = h(\lambda)\psi$. *Positivity.* If $h \ge 0$ then for every $\psi$
$$
\bigl(h(A)\psi,\psi\bigr) = \int_M h\bigl(f(m)\bigr)\,\lvert (U\psi)(m)\rvert^2\,d\mu(m) \;\ge\; 0 ,
$$
the integrand being a product of two non-negative functions; that is $h(A) \ge 0$.

**Step 13a: the calculus is unique.** Polynomials are not available here: they are not bounded on $\mathbb{R}$, and properties 1, 2 and 4 alone mention $A$ nowhere. Property 3 is the only link to $A$, and the argument has to start from it. Let $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$ be two maps with properties 1-4.

*They agree on one resolvent function.* Let $r(x) = (x-i)^{-1}$, which is bounded and continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, and let $h_n(x) = x\chi_{[-n,n]}(x)$, which are bounded Borel with $h_n(x)\to x$ for every $x$ and $\lvert h_n\rvert \le \lvert x\rvert$ - exactly the hypotheses of property 3. Fix $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$. Property 3 gives $\Phi(h_n)\psi \to A\psi$, and $\Phi(r)$ is bounded, so
$$
\Phi(r)(A-i)\psi \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \Phi(r)\bigl(\Phi(h_n)\psi - i\psi\bigr) \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi(r\,h_n)\psi \;-\; i\,\Phi(r)\psi ,
$$
the second equality by property 1. Now $\lvert r h_n\rvert \le \lvert x\rvert/\sqrt{x^2+1} \le 1$ and $r h_n \to x/(x-i)$ pointwise, so property 4 turns the limit into $\Phi\bigl(x/(x-i)\bigr)\psi$. Since $x/(x-i) - i/(x-i) = 1$ and $\Phi(1) = I$,
$$
\Phi(r)(A-i)\psi = \psi \qquad \text{for all } \psi \in \mathcal{D}(A) .
$$
By Step 9, $A-i$ maps $\mathcal{D}(A)$ onto $\mathcal{H}$, so $\Phi(r) = (A-i)^{-1}$: a bounded operator fixed by $A$ alone. The same holds for $\Phi'$, and by property 1, $\Phi(\bar r) = \Phi(r)^{*} = \Phi'(\bar r)$.

*They agree on continuous functions.* Read $r$ as a continuous function on the one-point compactification $\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ with $r(\infty) = 0$. The unital $*$-algebra generated by $r$ contains the constants, is closed under conjugation, and separates the points of $\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$, because $x \mapsto (x-i)^{-1}$ is injective there. Its uniform closure inherits those properties and is closed, so the complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem (:cite[Folland]{work=folland-1999 loc="Thm 4.51, p. 141"}), applied exactly as in Step 3, makes that closure either all of $C(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\})$ or the functions vanishing at one point - and the constants rule the second out. So the algebra is dense in $C(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\})$ for the supremum norm. Property 1 makes $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$ agree on that algebra, and property 2 makes both contractions for $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$, so they agree on its uniform closure: on every continuous $h$ with a limit at infinity.

*They agree everywhere.* Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of bounded Borel $h : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}$ with $\Phi(h) = \Phi'(h)$. Property 1 makes $\mathcal{C}$ an algebra containing the constants: if $h_1,h_2\in\mathcal{C}$ then $\Phi(h_1h_2) = \Phi(h_1)\Phi(h_2) = \Phi'(h_1)\Phi'(h_2) = \Phi'(h_1h_2)$, and likewise for linear combinations. Property 4 closes $\mathcal{C}$ under pointwise limits of uniformly bounded sequences. The previous paragraph puts every continuous function with a limit at infinity into $\mathcal{C}$. Those three facts suffice, in three moves, and no result outside this handbook is needed for them.

*(i) Indicators of compact sets.* Let $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be compact and non-empty, and put $g_n(x) = \max\bigl(0,\,1 - n\,\mathrm{dist}(x,K)\bigr)$. Each $g_n$ is continuous with values in $[0,1]$ and vanishes outside a bounded set, so it has limit $0$ at infinity and lies in $\mathcal{C}$; and $g_n(x) \to \chi_K(x)$ at every $x$, because $\mathrm{dist}(x,K) = 0$ exactly on $K$, $K$ being closed. The sequence is bounded by $1$, so $\chi_K\in\mathcal{C}$; and $\chi_\varnothing = 0\in\mathcal{C}$ trivially, so $\chi_K\in\mathcal{C}$ for every compact $K$.

*(ii) Indicators of all Borel sets.* Let $\mathcal{D} = \{E\subseteq\mathbb{R}\text{ Borel} : \chi_E\in\mathcal{C}\}$. It contains $\mathbb{R}$, since $\chi_{\mathbb{R}} = 1$; it is closed under complements, since $\chi_{E^{c}} = 1 - \chi_E$ and $\mathcal{C}$ is a vector space containing $1$; it is closed under finite intersections, since $\chi_{E\cap F} = \chi_E\chi_F$ and $\mathcal{C}$ is an algebra; and it is closed under countable increasing unions, since the indicators of $E_1\cup\cdots\cup E_N$ are bounded by $1$ and converge pointwise to the indicator of $\bigcup_n E_n$. An algebra of sets closed under countable increasing unions is a $\sigma$-algebra, because an arbitrary countable union is the increasing union of the finite ones. By (i) $\mathcal{D}$ contains every compact set, while each open $V\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is the union of the countably many compact sets $\{x\in V : \lvert x\rvert\le n,\ \mathrm{dist}(x,V^{c})\ge 1/n\}$; so $\mathcal{D}$ contains the open sets and hence all of the Borel $\sigma$-algebra they generate (:ref[]{to=measure.sigma-algebras-measures}).

*(iii) All bounded Borel functions.* By (ii) and linearity $\mathcal{C}$ contains every simple Borel function. Any bounded Borel $h$ is a uniform limit of such: given $\varepsilon>0$, cut the disc $\{\lvert z\rvert \le \lVert h\rVert_\infty\}$ into finitely many disjoint Borel pieces of diameter less than $\varepsilon$, choose a point in each, and let the approximant take that value on the preimage of that piece. A uniform limit is in particular a uniformly bounded pointwise limit, so $h\in\mathcal{C}$. Hence $\Phi = \Phi'$.

**Step 14: the projection-valued measure.** Put $P_\Omega = \chi_\Omega(A)$. Axiom 1 holds since $\chi_\Omega^2 = \bar\chi_\Omega = \chi_\Omega$, so $P_\Omega$ is idempotent and self-adjoint; axiom 2 and axiom 4 are the identities $\chi_{\varnothing}=0$, $\chi_{\mathbb{R}}=1$, $\chi_{\Omega_1}\chi_{\Omega_2} = \chi_{\Omega_1\cap\Omega_2}$ transported through the homomorphism; axiom 3 is property 4 applied to the partial sums $\sum_{n\le N}\chi_{\Omega_n}$, which are uniformly bounded by $1$ and converge pointwise to $\chi_\Omega$. For $\psi\in\mathcal{H}$ the set function $\mu_\psi(\Omega) = (P_\Omega\psi,\psi)$ is therefore a finite positive Borel measure, and by construction $\mu_\psi$ is the image under $f$ of the measure $\lvert U\psi\rvert^2 d\mu$; hence for bounded Borel $h$,
$$
\bigl(h(A)\psi,\psi\bigr) = \int_M h(f(m))\,\lvert (U\psi)(m)\rvert^2 d\mu(m) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} h(\lambda)\, d\mu_\psi(\lambda) ,
$$
by the substitution rule for an image measure, which needs nothing from outside: if $\kappa$ is a finite positive measure on $M$ and $\rho(\Omega) = \kappa\bigl(f^{-1}(\Omega)\bigr)$ is its image under $f$, then $\int_{\mathbb{R}} g\,d\rho = \int_M g\bigl(f(m)\bigr)\,d\kappa(m)$ for every Borel $g\ge0$ - for $g=\chi_\Omega$ this is the definition of $\rho$, linear combinations give the simple $g$, and monotone convergence along simple $g_k\uparrow g$ (:ref[]{to=measure.monotone-convergence-fatou}) gives the rest - and hence for every Borel $g$ with $\int\lvert g\rvert\,d\rho<\infty$, by splitting the real and imaginary parts of $g$ into their positive and negative parts. Here $\kappa = \lvert U\psi\rvert^2 d\mu$ and $g = h$; the rule is used again in the two-vector paragraph below, with $\kappa$ running over $\lvert U\psi\rvert\lvert U\varphi\rvert\,d\mu$ and the four non-negative pieces of $(U\psi)\overline{(U\varphi)}\,d\mu$. The same rule with $g(\lambda) = \lambda^2$ gives $\int\lambda^2 d\mu_\psi = \int_M \lvert f\,U\psi\rvert^2 d\mu$, finite exactly when $\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$ by condition 1 of part A and equal then to $\lVert U(A\psi)\rVert^2 = \lVert A\psi\rVert^2$; so $\mathcal{D}(A) = \{\psi : \int\lambda^2 d\mu_\psi<\infty\}$. Take now $h_n(\lambda) = \lambda\chi_{[-n,n]}(\lambda)$ and a $\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$. On the left $(h_n(A)\psi,\psi)\to(A\psi,\psi)$, by property 3 of part C. On the right the limit may be taken inside the integral, but not for nothing: $\mu_\psi$ is finite and $\lambda\in L^2(\mu_\psi)$, so Cauchy-Schwarz gives $\int\lvert\lambda\rvert\,d\mu_\psi \le \bigl(\int\lambda^2 d\mu_\psi\bigr)^{1/2}\mu_\psi(\mathbb{R})^{1/2} = \lVert A\psi\rVert\,\lVert\psi\rVert<\infty$, and $\lvert h_n\rvert \le \lvert\lambda\rvert \in L^1(\mu_\psi)$ with $h_n\to\lambda$ pointwise, so dominated convergence (:ref[]{to=measure.dominated-convergence}) applies. Hence $(A\psi,\psi) = \int\lambda\,d\mu_\psi$ for every $\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$.

*The version with two vectors.* For $\psi,\varphi\in\mathcal{H}$ write $F = U\psi$ and $G = U\varphi$, and put $\nu_{\psi\varphi}(\Omega) = (P_\Omega\psi,\varphi)$. Since $h(A) = U^{-1}T_{h\circ f}U$ and $U$ is unitary,
$$
\bigl(h(A)\psi,\varphi\bigr) \;=\; \bigl( (h\circ f)\,F,\,G\bigr)_{L^2(M,d\mu)} \;=\; \int_M h\bigl(f(m)\bigr)\,F(m)\,\overline{G(m)}\,d\mu(m)
$$
for every bounded Borel $h$ - the computation that opened this step, with $G$ in the second slot in place of $F$. Read with $h = \chi_\Omega$, it says that $\nu_{\psi\varphi}$ is the image under $f$ of $F\bar G\,d\mu$, and $F\bar G \in L^1(M,d\mu)$ by Cauchy-Schwarz. Splitting $F\bar G$ into the positive and negative parts of its real and imaginary parts, and taking the image of each under $f$, therefore writes
$$
\nu_{\psi\varphi} \;=\; \rho_1 - \rho_2 + i(\rho_3 - \rho_4) , \qquad \rho_1,\dots,\rho_4 \ \text{finite positive Borel measures on } \mathbb{R} .
$$
This is at once the countable additivity of $\nu_{\psi\varphi}$ and the meaning of $\int g\,d\nu_{\psi\varphi}$: the same combination of four ordinary integrals, defined whenever $g$ is integrable against each $\rho_j$ - automatic for bounded $g$, the $\rho_j$ being finite - and equal then, by the substitution rule, to $\int_M g(f)\,F\bar G\,d\mu$. In particular
$$
\bigl(h(A)\psi,\varphi\bigr) \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}} h\,d\nu_{\psi\varphi} \qquad (\psi,\varphi\in\mathcal{H},\ h \text{ bounded Borel}) ,
$$
an identity polarisation would also give, both sides being bounded sesquilinear forms which agree on the diagonal by the first display of this step.

What polarisation does not give is the passage from bounded $h$ to $h(\lambda) = \lambda$, and that passage is the whole of part D's claim for $\varphi\notin\mathcal{D}(A)$. Let $\rho_{\psi\varphi}$ be the image under $f$ of the positive measure $\lvert F\rvert\,\lvert G\rvert\,d\mu$, which is finite of total mass $\int_M\lvert F\rvert\lvert G\rvert\,d\mu \le \lVert\psi\rVert\lVert\varphi\rVert$ by Cauchy-Schwarz in $L^2(M,d\mu)$. Each of the four densities above is at most $\lvert F\bar G\rvert = \lvert F\rvert\lvert G\rvert$ pointwise, so $\rho_j \le \rho_{\psi\varphi}$ for every $j$: the positive finite measure $\rho_{\psi\varphi}$ dominates $\nu_{\psi\varphi}$, and running the same Cauchy-Schwarz over $f^{-1}(\Omega)$ instead of over $M$,
$$
\lvert \nu_{\psi\varphi}(\Omega)\rvert \;\le\; \rho_{\psi\varphi}(\Omega) \;=\; \int_{f^{-1}(\Omega)} \lvert F\rvert\,\lvert G\rvert\,d\mu \;\le\; \mu_\psi(\Omega)^{1/2}\,\mu_\varphi(\Omega)^{1/2} ,
$$
so $\nu_{\psi\varphi}$ is controlled by the two spectral measures and vanishes on every set null for either of them. Now let $\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$, so that $fF = U(A\psi) \in L^2(M,d\mu)$. One more change of variables and one more Cauchy-Schwarz give
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}} \lvert\lambda\rvert\,d\rho_{\psi\varphi}(\lambda) \;=\; \int_M \lvert f\rvert\,\lvert F\rvert\,\lvert G\rvert\,d\mu \;\le\; \lVert fF\rVert_{L^2}\,\lVert G\rVert_{L^2} \;=\; \lVert A\psi\rVert\,\lVert\varphi\rVert \;<\;\infty ,
$$
and $\rho_j \le \rho_{\psi\varphi}$ makes $\lambda$ integrable against each of the four. That is the absolute convergence promised in part D, for *every* $\varphi\in\mathcal{H}$ and not only for $\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$, and it is had without ever forming the total variation of $\nu_{\psi\varphi}$: the dominating measure is a positive one, built by hand out of $\lvert U\psi\rvert$ and $\lvert U\varphi\rvert$. The value of the integral is then read off by the substitution rule, this time with $g(\lambda) = \lambda$:
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}} \lambda\,d\nu_{\psi\varphi}(\lambda) \;=\; \int_M f\,F\,\bar G\,d\mu \;=\; \bigl( T_f F, G\bigr)_{L^2(M,d\mu)} \;=\; \bigl( U(A\psi), U\varphi\bigr) \;=\; (A\psi,\varphi) .
$$
Hence $(A\psi,\varphi) = \int\lambda\,d(P_\lambda\psi,\varphi)$ for $\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$ and every $\varphi\in\mathcal{H}$. :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, Thm VIII.6, p. 263"} state the identity for $\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)$, where polarising $(A\cdot,\cdot)$ itself is already legitimate; the passage to all of $\mathcal{H}$ is the estimate just made.

Two things are left in the one-to-one correspondence of part D, and they are not of the same kind. *Injectivity* is immediate from what has just been proved: if self-adjoint $A$ and $A'$ have the same p.v.m. $\{P_\Omega\}$, then each $\psi$ has one and the same spectral measure $\mu_\psi$ for both, so the domain description gives $\mathcal{D}(A) = \{\psi : \int\lambda^2 d\mu_\psi<\infty\} = \mathcal{D}(A')$, and on that common domain the two-vector identity just proved gives $(A\psi,\varphi) = \int\lambda\,d(P_\lambda\psi,\varphi) = (A'\psi,\varphi)$, whence $A\psi = A'\psi$. Injectivity does not in fact need the extension to all of $\mathcal{H}$: $\mathcal{D}(A) = \mathcal{D}(A')$ is dense, so the identity for $\varphi$ in that dense set already pins $A\psi$ down. The extension earns its place in part D, where it strengthens what Reed and Simon state, not here. *Surjectivity* - that every p.v.m. on $\mathbb{R}$ is the p.v.m. of some self-adjoint operator, namely of $\int\lambda\,dP_\lambda$ on $\{\psi : \int\lambda^2 d\mu_\psi<\infty\}$ - is the other half of Theorem VIII.6 of :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.3, Thm VIII.6, p. 263"}, and we quote it rather than prove it: it starts from a p.v.m. with no operator attached, so Steps 4-7 would have to be run again with $\{P_\Omega\}$ in place of the continuous functional calculus, and that is a second construction rather than a corollary of this one. $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used]{provenance=original}
- **Self-adjointness, not symmetry.** Step 9 is the whole of it: the proof needs $A \pm i$ to be *onto*, which is precisely the basic criterion for self-adjointness (:ref[]{to=funcan.symmetric-self-adjoint}), and nothing weaker will do. The failure is not technical. :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VIII.2, pp. 257-259"} exhibit $T = i\,d/dx$ on the absolutely continuous functions on $[0,1]$ vanishing at both ends: it is closed and symmetric, and it has a whole circle of distinct self-adjoint extensions $T_\alpha$, indexed by $\lvert\alpha\rvert=1$, with different spectra. There is no single spectral decomposition of $T$ to be had, because there is no single operator.
- **Separability.** Used in Step 6, to get an at most countable family of cyclic subspaces, and again inside the bounded normal case quoted in Step 10, whose proof needs it for the same reason - Reed and Simon's Problem 5 says only "let $A$ be a normal operator", but it builds on their Problem 4, which does assume a separable space, so the hypothesis is one we carry rather than one they print; so everything downstream of Step 10 carries the hypothesis too. Dropping it costs more than the countability of the direct sum in part E: it costs the **finiteness of $\mu$** that part A asserts. On the non-separable $\mathcal{H} = \ell^{2}([0,1])$ take the bounded self-adjoint $A e_t = t\,e_t$. Every $t \in [0,1]$ is then an eigenvalue with a one-dimensional eigenspace, and the $\lambda$-eigenspace of a multiplication operator $T_f$ is $L^{2}(f^{-1}(\lambda),\mu)$, so each $f^{-1}(t)$ would have to have positive measure - and, the eigenspace being one-dimensional, to be an atom. Positive measure is already enough to settle the matter: uncountably many disjoint sets of positive measure do not fit inside a finite measure space, since for some $k$ uncountably many of them have measure $> 1/k$. So $\mu(M) = \infty$, and no finite measure space represents this $A$ - nor does a $\sigma$-finite one, since in a countable exhaustion of $M$ by sets of finite measure some single piece would meet uncountably many of the $f^{-1}(t)$ in positive measure, and the same count rules that out. What the construction still delivers for bounded $A$ is the multiplication form over a disjoint union of finite measure spaces - one copy of $\mathbb{R}$ carrying $\mu_n$ for each member of the maximal family of Step 6, exactly as in Step 7, with only the countability of the index set lost - and parts B, C and D are read off it as before, Step 12's approximate eigenvectors now being formed inside a single piece, where the measure is finite. For unbounded $A$ what stops is our *route*, not the theorem: the bounded normal case quoted in Step 10 carries separability with it, so the reduction through the resolvent of Step 9 is unavailable, and nothing said here shows that an unbounded self-adjoint operator on a non-separable space is without a multiplication form.
- **Finiteness of $\mu$.** This is part of the statement as Reed and Simon give it. Where it is arranged depends on the case: for bounded $A$, in Step 7, by scaling the cyclic vectors so that the masses $\mu_n(\mathbb{R}) = \lVert\varphi_n\rVert^2$ are summable; for unbounded $A$, not in our hands at all, since the finite measure space arrives with the bounded normal theorem quoted in Step 10. Where it is *used* is Steps 11 and 12: both need $\chi_S \in L^2(M,d\mu)$ for a set $S$ of positive measure on which $f$ is bounded - in Step 11 to contradict self-adjointness when $\operatorname{Im} f \ne 0$, in Step 12 to build the approximate eigenvectors - and $\chi_S \in L^2$ asks for $\mu(S) < \infty$. It is a convenience, not a constraint: the example below realises the momentum operator on $L^2(\mathbb{R}, dk)$, with Lebesgue measure, which is only $\sigma$-finite, and the reweighting at the end of Step 11 converts that to a finite measure without changing the operator.
- **Boundedness of $h$ in part C.** Needed for $h(A)$ to be everywhere defined and bounded. For unbounded $h$ the operator $h(A) = \int h\,dP$ - the clause of part D that is quoted from Reed and Simon rather than built here, as Step 10 records - exists but only on $\mathcal{D}_h$, and $\mathcal{D}_h = \mathcal{H}$ if and only if $h$ is **essentially** bounded on the spectrum - bounded off a set null for every spectral measure $\mu_\psi$, the same essential-versus-literal distinction drawn in part B and in the definition of the essential range. Literal boundedness is not necessary: for multiplication by $x$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R},dx)$ every $\mu_\psi$ is absolutely continuous, so the Borel function equal to $1$ off $\mathbb{Q}$ and to $q$ at the rational $p/q$ written in lowest terms with $q \ge 1$ is unbounded on $\sigma(A)=\mathbb{R}$ while $\int\lvert h\rvert^2\,d\mu_\psi = \lVert\psi\rVert^2$ for every $\psi$, giving $\mathcal{D}_h = \mathcal{H}$. Part A is the case $h(\lambda)=\lambda$.
- **The two conditions in part A are not interchangeable.** Condition 1 fixes the domain and condition 2 the action. Giving only condition 2 would leave the operator undetermined: multiplication by $f$ restricted to a smaller dense domain is a symmetric operator with the same formula and a different, usually much larger, spectrum.
:::

:::method[Finding a spectral representation]{provenance=original}
1. **Check self-adjointness on the stated domain.** Symmetric is not enough. In practice one exhibits a core and applies the basic criterion, or quotes a known result (:ref[]{to=funcan.symmetric-self-adjoint}).
2. **Look for a unitary that already diagonalises.** A translation-invariant operator on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is diagonalised by the Fourier transform; a Sturm-Liouville operator on a finite interval by its eigenfunction expansion (:ref[]{to=ode.sturm-liouville-theory}); a compact self-adjoint operator by its eigenbasis (:ref[]{to=funcan.compact-self-adjoint}). In each case read off $M$, $\mu$ and $f$.
3. **Read the spectrum.** $\sigma(A)$ is the essential range of $f$ - the set of values $f$ takes on sets of positive measure. A point $\lambda$ is an *eigenvalue* exactly when $\mu\bigl(f^{-1}\{\lambda\}\bigr) > 0$, since $\chi_{f^{-1}\{\lambda\}}$ is then a non-zero element of $L^2(M,d\mu)$ with $f\,\chi_{f^{-1}\{\lambda\}} = \lambda\,\chi_{f^{-1}\{\lambda\}}$, while conversely an eigenvector of $T_f$ for $\lambda$ must vanish a.e. off $f^{-1}\{\lambda\}$ (Step 13); in the direct-sum normal form of part E, where $f$ is the coordinate, that says some $\mu_n$ has an atom at $\lambda$. Where the measure has no such level set the spectrum is continuous, as in Step 2 of the example below.
4. **Compute the spectral measure of the state you care about.** $\mu_\psi$ is the image under $f$ of $\lvert U\psi\rvert^2 d\mu$. Everything about $A$ that $\psi$ can see is in this one finite measure.
5. **Apply functions.** For bounded Borel $h$, $h(A) = U^{-1}T_{h\circ f}U$ and $(h(A)\psi,\psi) = \int h\,d\mu_\psi$. For unbounded $h$, first check $\int \lvert h\rvert^2 d\mu_\psi < \infty$.
:::

:::example[The momentum operator and its spectral data]{provenance=original}
Take $\mathcal{H} = L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and
$$
A = -i\frac{d}{dx} , \qquad \mathcal{D}(A) = \{ \psi \in L^2(\mathbb{R}) : \psi \text{ is absolutely continuous and } \psi' \in L^2(\mathbb{R}) \} ,
$$
which is self-adjoint (:ref[]{to=funcan.symmetric-self-adjoint}).

**Step 1: the spectral representation.** Let $U$ be the Fourier-Plancherel transform in the unitary convention
$$
(U\psi)(k) = \hat\psi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx ,
$$
a unitary of $L^2(\mathbb{R},dx)$ onto $L^2(\mathbb{R},dk)$ (:ref[]{to=fourier.plancherel}). Differentiation becomes multiplication: $\widehat{\psi'}(k) = ik\hat\psi(k)$, so $\widehat{A\psi}(k) = k\hat\psi(k)$. So $M = \mathbb{R}$, $f(k) = k$, and condition 1 of part A reads: $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(A)$ exactly when $k\hat\psi(k) \in L^2$ - which is the Plancherel statement that $\psi' \in L^2$. The measure $dk$ is $\sigma$-finite but not finite; to match the statement literally, replace it by $d\tilde\mu(k) = \pi^{-1/2}e^{-k^2}dk$, a probability measure, and conjugate by the unitary $V : L^2(dk)\to L^2(d\tilde\mu)$, $(V\varphi)(k) = \pi^{1/4}e^{k^2/2}\varphi(k)$. Since $V$ commutes with multiplication by $k$, the operator is unchanged.

**Step 2: the spectrum.** The essential range of $f(k)=k$ is all of $\mathbb{R}$: every interval around every $\lambda$ has positive Lebesgue measure. So $\sigma(A) = \mathbb{R}$, and there are no eigenvalues, since $\{k=\lambda\}$ is Lebesgue-null and $k\hat\psi = \lambda\hat\psi$ forces $\hat\psi = 0$ a.e.

**Step 3: a state and its spectral measure.** Take the normalised Gaussian
$$
\psi(x) = \pi^{-1/4} e^{-x^2/2} , \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert\psi\rvert^2 dx = 1 .
$$
Its transform is $\hat\psi(k) = \pi^{-1/4}e^{-k^2/2}$ (the Gaussian is a fixed point of this convention), so
$$
d\mu_\psi(k) = \lvert\hat\psi(k)\rvert^2 dk = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\,e^{-k^2}\,dk ,
$$
a Gaussian probability measure of mean $0$ and variance $\tfrac12$. Three checks follow directly from it.

- **Total mass.** $\mu_\psi(\mathbb{R}) = \pi^{-1/2}\int e^{-k^2}dk = 1 = \lVert\psi\rVert^2$.
- **A spectral projection.** With $\Omega = [-1,1]$,
$$
(P_{[-1,1]}\psi,\psi) = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-1}^{1} e^{-k^2}dk = \operatorname{erf}(1) = 0.842700792949\ldots
$$
- **A second moment.** $\int k^2 d\mu_\psi = \tfrac12$, and indeed $\lVert A\psi\rVert^2 = \int\lvert\psi'\rvert^2 dx = \pi^{-1/2}\int x^2 e^{-x^2}dx = \tfrac12$.

**Step 4: a bounded function of $A$, checked two ways.** Take $h(\lambda) = e^{i\lambda}$, which is bounded with $\lVert h\rVert_\infty = 1$. By the recipe, $h(A) = U^{-1}T_{e^{ik}}U$, and
$$
\bigl(h(A)\psi,\psi\bigr) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{ik}\,d\mu_\psi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{ik}e^{-k^2}dk = e^{-1/4} .
$$
Independently, multiplying $\hat\psi$ by $e^{ik}$ is translation: $\bigl(h(A)\psi\bigr)(x) = \psi(x+1)$, so
$$
\bigl(h(A)\psi,\psi\bigr) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x+1)\psi(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x+1)^2/2}e^{-x^2/2}dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\,e^{-1/4}\!\int e^{-(x+\frac12)^2}dx = e^{-1/4} .
$$
Both routes give
$$
\bigl(e^{iA}\psi,\psi\bigr) = e^{-1/4} = 0.778800783071\ldots ,
$$
which is the functional calculus delivering a number that can be checked without it. (That $e^{iA}$ should be the translation by one unit is no accident; it is :ref[Stone's theorem]{to=funcan.stone-theorem}.)
:::

:::application[Why unbounded domains give integrals and not series]{provenance=original}
Separation of variables on a bounded domain produces a *series*: a Fourier series, a Bessel series, a sum over normal modes. On an unbounded domain it produces an *integral* - a Fourier transform, a Hankel transform, a Weyl-Titchmarsh expansion. Students usually meet this as a change of technique. The spectral theorem says it is a change in the operator's spectral measure, and nothing else.

A Sturm-Liouville operator on a finite interval with sensible boundary conditions has compact resolvent, hence a purely atomic spectral measure; part E then has $\mu_n$ a sum of point masses and the $L^2$ space is a sequence space, which is the eigenfunction series. On $\mathbb{R}$ the atoms are gone: $-d^2/dx^2$ there, and the first-order $-i\,d/dx$ of the example above, both have spectral measures with a density, so $L^2(\mathbb{R},d\mu)$ is a genuine function space and the expansion is an integral. :cite[Reed and Simon]{work=reed-simon-1980 loc="§VII.2, Example 4, p. 229, and the Dirac-notation remark, pp. 227-228"} put the point bluntly by observing that the Fourier transform *is* a spectral representation of $-i\,d/dx$, and that the multiplication-operator form is a rigorous version of the physicists' continuum of improper eigenstates.

This also supplies the standard vocabulary for scattering. Decomposing each $\mu_\psi$ into its pure point, absolutely continuous and continuous singular parts (:ref[]{to=measure.radon-nikodym}) splits the Hilbert space as
$$
\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{pp}} \oplus \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ac}} \oplus \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{sing}} ,
$$
each summand invariant under $A$; on the first, $A$ has a complete set of eigenvectors. For a Hamiltonian, $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{pp}}$ holds the bound states - the discrete energy levels a spectroscope measures - and $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ac}}$ holds the states that escape to infinity. The singular part is the pathological case one works to rule out. None of this vocabulary is available without the theorem, because without it a Hamiltonian with no eigenvectors has no decomposition at all.
:::
