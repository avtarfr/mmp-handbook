:::definition[Step, sign, ramp and rectangle]{provenance=adapted src="rhb-2006:§13.1.3, Eq. (13.22), p. 441; arfken-2013:§20.7, Eq. (20.137), p. 1010; strauss-2008:§12.1, p. 335; stakgold-holst-2011:§2.1, Example 2, p. 98"}
The **Heaviside step function**, or unit step, is

$$
H(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x > 0,\\ 0, & x < 0,\end{cases}
$$

which is Riley, Hobson and Bence's Eq. (13.22). Arfken writes $u(t-k)$ for the step switched on at $k$ and defines it the same way. As a distribution, $H$ is the functional generated by the indicator function of $(0,\infty)$:

$$
\langle H,\varphi\rangle = \int_0^{\infty}\varphi(x)\, dx ,
$$

Stakgold and Holst's Example 2. Three related functions are built from it:

$$
\operatorname{sgn} x = \begin{cases} +1, & x>0,\\ -1, & x<0,\end{cases}
\qquad
x_+ = \begin{cases} x, & x \ge 0,\\ 0, & x<0,\end{cases}
\qquad
\Pi_{a,b}(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & a<x<b,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
$$

the **sign** (or signum) function, the **ramp** function — Strauss's *plus function* $p(x)=x_+$, Reed and Simon's Example 8 — and the **rectangle** (or boxcar, or top-hat) function on $(a,b)$. They are related by

$$
\operatorname{sgn} x = 2H(x) - 1, \qquad
H(x) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \operatorname{sgn} x\bigr), \qquad
\lvert x\rvert = x\operatorname{sgn} x, \qquad
x_+ = x\,H(x) = \tfrac12\bigl(x + \lvert x\rvert\bigr),
$$

$$
\Pi_{a,b}(x) = H(x-a) - H(x-b) ,
$$

each valid for every $x \ne 0$ and hence as an identity of distributions. The rectangle relation is the one Arfken uses to write a square pulse of height $A$ on $[0,t_0]$ as $A[u(t)-u(t-t_0)]$.

**The value at the jump.** $H(0)$ is left undefined above. Riley, Hobson and Bence set $H(0)=\tfrac12$, which is the value the Fourier and Dirichlet theories assign to a jump and the one Arfken's Eq. (20.146) produces, $\int_0^\infty x^{-1}\sin tx\,dx = \tfrac\pi2[2u(t)-1]$, whose value at $t=0$ is $0$. Other books take $H(0)=1$. The choice is immaterial for everything in this unit: functions differing at one point are equal almost everywhere and therefore define the *same* distribution (:ref[]{to=fourier.distributions}), so no formula below depends on it. The convention matters only when $H$ is evaluated, not integrated.

**The step as a limit.** $H$ is the pointwise limit of smooth switches, for instance Arfken's

$$
u_n(x) = \tfrac12\bigl(1+\tanh nx\bigr) \longrightarrow H(x) \quad (x\ne0) ,
$$

whose derivative $u_n'(x) = \tfrac12 n\operatorname{sech}^2 nx$ is one of the delta families of :ref[]{to=fourier.delta-identities}. Hassani's piecewise-linear smoothing gives the rectangular family the same way. Each such pair exhibits $H' = \delta$ before any distribution theory is invoked.
:::

:::statement[The derivative chain]{provenance=adapted src="strauss-2008:§12.1, Example 7 and Eq. (15), p. 335; rhb-2006:§13.1.3, Eq. (13.23), p. 441; stakgold-holst-2011:§2.1, Eq. (2.1.15), p. 102; reed-simon-1980:§V.3, Example 8, p. 138"}
In $\mathcal{D}'(\mathbb{R})$, and also in $\mathcal{S}'(\mathbb{R})$ since all these functions are of slow growth:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}x_+ = H, \qquad
\frac{d}{dx}H = \delta, \qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}x_+ = \delta ,
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\lvert x\rvert = \operatorname{sgn} x, \qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{sgn} x = 2\,\delta, \qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\lvert x\rvert = 2\,\delta ,
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\Pi_{a,b} = \delta(x-a) - \delta(x-b), \qquad
\frac{d}{dx}H(x-a) = \delta(x-a) .
$$

Conversely, $H$ is the indefinite integral of $\delta$: for every $\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})$,

$$
H(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}\delta(t)\, dt
$$

in the sense that $x\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^x\delta(t)\,dt$, defined as the distribution whose derivative is $\delta$ and which vanishes for $x<0$, equals $H$. By the uniqueness statement of :ref[]{to=fourier.distributions}, $H$ is the *only* such antiderivative up to an additive constant, and the two side conditions $H=0$ for $x<0$ and $H=1$ for $x>0$ fix the constant. Stakgold and Holst obtain this by integrating Eq. (2.1.15) formally; it is the statement that the fundamental theorem of calculus survives the jump.

In two variables, with $x=(x_1,x_2)$,

$$
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_2}\Bigl[H(x_1)H(x_2)\Bigr] = \delta^{(2)}(x) ,
$$

Stakgold and Holst's Eq. (2.1.16): the indicator function of the first quadrant is a two-dimensional ramp whose mixed second derivative is the point source at the corner.
:::

:::idea{provenance=original}
All of these are the jump formula of :ref[]{to=fourier.distributions} read off a graph. Differentiating a piecewise-smooth function slides each corner down one order of singularity: a corner in the graph of $x_+$ becomes a jump in $H$, the jump in $H$ becomes a delta, the delta becomes a dipole. Nothing is lost at each stage, which is why integrating back recovers the original function — and that recovery is precisely what the naive answer $H'=0$ destroys.
:::

:::proof[The derivative chain]{provenance=adapted src="strauss-2008:§12.1, Example 7, p. 335; stakgold-holst-2011:§2.1, Example 9, p. 102"}
Let $\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})$, with $\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subset(-R,R)$.

**Step 1: $H' = \delta$.** By the definition of the distributional derivative and the definition of $H$ as a functional,

$$
\langle H',\varphi\rangle = -\langle H,\varphi'\rangle = -\int_0^{\infty}\varphi'(x)\, dx = -\bigl[\varphi(x)\bigr]_0^{R} = \varphi(0) ,
$$

by the fundamental theorem of calculus on $[0,R]$, where $\varphi$ is $C^1$, together with $\varphi(R)=0$. The right-hand side is $\langle\delta,\varphi\rangle$, so $H'=\delta$. Replacing $H$ by $H(\cdot-a)$ shifts the integral and gives $H(\cdot-a)' = \delta(\cdot-a)$.

**Step 2: $(x_+)' = H$.** Here $x_+$ is continuous, so no delta can appear; the computation checks it. Integrating by parts on $[0,R]$, where $x_+ = x$ is $C^1$,

$$
\langle (x_+)',\varphi\rangle = -\int_0^{\infty}x\,\varphi'(x)\, dx = -\Bigl(\bigl[x\varphi(x)\bigr]_0^{R} - \int_0^{R}\varphi(x)\, dx\Bigr) = \int_0^{\infty}\varphi(x)\, dx = \langle H,\varphi\rangle ,
$$

the boundary term vanishing at $x=0$ because of the factor $x$ and at $x=R$ because $\varphi(R)=0$. Combining with Step 1, $(x_+)'' = \delta$: Reed and Simon's observation that $\delta$ is the second derivative of a continuous function.

**Step 3: the sign and the modulus.** $\operatorname{sgn} = 2H-1$ and constants have zero derivative, so $\operatorname{sgn}' = 2H' = 2\delta$. And $\lvert x\rvert = 2x_+ - x$, so $\lvert x\rvert' = 2H - 1 = \operatorname{sgn} x$ and $\lvert x\rvert'' = 2\delta$. (Note $\lvert x\rvert' = \operatorname{sgn} x$ is the one case where the naive pointwise answer is also the right one — there is no jump in $\lvert x\rvert$ to generate a delta.)

**Step 4: the rectangle.** Linearity and Step 1 give $\Pi_{a,b}' = H(\cdot-a)' - H(\cdot-b)' = \delta(\cdot-a) - \delta(\cdot-b)$: a positive spike where the pulse switches on and a negative one where it switches off.

**Step 5: the step is the integral of the delta.** Let $u$ be a distribution with $u' = \delta$ and $u = 0$ on $(-\infty,0)$. Then $(u-H)' = \delta-\delta = 0$, so $u - H$ is a constant $c$ by the theorem on distributions with vanishing derivative in :ref[]{to=fourier.distributions}. Testing against a $\varphi$ supported in $(-\infty,0)$, where both $u$ and $H$ vanish, gives $c\int\varphi = 0$ for every such $\varphi$, hence $c=0$ and $u=H$. $\square$
:::

:::remark[Where the hypotheses are used]{provenance=original}
- **Compact support of $\varphi$** supplies the vanishing boundary terms at $x=R$ in Steps 1 and 2. Without it the same integrations by parts leave behind $\varphi(\infty)$, and $H'=\delta$ becomes false.
- **Why $H'=0$ is wrong and $\lvert x\rvert' = \operatorname{sgn} x$ is right.** Stakgold and Holst put the test crisply: $\int_0^x\operatorname{sgn} t\,dt = \lvert x\rvert$, so discarding the single bad point in $\lvert x\rvert'$ costs nothing, whereas $\int_{-\infty}^x 0\,dt = 0 \ne H(x)$, so discarding the jump in $H'$ loses the whole function. The information sits in a set of measure zero for the *function* and in a non-zero distribution for the *derivative*; only the second description survives integration.
- **The value $H(0)$ is irrelevant** because $H$ enters every statement only through $\int H\varphi$. This is a consequence of part (2) of the theorem in :ref[]{to=fourier.distributions}, and it is why the dispute between $H(0)=\tfrac12$ and $H(0)=1$ never shows up in a transform or a Green's function.
- **An alternative derivation, and what it needs.** Stakgold and Holst define $H'$ instead as the weak limit of the difference quotients $\varepsilon^{-1}[H(x+\varepsilon)-H(x)]$, which is the rectangular family $\varepsilon^{-1}\Pi_{-\varepsilon,0}$; pairing with $\varphi$ gives $\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{-\varepsilon}^0\varphi \to \varphi(0)$, and the hypothesis used is continuity of $\varphi$ at $0$ and nothing more. The two definitions agree, which is their Exercise 2.1.3.
- **Products are still forbidden.** $x_+ = xH(x)$ is legitimate because $x$ is smooth; $H(x)\delta(x)$ is not legitimate at all, and $H^2 = H$ is an identity about functions that happens to survive, not a licence to multiply distributions.
- **How far does this go?** Every one of these functions is a derivative of a continuous function, and Reed and Simon's regularity theorem (Theorem V.10, p. 139) says this is the general picture: every tempered distribution is $D^\alpha g$ for some continuous $g$ of polynomial growth. So nothing more exotic than repeated differentiation of ordinary continuous functions is ever needed to produce a tempered distribution.
:::

:::method[Transforming a switched-on signal]{provenance=adapted src="arfken-2013:§20.7, Eqs. (20.137)–(20.139), p. 1010, and §20.8, Eq. (20.166), p. 1023"}
A signal that is piecewise polynomial, or that switches on at a finite time, is transformed most cheaply by differentiating it until only deltas are left.

1. **Write the signal with steps and ramps.** Replace every "for $t>a$" clause by a factor $H(t-a)$, and every straight segment by a ramp $(t-a)_+$. A piecewise-linear signal is always a finite combination $\sum_j c_j\,(t-a_j)_+$, and a signal with jumps needs steps as well.
2. **Differentiate until the deltas appear.** For a piecewise-linear signal two derivatives suffice: by the chain above, $\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\sum_j c_j(t-a_j)_+ = \sum_j c_j\,\delta(t-a_j)$, where $c_j$ is the change of slope at $a_j$.
3. **Transform the deltas.** With $\hat f(\omega)=\int f e^{-i\omega t}dt$ the deltas give pure phases, $\widehat{\delta(\cdot-a)} = e^{-i\omega a}$; with the Laplace transform $\mathcal{L}\{F\}(s) = \int_0^\infty F(t)e^{-st}dt$ they give $\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-a)\} = e^{-as}$ for $a>0$, which is Arfken's Eq. (20.139).
4. **Undo the differentiations by dividing.** $\widehat{f^{(n)}} = (i\omega)^n\hat f$, so $\hat f(\omega) = (i\omega)^{-n}\widehat{f^{(n)}}(\omega)$; for the Laplace transform of a signal vanishing at $t=0$ together with its first $n-1$ derivatives, $\mathcal{L}\{f\} = s^{-n}\mathcal{L}\{f^{(n)}\}$.
5. **Check the division at $\omega=0$.** Step 4 is only legitimate if $\widehat{f^{(n)}}$ vanishes to order $n$ at the origin, that is, if the delta weights satisfy $\sum_j c_j = 0$, $\sum_j c_j a_j = 0$, and so on up to order $n-1$. For a signal of compact support these hold automatically, and the limiting value $\hat f(0) = \int f$ is a free check on the arithmetic.
6. **For a signal switched on at $t=k$**, use the shifting theorems directly instead: $\mathcal{L}\{u(t-k)\} = e^{-ks}/s$ (Arfken's Eq. (20.138)) and $\mathcal{L}\{F(t-k)u(t-k)\} = e^{-ks}\mathcal{L}\{F\}(s)$, the **Heaviside shifting theorem**, Arfken's Eq. (20.166). The step function in the second formula is what restricts $F$ to positive arguments; omitting it transforms the wrong function. The Fourier analogue of the simplest switched-on signal is $\widehat{H(t)e^{-at}}(\omega) = (a+i\omega)^{-1}$ for $a>0$, whose $a\to0^+$ limit is the transform of $H$ itself (:ref[]{to=fourier.tempered-distributions}).
:::

:::example[A trapezoidal pulse]{provenance=original}
A pulse rises linearly from $0$ at $t=0$ to $1$ at $t=1$, stays at $1$ until $t=3$, and falls linearly back to $0$ at $t=4$.

**Step 1: write it with ramps.** The slope is $+1$ on $(0,1)$, $0$ on $(1,3)$, $-1$ on $(3,4)$ and $0$ after. The changes of slope are $+1$ at $t=0$, $-1$ at $t=1$, $-1$ at $t=3$ and $+1$ at $t=4$, so

$$
T(t) = t_+ - (t-1)_+ - (t-3)_+ + (t-4)_+ .
$$

Spot-checks: $T(0.5) = 0.5$, $T(2) = 2-1 = 1$, $T(3.5) = 3.5-2.5-0.5 = 0.5$, $T(5) = 5-4-2+1 = 0$.

**Step 2: differentiate twice.** $T$ is continuous, so $T'$ has no deltas; $T'$ has jumps, so $T''$ is pure delta:

$$
T'(t) = H(t) - H(t-1) - H(t-3) + H(t-4), \qquad
T''(t) = \delta(t) - \delta(t-1) - \delta(t-3) + \delta(t-4) .
$$

**Step 3: transform.** $\widehat{T''}(\omega) = 1 - e^{-i\omega} - e^{-3i\omega} + e^{-4i\omega}$, which factorises:

$$
\widehat{T''}(\omega) = \bigl(1-e^{-i\omega}\bigr) - e^{-3i\omega}\bigl(1-e^{-i\omega}\bigr) = \bigl(1-e^{-i\omega}\bigr)\bigl(1-e^{-3i\omega}\bigr) .
$$

**Step 4: divide by $(i\omega)^2 = -\omega^2$.**

$$
\hat T(\omega) = -\frac{\bigl(1-e^{-i\omega}\bigr)\bigl(1-e^{-3i\omega}\bigr)}{\omega^{2}} .
$$

**Step 5: the checks.** The delta weights are $1,-1,-1,1$ with positions $0,1,3,4$, and

$$
\sum_j c_j = 1-1-1+1 = 0, \qquad \sum_j c_j a_j = 0 - 1 - 3 + 4 = 0 ,
$$

so $\widehat{T''}$ has a double zero at $\omega=0$ and the division is legitimate. Expanding $1-e^{-i\omega} = i\omega + O(\omega^2)$ and $1-e^{-3i\omega} = 3i\omega + O(\omega^2)$ gives a numerator $\sim (i\omega)(3i\omega) = -3\omega^2$, so

$$
\hat T(0) = 3 ,
$$

which must be $\int T\,dt$ — and indeed the trapezoid has area $2 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 3$. At $\omega=\pi$ the exponentials are $e^{-i\pi} = e^{-3i\pi} = -1$, so

$$
\hat T(\pi) = -\frac{(1+1)(1+1)}{\pi^2} = -\frac{4}{\pi^2} = -0.405285 ,
$$

real and negative. Direct numerical quadrature of $\int_0^4 T(t)e^{-i\pi t}dt$ returns $-0.4052847$, and the agreement holds at other frequencies too: at $\omega=1$ both routes give $-0.796047 - 1.739393\,i$.

**The Laplace transform**, for comparison. Here $T(t)=0$ for $t<0$, so by the Heaviside shifting theorem with $\mathcal{L}\{t\} = 1/s^2$,

$$
\mathcal{L}\{T\}(s) = \frac{1 - e^{-s} - e^{-3s} + e^{-4s}}{s^2} = \frac{\bigl(1-e^{-s}\bigr)\bigl(1-e^{-3s}\bigr)}{s^2} ,
$$

the same function of $s=i\omega$, as it must be for a signal supported in $t\ge0$. Letting $s\to0$ again gives $3$.
:::

:::application[Switching, impulse response and causality]{provenance=adapted src="rhb-2006:§13.1.3, Eq. (13.20), p. 441; arfken-2013:§20.7, Example 20.7.1, p. 1010"}
**Switched sources.** Every circuit that is switched on, every force applied at a definite instant, every beam that is gated, is modelled by multiplying the source by a step. Arfken's square pulse $A[u(t)-u(t-t_0)]$ is the elementary case, and its Laplace transform $A(1-e^{-t_0s})/s$ shows what the method above gives in general: the shape of the pulse enters only through a factor built from exponentials, so a train of pulses is a geometric series and can be summed in closed form. Riley, Hobson and Bence's complementary idealisation, the impulse $j(t) = J\delta(t-t_0)$, is legitimate precisely when the forcing lasts much less than the system's own response time; the step and the delta are the two ends of the same approximation, linked by $H'=\delta$.

**Step response and impulse response.** Because $H' = \delta$ and a linear time-invariant system commutes with differentiation, the response to a step is the integral of the response to an impulse:

$$
\text{(step response)}(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t}\text{(impulse response)}(\tau)\, d\tau .
$$

This is why a single measurement suffices to characterise such a system, and why engineers measure the step response — easy to generate — and differentiate it to obtain the impulse response, which is the Green's function. The algebra is the derivative rule for transforms: dividing a transform by $i\omega$ or by $s$ integrates in time.

**Causality.** A causal response function is one supported in $t\ge0$, that is, one of the form $G(t) = H(t)g(t)$. The step is therefore not a cosmetic factor but the mathematical content of causality, and it has consequences: the transform of a product with $H$ involves $\hat H = \pi\delta-i\operatorname{pv}(1/\omega)$ (:ref[]{to=fourier.tempered-distributions}), whose principal-value part ties the real and imaginary parts of the response together into the dispersion relations of :ref[]{to=complex.dispersion-relations}. The retarded Green's functions of :ref[]{to=green.causal-green-functions} are written with exactly this factor, and the jump conditions that determine them are the derivative identities of this unit read backwards.

**Characteristics and shocks.** A step is a weak solution of the wave equation and of first-order conservation laws, as :ref[]{to=fourier.distributions} notes: $u = H(x-ct)$ satisfies $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$ in $\mathcal{D}'$. Differentiating a piecewise-linear initial profile, as Strauss does for the plucked string, puts deltas on the characteristics and shows where the singularities of the solution travel — the starting point for :ref[]{to=pde.conservation-laws-shocks}.
:::
